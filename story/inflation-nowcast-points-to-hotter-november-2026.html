The Fed's Own Nowcast Says Inflation Is Heating Up Again

The Cleveland Fed's real-time tracker puts November inflation at an annualised 4.25 percent, with the Fed's preferred gauge above 4 percent — and fuel prices and tariffs are doing the damage.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 8, 2026 · 3 min readFacebookX

The Federal Reserve's own real-time inflation tracker is flashing a warning that Wall Street did not want to see. The Cleveland Fed's Inflation Nowcasting tool, which updates its forecasts as new economic data arrives, projects that the broad Consumer Price Index will creep slightly higher to 3.74 percent in October before jumping to an annualised 4.25 percent in November, a reacceleration that would put inflation back at the centre of the market's worries.

The picture is worse on the measure the Fed watches most closely. Personal Consumption Expenditures inflation, the central bank's preferred gauge, is expected to rise from a reported 3.6 percent in September to 3.77 percent in October and 4.03 percent in November. Core PCE, which strips out volatile food and energy costs, is expected to climb from 3.1 percent in September to 3.2 percent in October and 3.31 percent in November.

Nowcasting is not a crystal ball. No inflation forecasting tool is perfect, and the Cleveland Fed's model adjusts with every new data release, so these numbers will move. But the tool has an exceptional record of tracking inflation in real time, and its first look at November is the most discouraging signal on prices in months, arriving just as the market was already on edge about interest rates.

The forces behind the forecast are largely mechanical, and they start with fuel. A report published on November 2 found that the Producer Price Index, which measures the prices businesses receive for goods and services, had increased by 6.3 percent through September, while the Consumer Price Index had risen by 3.7 percent over the same period. That gap between producer and consumer inflation is the widest since April 2022, and it is driven primarily by energy costs swollen by the war with Iran.

Gasoline prices alone have surged, and energy accounts for about 7 percent of the CPI basket, so the increase feeds directly into the headline inflation rate. Other components, excluding food and energy, have shown only modest increases, which suggests the current inflation pressure is concentrated in fuel rather than broad-based consumer demand — but fuel is the price Americans notice first, and it is the one that shaped the midterm elections.

Tariffs are also part of the story. According to a newly released report by three New York Federal Reserve economists writing for Liberty Street Economics, the president's 2025 tariffs are estimated to have increased goods price inflation by 2.9 percentage points by February 2026, a substantial contribution that is still working its way through the economy.

For Wall Street, the timing could hardly be worse. The nowcast landed days after a strong October jobs report, employers added 165,000 jobs as unemployment slipped to 4.1 percent, pushed the odds of a December rate hike to about 80 percent. Stocks sold off sharply this week as investors repriced both rates and the artificial intelligence boom, and a hotter inflation reading would make the Fed's job harder still.

There is a genuine test coming. The official October CPI report is due on Tuesday, November 10, and it will show whether the nowcast's warning is borne out. If it is, the debate in markets will shift from whether the Fed hikes in December to how many more hikes might follow.

Inflation was the issue that decided the midterms, with about half of voters telling pollsters that the high cost of living was crucial to their choice for Congress. The new Congress inherits that problem, and the Cleveland Fed's numbers suggest it is getting worse, not better, just as the country heads into the winter.
