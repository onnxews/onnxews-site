White House Fires National Cyber Director Sean Cairncross After Week of Tech Outages

Sean Cairncross is out after little more than a year in the Senate-confirmed role, and Pentagon technology chief Kirsten Davies will take over in an acting capacity.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 20, 2026 · 5 min readFacebookX

The White House has fired National Cyber Director Sean Cairncross, administration officials confirmed on Thursday, removing the official charged with coordinating federal cybersecurity policy in the middle of a string of major technology outages. Kirsten Davies, the Senate-confirmed chief information officer at the Defense Department, will take over the role in an acting capacity.

The exact circumstances of Cairncross's departure were described differently by different accounts on Thursday. Axios first reported he was leaving, and a source close to the White House told reporters that President Donald Trump “fully supported firing Sean Cairncross from his position as he lacked the strong cyber credentials to perform the job.” Axios separately reported that the departure was amicable, and that Cairncross did not want to serve another two years in the role, preferring to leave now so the White House would have time to secure Senate confirmation for a successor before Democrats take control of Congress in January.

Cairncross was nominated by Trump and had served as the Senate-confirmed national cyber director since August 2025, a tenure of little more than a year. The White House had previously touted his “extensive leadership experience” when he was confirmed to the role. He also played a key role in the administration's dealings with major artificial intelligence firms, a portfolio that put him at the center of the government's approach to the technology now reshaping the economy.

In a statement on Thursday night, Cairncross struck a grateful tone. “It has been the honor of a lifetime to serve President Donald J. Trump. I leave my role as Director of Cyber incredibly proud of our work to strengthen America's cybersecurity, protect our critical infrastructure, confront foreign threats, and position America to lead the world in Super Intelligence,” he said, using the administration's preferred term for advanced artificial intelligence. “None of it would have been possible without President Trump's extraordinary leadership.”

Davies moved quickly to embrace the job. In a social media post thanking Trump, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth and White House chief of staff Stephen Miller on Thursday, she wrote that the country “stands in the crossroad of analog, digital, and quantum technologies, our American way of living increasingly interconnected.” She added, “Our opportunity is to dominate the cyber landscape for generations to come ... but only if we modernize and drive resilience into the foundations our great nation runs on, as well as build cyber talent for our future.” Hegseth and Secretary of State Marco Rubio both congratulated her publicly on Thursday.

The shakeup comes as the country is still reeling from a string of cyber outages that have hit air travel, banking and national security systems in recent days. The failures included Microsoft 365 services, Federal Aviation Administration routing systems and disrupted banking at Chase, along with an outage on the National Security Agency's classified internal network. Democrats have faulted the administration's response to the outages and warned that the country's cyber defenses are not keeping up with AI-driven threats.

Top administration officials have repeatedly said the outages are separate from each other and not related to any cyberattacks. Rubio addressed the disruptions earlier this week in Manila, saying there was “zero evidence” that hackers, criminals or foreign governments were behind the tech outages, or that the incidents are connected. “We are not under systemic attack,” he said, in what appeared to be a tacit acknowledgment that the administration had been less than open about the outages until then. Trump has downplayed the situation, telling Americans to “relax.”

The Office of the National Cyber Director coordinates cybersecurity policy and strategy across the federal government, a role that has grown more prominent as AI systems have spread through government and industry. Unlike most White House jobs, the director's post must be confirmed by the Senate, and the Senate will have to confirm whoever fills the role permanently. The White House did not say when Cairncross's last day will be, and a White House official confirmed to CNN that he is leaving the Trump administration entirely.

Cairncross is the latest top cyber official to leave the administration, and his departure leaves the government's cybersecurity leadership in transition at a moment of unusual strain. With the outages still fresh, questions about how well the United States protects itself online unresolved, and a change of control in Congress weeks away, the acting director will inherit both the job and the scrutiny that comes with it.

Why it matters: This firing matters because it removes the government's top cyber coordinator just as a run of outages has shaken public confidence in the systems Americans rely on every day, from air travel to banking. The conflicting accounts of why Cairncross left, amicable departure or dismissal for weak credentials, also expose a split inside the administration over who is to blame for the failures. Whoever holds the job permanently will have to rebuild that confidence while Congress, soon under Democratic control, watches closely.
