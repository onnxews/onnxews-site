Van Hollen Challenges Schumer for Senate Majority Leader in First Contested Race in Decades

Maryland Sen. Chris Van Hollen announced he will challenge Chuck Schumer to lead Senate Democrats, saying voters called for new leadership after the midterm sweep.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 13, 2026 · 6 min readFacebookX

Sen. Chris Van Hollen of Maryland announced Thursday that he will challenge Sen. Chuck Schumer for Senate majority leader, setting up the first contested race for the top Democratic job in the chamber in more than 30 years. Van Hollen made the announcement in an interview with MS NOW host Jen Psaki, days after Democrats swept to a Senate majority in the midterm elections.

"This is not about any one individual, but it is about answering the call of the American people," Van Hollen said in the interview. "The call was really to reject the status quo and to reject business as usual," he added. "And to do that, I think we need new leadership." CNN reported that the closed-ballot leadership election is scheduled for December 1, and the winner will need the support of a majority of the caucus.

Schumer, 75, has led Senate Democrats since 2017 and moved swiftly to defend his position. "I welcome any discussion about the future of our caucus and the important work ahead," he posted on X. "I'm proud of what we've accomplished together — 55 seats and counting is an amazing result — and I have the votes to continue serving as Democratic Leader."

The challenge lands after a midterm result that was far stronger than Democrats expected. Democrats have flipped eight seats so far and will enter January with at least 55 senators, according to CNN. That majority is what makes the leadership fight matter: the winner will run the chamber for the final two years of President Donald Trump's term and shape the party's identity heading into the 2028 presidential election.

Van Hollen, 67, conceded he is the underdog. He told Psaki he had talked to most Senate Democrats and believes the race "is very much in flux," and said he had "more than 10" commitments but expects his support to grow now that colleagues know he is running. He received a swift endorsement from one incoming freshman, Sen.-elect Abdul El-Sayed of Michigan, who has suggested for weeks that he could back Van Hollen if he ran.

Schumer also picked up a quick endorsement, from Maine Sen. Angus King, an independent who caucuses with Democrats. "Chuck has outlined an aggressive plan for the new Democratic majority which is directly responsive to the message we heard from the voters, and he has the experience and unique mastery of the Senate process to make it happen," King said in a statement.

The discontent with Schumer has been building in public for days. Sen. Bernie Sanders, a Vermont independent who caucuses with Democrats, held a news conference Tuesday with Sen. Elissa Slotkin of Michigan at which both made clear they wanted a new leader. "If this campaign was about anything, it was about change and the need for the Democratic Party, in my view, to move in a new and bold direction," Sanders said. "But in my view, the time is now for change, and I will not be supporting Chuck to be reelected as the Democratic leader." Incoming Democratic senators Adam Hamilton of Kansas, Troy Jackson of Maine, Annie Andrews of South Carolina and Peggy Flanagan of Minnesota have also said they want new leadership.

Van Hollen has drawn the contrast with Schumer in substantive terms as well as generational ones. He said he would use the "full power" of the Senate to respond to voters' demands to end the war in Iran and lower the cost of living, and promised he would not intervene in primaries for open Senate seats the way Schumer has. He has accused Schumer and the Democratic Senatorial Campaign Committee of siding with the cryptocurrency industry and the American Israel Public Affairs Committee against progressive candidates in primaries this cycle. "We have two members in that room, newly elected senators, who would not be there if Schumer and the DSCC had had their way," Van Hollen said Tuesday at a post-election Democratic conference in Washington.

The race is a reversal for Van Hollen himself. As recently as Saturday he told CNN that seeking the job was "not my focus," noting that he was also interested in running for president. By Thursday he had shelved that ambition and made the leadership bid instead. Van Hollen has served in the Senate since 2017 after 14 years in the House, and has held leadership roles in both chambers, including chairing both parties' campaign arms. His national profile has grown over the past two years, in part because he traveled to El Salvador to meet Kilmar Abrego Garcia, a Maryland resident who had been mistakenly deported there.

Schumer's allies argue that the man who delivered the majority deserves to keep leading it. "When the manager takes you to the World Series, you give them another contract," Sen. Ruben Gallego, an Arizona Democrat, told reporters earlier this week. Sen. Kirsten Gillibrand of New York, who ran the Senate Democrats' campaign arm this year, praised Schumer for overseeing what she called a standout cycle. Schumer also faces a possible primary challenge if he runs for reelection in 2028, potentially from Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez of New York.

Senate Democrats have not held a contested leadership election since 1994, when Tom Daschle of South Dakota defeated Chris Dodd of Connecticut by a single vote. The December 1 ballot will be secret, and Schumer needs only a majority of his caucus to win. The question for the next three weeks is whether Van Hollen's argument — that a sweeping victory demands a change of leadership — can peel away enough senators from a leader who has held the job for nine years and just delivered them the majority.

Why it matters: This is the first real test of what the midterm sweep means inside the Democratic Party. Schumer delivered the majority, but a vocal bloc of the caucus — including incoming freshmen who owe him nothing — says the victory was a demand for confrontation, not continuity. The December 1 vote will settle whether Democrats go into the final two years of the Trump presidency with the leader who won the majority or the challenger who says the moment requires a different one, and the winner will set the party's posture on the Iran war, the cost of living and the 2028 race.
