Boston's Beloved CITGO Sign Lights Up Again Tonight, 30 Feet Higher Over Kenmore Square

After six dark months, Boston's most famous sign returns on Thursday night, rebuilt and raised above the new towers crowding Kenmore Square.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 19, 2026 · 4 min readFacebookX

Boston's CITGO sign will glow over Kenmore Square again on Thursday night, when the rebuilt landmark is switched back on at 6 p.m. in a free public ceremony at the Commonwealth Avenue Mall. The relighting ceremony is scheduled for Thursday evening, Nov. 19, from 6 p.m. to 8 p.m.

The sign has been dark since May, when crews began taking it apart as part of a months-long restoration. Over the last six months, Suffolk Construction crews dismantled, moved, and rebuilt the branded sign to preserve it and make it easier to see among the surrounding buildings, according to CITGO.

The sign that comes back is not quite the one that left. It now stands 30 feet higher and 120 feet east of its previous location, atop a new truss superstructure designed to meet current building codes and safely support its weight. It kept its same dimensions, blue lettering, and familiar red triangle logo, and it still sits atop 660 Beacon St.

CITGO spokesperson Karl Schmidt said the move will "reestablish the sightlines" the sign has held over Kenmore Square since 1965, when it took on its current CITGO look. The higher perch is meant to keep the sign visible as new high-rise development rises around it.

The rebuild became necessary after engineers found that decades of exposure to New England weather had worn down key parts of the old sign's structure, a finding city officials announced last December. CITGO is paying for the roughly $8 million project.

Much of the heavy work happened about an hour south of Boston, where Poyant Signs of New Bedford worked on the sign's 36 panels, each 10 feet tall, 20 feet wide and about 1,400 pounds. The red CITGO emblem and the big letters were worked on at a facility in Texas.

The sign's history stretches back to 1940, when it was first put up as an advertisement for Cities Service, and it was given its CITGO look in 1965. It went dark from 1979 to 1983 after the governor ordered it shut off to save energy, and public outcry kept it on the roof. In 2018, the Boston Landmarks Commission voted unanimously to make it a local landmark, but then-Mayor Marty Walsh vetoed the designation in favor of a private deal between CITGO and the building's developer to keep the sign in Kenmore Square, which became a 30-year agreement. The sign is Massachusetts' only federally landmarked sign.

One piece of the old sign never left the neighborhood. An 11-foot blue "C" from the original now sits in the window at Cornwall's, the bar that sat right beneath it, while the rest of the old letters are stored in New Bedford.

Thursday night's ceremony will include a speaking program and countdown, as well as performances by the Boston University Pep Band and the Berklee College of Music House Band, CITGO said. Local elected officials and CITGO executives are expected to attend, and the outbound side of Commonwealth Avenue will be closed to traffic during the ceremony. "It's probably one of the most iconic signs in the Boston skyline," Poyant's Jason Fredette told NBC 10 this summer.

The relighting caps a project that took the sign down piece by piece in late May and rebuilt it through the summer and fall. When the triangle glows above Kenmore Square again on Thursday night, over the bars, the T stop and the walk to Fenway Park, it will be just a little higher than Boston remembers it.

Why it matters: The CITGO sign is more than an advertisement; it is the visual anchor of Kenmore Square and Fenway Park, a landmark Bostonians have fought to keep lit before, including when it went dark in the late 1970s. Its return matters because it shows how the city is handling the tension between new development and old identity: rather than letting the towers swallow the sign, the project spent roughly $8 million to lift it above them. The 30-year deal keeping it in place means the argument is settled for a generation, but the rebuilt sign is also a reminder that preservation now comes with engineering bills, not just sentiment.
