At Least 24 Dead as Boat Carrying Mourners to a Funeral Capsizes in Southern Nigeria

A crowded boat carrying mourners to a burial ceremony capsized in Bayelsa State on Friday, and the death toll rose to 24 on Saturday as rescue teams kept searching the creeks.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 22, 2026 · 4 min readFacebookX

At least 24 people died when a crowded boat carrying people to a funeral in southern Nigeria capsized on Friday, the National Emergency Management Agency said. Search and rescue operations were continuing on Saturday, with 24 bodies recovered so far and 24 survivors rescued, the agency said in a statement.

The boat was carrying passengers from Ayama to Ukubie community, in Bayelsa State, for a burial ceremony when the accident occurred. The vessel went down in the creeks of the southern Nigerian state on Friday, and police said preliminary reports suggested the boat had suffered a mechanical failure. The death toll marks a sharp rise from the figure first announced by police, who initially reported four deaths. Authorities have not said how many passengers were believed to have been on board, and the number of people still missing was not specified.

Police spokesman Musa Mohammed said on Friday that another 24 people had been rescued and sought medical assistance. He said passengers had asked the boat operator to steer toward land, but the operator allegedly declined. The boat operator then reportedly fled the scene, while the manager of the boat was taken into custody. The Bayelsa state government responded by urging boat operators and passengers to adhere to safety regulations. "Overloading, speeding, recklessness and traveling without life jackets must stop. No journey is worth a life," Bayelsa Commissioner for Information Ebiuwou Koku-Obiyai said in a statement on Saturday.

The governor of Bayelsa State, Douye Diri, visited the scene on Saturday and ordered an investigation into the cause of the accident, which he said "should not have occurred if every department of government at the federal, state and local government diligently did their job," according to a statement from his office. The investigation will examine the condition of the vessel, why it was carrying so many passengers and whether safety rules were followed.

Boat accidents are common on Nigeria's inland waterways, especially in riverine communities where overcrowding is widespread and safety regulations are often flouted. In Bayelsa and other states of the Niger Delta, rivers and creeks are the main roads: boats carry market traders, schoolchildren, worshippers and mourners between communities that have no road connection at all. A lack of enforcement means faulty boats without life jackets sometimes operate anyway, and when a vessel fails far from shore, there is often no rescue service close enough to help in time.

The funeral trip is a familiar pattern in the region's tragedies. Boats hired for burials and weddings often carry whole extended families and their neighbors, and the pressure to fit everyone aboard can overwhelm a vessel built for far fewer people. Friday's boat was carrying mourners between two small communities, Ayama and Ukubie, in the Southern Ijaw area of Bayelsa State, when it capsized at a river junction. The exact number of passengers remains unconfirmed, and with search operations still under way, the toll could rise further as more of the river is searched.

Nigeria has suffered repeated mass drownings on its rivers in recent years, and each one has brought the same promises of enforcement and the same warnings about overloading. The Bayelsa government said on Saturday that it would act, but the history of these accidents suggests that the rules already exist and the failure is in applying them. Life jackets are required, passenger limits are set, and boats are supposed to be inspected — yet vessels keep leaving shore overloaded, uninspected and without enough life jackets for everyone aboard.

For the families in Ayama and Ukubie, the weekend brought a burial ceremony that ended in more funerals. The 24 bodies recovered so far are being identified, and the 24 survivors are receiving medical attention. As of Saturday, rescue teams were still searching the creeks for the missing, and the state government said the investigation ordered by Governor Diri would report on what went wrong. Until then, the communities along the river are left with the same question that follows every such disaster in Nigeria: how many more boats have to sink before the rules are enforced.

Why it matters: The Bayelsa disaster is not an isolated accident but a symptom of how river transport works across the Niger Delta, where boats are the only connection between communities and safety rules are routinely ignored. The governor's own words — that the accident should not have happened if every level of government had done its job — point to the real story: the regulations exist, the warnings are repeated after every sinking, and yet overloaded boats without life jackets keep leaving shore. Until enforcement follows the promises, the next funeral boat is only a matter of time.
