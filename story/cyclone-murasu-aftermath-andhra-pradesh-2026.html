Cyclone Murasu Toll Rises to Eight as Andhra Pradesh Counts 1,918 Crore in Losses

Cyclone Murasu killed eight people and caused an estimated 1,918 crore rupees in losses in Andhra Pradesh, as a new assessment shows the storm's toll was far higher than first reported.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 8, 2026 · 4 min readFacebookX

The death toll from Cyclone Murasu has risen to eight people, and estimated losses have climbed to about 1,918 crore rupees, roughly 230 million dollars, according to a preliminary state assessment released on November 6. The figures, compiled in a situation report by relief agencies, show a storm that did far more damage than first thought when it came ashore on India's Andhra Pradesh coast three days earlier.

Severe Cyclonic Storm Murasu crossed the south Andhra coast near Ongole, in Prakasam district, in the early hours of November 3, between roughly 3:30 and 5:30 a.m. local time, as a severe cyclonic storm with sustained winds of 100 to 110 kilometres per hour gusting to 120. It was the second cyclone to hit India's east coast in a week, following Cyclone Arnab, and it weakened quickly as it moved inland over interior Andhra Pradesh and Telangana.

The toll rose as district officials completed their first assessments. Early reports on landfall day had counted two deaths, both in Bapatla district, where a 25-year-old man and a 65-year-old woman died when buildings collapsed on them. By November 6, the preliminary state assessment had raised the toll to eight: five in Bapatla district, two in Guntur and one in Palnadu. The figures are preliminary and may still be revised as final compensation assessments are completed.

Farms took the heaviest hit. Crops on about 1.43 lakh hectares were damaged, affecting more than 1.86 lakh farmers, along with 6,749 hectares of horticulture crops. Officials also counted 983 damaged houses, 346 boats and 220 fishing nets. The preliminary loss estimate climbed from 344 crore rupees on landfall day to about 1,918 crore rupees once fuller assessments came in, with paddy, cotton and maize taking the worst of the crop damage.

The storm left thousands of uprooted trees, downed power poles and flooded fields across the coastal districts. More than 2,750 electricity poles were brought down and transmission lines and substations damaged, with Prakasam and Bapatla hit most. About 12,230 people were staying in 171 relief camps in Guntur, Bapatla and Palnadu, and more than 3,000 pregnant women and 1,000 nursing mothers were moved to hospitals as a precaution.

Murasu's rain also brought an unexpected benefit. Until late October, Andhra Pradesh had a seasonal rainfall deficit of about 53 percent, and much of Telangana and Andhra Pradesh was under drought advisories. After Arnab and Murasu, the deficit stands at about 28.9 percent, nearly halved by the two storms. It is a paradox familiar to farmers in the region: the same rain that drowned standing crops also replenished reservoirs and groundwater after months of dry weather.

The situation report also covered the damage from Cyclone Arnab, which struck the week before. In Odisha, three people died in a landslide in Kandhamal, and 107.7 crore rupees has been sanctioned for 4.31 lakh families, with 2,526 crore rupees in central aid sought. In Andhra Pradesh, Arnab's toll was revised to 781 villages affected, more than three lakh hectares of crops damaged and 4,563 crore rupees in losses, with the chief minister seeking the same amount from the central government.

Elsewhere, Telangana and Tamil Nadu reported breaches and crop loss in Khammam, and four fishermen from Chennai were found dead on the Prakasam coast. The back-to-back storms have stretched disaster response teams across four states, with assessments still under way in many districts and compensation claims yet to be settled.

For the farmers of coastal Andhra Pradesh, the immediate concern is what comes next. The state has sent the central government an interim damage report, and officials say the figures will change as final district-wise assessments are completed. Power restoration, the repair of damaged houses and boats, and the payment of compensation to the 186,000 farmers whose crops were hit will take weeks, and the full cost of the two cyclones is still being counted.

The two storms are a reminder of how exposed India's east coast is to the Bay of Bengal's cyclone season, and of how quickly a storm that weakens within a day of landfall can still leave a trail of destruction. Murasu has since dissipated, but the flooded fields, the damaged homes and the eight families who lost someone to the storm will be living with its consequences long after the weather has moved on.
