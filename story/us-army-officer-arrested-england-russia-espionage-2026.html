U.S. Army Officer Arrested in England on Suspicion of Spying for Russia

A 41-year-old U.S. Army officer assigned to NATO's Allied Rapid Reaction Corps was detained near Gloucester under Britain's National Security Act, with police saying the allegations relate to Russia.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 11, 2026 · 4 min readFacebookX

A senior U.S. Army officer assigned to a NATO rapid-response command in Britain was arrested on Tuesday on suspicion of spying for Russia, British counterterrorism police said, in a case that has drawn in the FBI, American military counterintelligence and intelligence officials on both sides of the Atlantic. The 41-year-old soldier, whose name has not been released, was detained near Gloucester in western England and remains in custody at a London police station.

London's Metropolitan Police said the man was arrested on suspicion of offenses under Sections 1 and 3 of Britain's National Security Act 2023, which cover obtaining or disclosing protected information and assisting a foreign intelligence service. Officers were searching two locations in Gloucestershire as part of the investigation. In its statement, the force said plainly that the country to which the allegations relate is Russia, and that counterterrorism officers had worked closely with U.S. authorities, the U.S. military and the FBI.

A senior NATO official told CNN that the soldier was assigned to the Allied Rapid Reaction Corps, a British-led NATO corps headquartered at Imjin Barracks near Gloucester. A U.S. official said the detained man is a field-grade Army officer, a rank band that runs from major through lieutenant colonel to colonel. The Allied Rapid Reaction Corps is a multinational land force designed to deploy quickly in response to crises and short-notice operations, and it is one of only two NATO strategic reserve corps.

Brigadier General Sean Stinchon, commanding general of U.S. Army Counterintelligence Command, described the suspect in a news release as a senior soldier who had been entrusted with sensitive information, and warned about the risks posed by people with access to military intelligence. FBI Director Kash Patel said the arrest involves allegations of espionage. No formal charges have been announced, and police have not said what information the officer is suspected of handling or given a timeline for a charging decision.

The arrest comes at a moment of heightened concern about threats to American forces stationed in Britain and about Russian covert activity across Europe. Since Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine, Western officials have attributed more than 210 incidents of sabotage and other malign activity across Europe to Russia, according to tracking by the Associated Press. The case also follows an earlier prosecution of a 22-year-old Army soldier accused of trying to pass classified information about the M1A2 Abrams tank to someone he believed was a Russian intelligence officer.

British police stressed that the arrest is not linked to any other ongoing counterterrorism investigation, a point that separates it from a separate security scare in September, when five men were arrested near RAF Fairford, a U.S.-operated air base near Gloucester, over a suspected Iran-linked plot. That incident prompted Washington to pull aircraft out of British bases. Officials said the new case concerns Russia, not Iran, and there is no indication the two matters are connected.

The Kremlin moved quickly to distance itself from the case. Spokesman Dmitry Peskov told reporters on Wednesday that Russia has no connection to the arrest whatsoever. The denial does little to settle the matter, and the investigation itself remains in its early stages. Authorities have not publicly established whether any classified information reached Russian intelligence, and the officer has not been convicted of any offense. Under British law, as in the United States, he is presumed innocent unless proven guilty in court.

What happens next will depend on what investigators find in the searches and in the officer's communications and contacts. British prosecutors will decide whether to bring charges under the National Security Act, a 2023 law that replaced espionage statutes more than a century old and was written specifically to give authorities stronger tools against state-backed spying. If charged and convicted, the officer could face a lengthy prison sentence. The U.S. Army is also likely to run its own parallel process, since the case involves a serving officer and access to sensitive military information.

The wider picture is one of a NATO alliance on edge. American troops in Britain sit at the center of the alliance's planning for a crisis in Europe, and the Allied Rapid Reaction Corps is meant to be among the first formations to move if one erupts. An espionage case inside that headquarters, even one that is still only an allegation, raises uncomfortable questions about vetting, insider threats and how much an adversary might learn about the alliance's plans. For now, officials on both sides of the Atlantic are saying little beyond confirming that the investigation is ongoing and that it is being treated with the utmost seriousness.
