"Ebenezer" Tops Friday Box Office as Johnny Depp Scores First No. 1 in Eight Years

The dark Dickens retelling earned $7.1 million on Friday and is pacing for a $20 million-plus opening, Depp's first No. 1 since 2018.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 14, 2026 · 4 min readFacebookX

Johnny Depp is back on top of the box office. His new film "Ebenezer" earned $7.1 million on Friday, including $1.5 million from Thursday previews that began at 2 p.m., putting it on pace for a domestic opening north of $20 million as of Friday night.

The film is also expected to earn a similar amount, around $20 million, from 63 international markets, for a worldwide opening of roughly $40 million. That would make it Depp's first No. 1 opening since "Fantastic Beasts: The Crimes of Grindelwald" in 2018.

The opening is a strong result for a film that was seen as a gamble. "Ebenezer" is a dark retelling of Charles Dickens' "A Christmas Carol," directed by Ti West and written by Nathaniel Halpern. Depp plays Ebenezer Scrooge, with Ian McKellen as the ghost of Jacob Marley, Rupert Grint as Bob Cratchit, and Andrea Riseborough, Tramell Tillman, Daisy Ridley and Sam Claflin in supporting roles.

The film runs 116 minutes and is rated PG-13. Its opening is roughly in line with Depp's other adult dramas: "Black Mass" opened to $22.6 million in 2015, and "Public Enemies" opened to $25.2 million in 2009. A final weekend total in that range would count as a solid start for a mid-budget literary adaptation.

Critics have been split. The film sits at 49 percent on Rotten Tomatoes as of Friday night, a modest score that suggests audiences are showing up for Depp and the Dickens story rather than for the reviews. The film dethroned "Godzilla Minus Zero," which opened last weekend to $31.6 million, the biggest opening ever for a live-action foreign-language film in North America.

The economics are still tight. Depp reportedly took a pay cut to star, a bet on the film's back-end value, and the film will need strong legs beyond opening weekend to turn a profit.

The film also carries significance for Paramount. It is the first big greenlight of the studio's new administration since the Skydance merger, and its performance will be read as an early test of the new leadership's taste. A hit would validate the bet on a star-driven literary film; a miss would raise questions about the strategy.

For Depp, the opening is a comeback moment. His last several films played to smaller audiences, and "Ebenezer" is his most prominent release in years. If the weekend total lands where Friday's numbers suggest, it will be his biggest opening in more than a decade and a reminder that his name still draws a crowd.

The opening is also a test of how much the Christmas season still matters at the box office. A Dickens adaptation released in mid-November has weeks to build through the holidays, and a strong opening gives the film a platform for word of mouth. If audiences embrace it, the film could play through December; if not, it will fade quickly as bigger holiday releases arrive.

For Paramount, the stakes go beyond one weekend. The studio's new leadership inherited a slate and a strategy, and "Ebenezer" was its first major choice. A solid opening buys the new team credibility with filmmakers and investors, while a flop would invite questions about whether the studio is betting on the right kinds of movies. The Friday numbers suggest the bet is paying off so far.

The weekend is not over, and Saturday's numbers will decide whether the film clears $20 million or settles just below it. Either way, Depp has his No. 1, and the Christmas season has its first box office surprise. Read the box office report from MovieWeb

Why it matters: The opening matters because it answers the question Hollywood has been asking about Depp for years: can he still open a movie? A $20 million-plus start says yes, and it hands Paramount's new leadership its first win. The mixed reviews mean the film still needs strong word of mouth to profit, but for one weekend, the story is simple: Depp is a box office draw again, and a Dickens adaptation did it.
