Trump Sends Bipartisan SEC and CFTC Nominations to the Senate in First Picks in a Year

The White House named two Democrats and two Republicans to the market regulators, filling seats that have sat empty for months as the lame-duck clock runs down.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 13, 2026 · 5 min readFacebookX

President Donald Trump sent four nominations to the Senate on Thursday for the Securities and Exchange Commission and the Commodity Futures Trading Commission, the first nominations to either agency since 2025 and the first bipartisan slate of his second term. The picks would fill seats at the two regulators that oversee American stock, derivatives and much of the crypto market — agencies that have been running with skeleton crews for most of the year.

For the SEC, Trump named former CFTC Commissioner Kristin Johnson, a Democrat, and attorney Steven Levine, a Republican. Johnson would take the seat held by Caroline Crenshaw, for a term running to June 5, 2029. Levine would take the seat held by Hester Peirce, who led the SEC's Crypto Task Force and left the agency in October, for a term to June 5, 2030. For the CFTC, Trump named Nathan Anonick, a Republican, for a term to June 19, 2031, and Matthew Rowland, a Democrat, to complete a term ending April 13, 2028.

The bipartisan shape of the slate is the story. Trump has so far in his term sought to remove Democrats from commissions and had not nominated any Democrats to the SEC or CFTC until now, according to CoinDesk. Pairing a Democrat and a Republican at each agency is a break with that pattern, and it arrives just as the Senate is about to change hands.

Both agencies are thinly staffed. The SEC currently has two sitting commissioners, Chairman Paul Atkins and Commissioner Mark Uyeda, out of five seats. The CFTC has just one, Chairman Mike Selig. If all four nominees are confirmed, the SEC would have four of its five seats filled and the CFTC three of five — still short of full strength, but no longer a skeleton crew.

The nominees are not outsiders to the issues the agencies face. Johnson served as a CFTC commissioner from 2022 until September 2025, appointed by President Joe Biden, and in 2023 she argued that decentralized finance platforms acting as intermediaries "can and should be regulated." Levine's latest post was general counsel and a senior adviser at the White House Council of Economic Advisers; before that he worked at the SEC, where Uyeda named him deputy chief of staff in February 2025. No public stance on crypto from Levine has been found on the record.

The two CFTC picks both come out of the Senate Agriculture Committee, which shares jurisdiction over crypto market structure. Anonick, a Republican from Virginia, works for committee Chairman John Boozman as legislative counsel and helped negotiate the Agriculture Committee's share of the CLARITY Act, the crypto market-structure bill. Rowland, a Democrat, is a CFTC attorney detailed to the committee who worked under Ranking Member Amy Klobuchar and former Chairwoman Debbie Stabenow, and had a hand in the CLARITY Act as well as the committee's 2024 crypto bill.

The calendar is the problem. The Senate Banking Committee handles the SEC picks and the Agriculture Committee handles the CFTC picks, and Senate Republican leaders had set November 20 as the date for committees to clear nominees so they could be bundled into one last batch before the lame-duck session ends. Nominations still pending when the current Congress ends on January 3 are returned to the president, which would force the process to start over under a Democratic Senate.

There is some reason to think these four could beat that deadline. Unchained reported that the bipartisan slate and cross-aisle interest in staffing both agencies could give Senate leaders reason to make exceptions to the cutoff, and CFTC Chairman Selig publicly welcomed the Rowland and Anonick nominations. The crypto industry has also been pressing for full commissions, arguing that thin staffing has made decisions on digital assets harder to reach.

The stakes go beyond crypto. The SEC and CFTC together oversee the plumbing of American financial markets, from stock trading to derivatives, and both have been operating with limited slates since Trump retook the White House in January 2025. The nominations also land as the CLARITY Act, the crypto market-structure bill, faces a January 3 deadline of its own — a deadline Orbit reported this week — and the shape of the two commissions will help decide how any new crypto rules are actually enforced.

What happens next is a committee sprint. Banking and Agriculture must hold hearings and vote the nominees out before the full Senate can act, and the lame-duck session has only weeks left. If the Senate confirms all four, Trump will have filled the regulators with a bipartisan slate in his final weeks of unified control. If it does not, the seats stay empty into January, and the decision passes to a Democratic majority that may have different ideas about who should police the markets.

Why it matters: This is a small story with a large tell: Trump, who spent his second term removing Democrats from independent commissions, just sent the Senate a bipartisan slate for the two market regulators — because the clock forced his hand. The SEC and CFTC have been running nearly empty for a year, and the nominations expire with the Congress on January 3. Whether the Senate confirms them in the lame-duck sprint will decide who writes and enforces the rules for crypto and derivatives in 2027, and it connects directly to the CLARITY Act deadline Orbit has been tracking.
