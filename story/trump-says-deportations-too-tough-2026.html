Trump Says Deportations Were 'Too Tough' and Opens Door to Immigration Deal With Democrats

In his first major interview since the midterm elections, the president conceded his crackdown may have gone too far and said a deal with the incoming Democratic Congress was doable.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 9, 2026 · 4 min readFacebookX

President Donald Trump said his administration's mass deportation campaign may have been too tough, conceding in a television interview broadcast Sunday that the crackdown hurt Republicans in last week's midterm elections and opening the door to an immigration deal with Democrats. It was the president's most extensive public assessment yet of the historic losses that cost his party control of Congress.

In the interview with CBS's "60 Minutes," taped on Friday and broadcast on Sunday, correspondent Ross Douthat asked Trump whether the deportation policy had gone too far. "I think it was too tough," Trump said. "I think it might be too tough. Yeah." He gave himself an "A+" for closing the border, but said the pursuit of mass deportations had cost his party at the polls.

"For getting people out, I get very high marks for getting the bad ones out," Trump said. But he added: "When you go into a hotel and you have a woman that's worked there as a maid, as a cleaner, as a this, as a that, as a waitress, a lotta good things, great job, and all of a sudden, they're being taken outta the country, it's tough." He pointed in particular to immigrants who have lived in the country for ten years or more without committing serious crimes.

Trump went further, saying he would like to reach an agreement with the Democrats who will control both chambers of Congress when they take their seats in January. "I think it, well, the deal would be we would work something out," he said. "And I think it's something that's actually doable." He said he would "love to do something on immigration" and indicated a deal could create legal status for some undocumented workers, including people holding good jobs in hotels, landscaping and other industries, in some cases paying taxes.

The concession matched what voters told pollsters on election day. An Associated Press survey of more than 100,000 voters found that about six in ten said the Trump administration's actions on immigration enforcement had gone too far. The administration deported nearly one million people during the first 18 months since January 2025, and about 71 percent of those deported had no criminal conviction, according to an October 2026 analysis by the Migration Policy Institute.

At the same time, the administration delivered on its promise to shut down illegal immigration at the southern border. Border Patrol encounters in fiscal year 2026 were down 93 percent compared with fiscal year 2024, the final year of the Biden administration. Trump has repeatedly cited that drop as the central achievement of his second term, and he repeated the claim in the interview, saying he had closed the border after 25 million people came in over four years under his predecessor.

The interview also exposed a contradiction at the heart of the administration's next phase. About an hour after the conversation was taped on Friday, Trump named Stephen Miller, the architect of the mass deportation policy, as his new White House chief of staff, replacing Susie Wiles. Miller takes over on Monday, and it is unclear whether the administration is planning any actual change to its immigration enforcement policies, even after the president conceded they were too hardline.

A deal, if one were attempted, would face obstacles on both sides. During his first term, Trump supported legislation giving Dreamers, undocumented immigrants brought to the United States as children, a path to legalization. But any agreement would likely require Trump to accept legal status for long-term residents, while Democrats would be asked to accept restrictions on legal immigration, such as caps on green cards, that many in the party oppose. Experts say the looming battle over the administration's enforcement tactics could further poison any negotiation.

Trump offered no specifics about what a deal would look like and no timetable, describing the arrangement only in broad terms. The interview also covered the midterm losses, his potential successors, the war with Iran, allegations of corruption and artificial intelligence, but immigration was the subject on which he sounded most changed from the candidate who ran on mass deportation in 2024.

What happens next will be decided in the weeks before the new Congress is seated. Democrats have said they plan to investigate the administration's enforcement tactics and to push legislation curbing them, while Trump has said he wants to work with them on immigration. Whether the president's words on Sunday were the start of a negotiation or a passing reflection after a bad election will become clear when the two sides sit down in January.
