Justice Department Drops Bid to Block Certification of Peltola's Alaska Senate Win

The Justice Department asked the Ninth Circuit to dismiss its lawsuit as moot after Mary Peltola won an outright majority, conceding there is no way to determine how voters meant to vote.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 21, 2026 · 4 min readFacebookX

The Justice Department on Thursday dropped its appeal in its attempt to block certification of this month's Alaska Senate election, after Democratic challenger Mary Peltola secured more than 50 percent of the vote. In a filing to the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit, Associate Attorney General Stanley Woodward asked the appeals court to dismiss the case, as the government reluctantly acknowledged there was no viable path to investigate the vote with the ballots already counted and no longer identifiable.

The results made the lawsuit's premise collapse. With about 99 percent of the votes counted, Peltola was ranked first by 50.3 percent of voters under Alaska's ranked-choice voting system, compared with 45.9 percent for Republican incumbent Senator Dan S. Sullivan and 1.7 percent for the other Sullivan. Another 2.1 percent of first-choice votes went to a fourth candidate. Those results mean that even if every first-choice vote for someone other than Peltola were grouped together, Peltola would still win the election. The arithmetic checks out: adding Sullivan's 45.9 percent to the other Sullivan's 1.7 percent gives 47.6 percent, still below Peltola's 50.3 percent.

The Justice Department had sought to preemptively delay certification of the vote in a lawsuit filed before the midterms, so it could probe whether a candidate with a similar name to the Republican incumbent had led to voter confusion. The ballot carried two men named Sullivan: the incumbent, listed as Sullivan, Dan S., Registered Republican, Incumbent, and an independent challenger listed simply as Sullivan, Daniel J. Jr. The department argued that certification should be delayed while investigators determined whether voters had been confused, and whether the second Sullivan's presence on the ballot was part of an effort to confuse them.

The race became controversial days before the vote, after the New York Times reported that Peltola's campaign recruited a retired school teacher named Dan J. Sullivan to run. Peltola has said she had no part in or knowledge of the reported effort. The government has also launched a criminal probe into whether that recruitment effort was an illegal attempt to confuse voters, and that investigation continues even as the civil lawsuit is being dropped.

A federal judge had already rejected the department's request before Election Day. U.S. District Judge Sharon Gleason dismissed the lawsuit, saying the government had no authority to force Alaska to segregate ballots, and that segregating votes cast for the second Sullivan would violate Alaskans' right to a secret ballot. The Justice Department appealed that decision to the Ninth Circuit, and on Thursday it told the appeals court that, because the ballots for the lesser-known Dan Sullivan were ultimately commingled with other ballots, there no longer exists any means of determining whether votes cast for Daniel J. Sullivan were meant to be cast for Senator Sullivan.

The department's filing did not simply walk away. Woodward also asked the appeals court to vacate the lower court's ruling dismissing the case, a move that would erase the loss from the books even as the case itself is dismissed as moot. The department argued that the case had become moot because the ballots have already been counted, while still seeking to undo the district court's conclusion that its request was unworkable.

The race itself was called on Tuesday, two weeks after the November 3 election, with the count showing Peltola leading by four percentage points. Her victory, confirmed once she crossed 50 percent of the vote, gave Democrats a Senate majority and made her the ninth Senate pickup for the party in a midterm blowout. The Justice Department moved to have the case declared moot two days after the race was decided.

The end of the certification lawsuit does not end the legal fallout from the Alaska race. The criminal investigation into the recruitment of the second Sullivan continues, and the department's request to vacate Judge Gleason's ruling is still before the Ninth Circuit. What is settled is the certification fight: Alaska will certify a result in which Peltola won an outright majority, and no court will now delay it.

Why it matters: The dropped appeal matters because it closes the most aggressive attempt by a Justice Department to interfere with a Senate election in recent memory — a pre-election lawsuit, filed at the president's direction, asking a court to halt certification of a race the administration's own candidate was losing. The department's own filing concedes the case was unworkable once ballots were counted, which is what the district judge said before Election Day. What remains is the criminal probe into the spoiler candidate's recruitment, and that investigation, not the certification fight, is now where the Alaska story will be decided.
