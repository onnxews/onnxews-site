Colombia Blames ELN for Deadly Drone Attack as Ombudsman Warns of Worsening Violence

Three soldiers were killed and dozens wounded when explosive-laden drones struck a military base in Bolívar, and a second drone attack was repelled in Cauca the next day.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 10, 2026 · 5 min readFacebookX

Colombia's Defence Ministry has attributed a deadly drone attack on an army base in the country's north to the National Liberation Army, known as the ELN, as the government warned that the armed group is escalating its campaign against the security forces. The attack on Saturday killed three soldiers and wounded dozens more, and it came amid a surge of violence across rural parts of the country that has intensified since President Abelardo de la Espriella, a conservative, took office in August.

The strike hit a military compound at Santa Rosa del Sur, a small town in the foothills of the San Lucas mountain range in Bolívar department, which houses the army's 48th Jungle Battalion. Explosive-laden drones struck the troop barracks during the lunch hour, killing three soldiers and wounding at least 16 people, according to the armed forces. Later reports put the number of wounded far higher, with some accounts saying 47 people were hurt. The casualty figures shifted as the injured were sorted into soldiers and civilians, and the death toll of three has been consistent across reports.

The Defence Ministry said the attack was carried out by the ELN as retaliation for the capture of a commander known by the alias Carrito, a leader of the group's Guamocó Resistance Front, and for drone attacks against the group on the Venezuelan side of the border that were carried out this month in collaboration with the United States. No group has publicly claimed responsibility for the attack, and the army had not initially identified those responsible. The social-media footage of the base has not been independently verified.

A second drone attack was reported on Sunday on the Pan-American Highway, at a tollbooth in Cauca, about 30 minutes outside the city of Cali. The armed forces announced that they managed to repel that attempt, and no casualties were reported. The two attacks in two days underscored how cheap drones have changed the nature of the conflict in Colombia, allowing armed groups to strike military installations from a distance with weapons that are difficult to detect and even harder to stop.

On Monday, Colombia's state ombudsman issued an urgent alert over deteriorating security in southern Bolívar, two days after the bomb attack on the army base killed three soldiers. The Ombudsman's Office said the attack was not an isolated incident, and it warned that civilians in the region face growing risks as armed groups fight for control of territory. The area is rich in gold mines and has long been disputed by rebel armed groups, including the Gulf Clan, the National Liberation Army and smaller groups led by former members of the Revolutionary Armed Forces of Colombia, the FARC.

President de la Espriella wrote on social media that those behind the attack would have nowhere to hide, adding that every attack against soldiers strengthens the resolve to defend Colombia. He said the state would use all legitimate force to dismantle the criminal organizations responsible. The president, who took office in August, has taken a harder line against armed groups than his predecessor, and the violence has intensified in several rural areas since he assumed power, with both state forces and illegal armed groups carrying out attacks with bombs and explosives.

The scale of the violence is documented by the Armed Conflict Location and Event Data project, which counted 34 attacks with bombs or explosives carried out by state forces and illegal armed groups in Colombia in the four weeks to October 16, a 9 percent increase on last year's four-week average. The figures capture only the attacks that are reported and verified, and the true number is likely higher. For the soldiers stationed in remote bases like Santa Rosa del Sur, the numbers translate into a daily reality of living under threat from an enemy that can strike without warning.

The attack also comes as the government moves to end the Total Peace policy of the previous administration, replacing it with a new law on submission to justice for armed groups. The shift reflects de la Espriella's view that negotiation with groups that continue to attack the state is futile, and that the priority must be to defeat them militarily. Critics of the approach warn that it risks escalating the violence further, while supporters say it is the only response to groups that have used past ceasefires to regroup and rearm.

For the families of the three soldiers killed on Saturday, the debate over policy is secondary to the loss. The army had not released the names of the dead soldiers at the time of reporting, and the wounded were being treated in hospitals across the region. The ombudsman's alert is a reminder that the conflict is not confined to military bases: the civilians who live in the gold-rich hills of southern Bolívar, caught between the army and the armed groups, face the same drones, the same bombs and the same fear, with far less protection than the soldiers they are meant to live beside.
