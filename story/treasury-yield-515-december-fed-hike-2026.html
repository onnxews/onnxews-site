10-Year Treasury Yield Jumps to 5.15% as Hot Inflation Locks In a December Fed Hike

The benchmark yield hit its highest close since mid-October after producer prices rose 6.5 percent from a year earlier, leaving traders pricing in a near-certain rate hike next month.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 15, 2026 · 5 min readFacebookX

The yield on the 10-year U.S. Treasury note jumped to 5.15 percent on Friday, its highest close since mid-October, as a hotter-than-expected inflation report locked in expectations of a Federal Reserve interest rate hike in December. The benchmark yield, which sets the floor for borrowing costs across the economy, touched 5.17 percent during the session before settling at 5.15 percent, up from 5.10 percent on Thursday and 4.86 percent a week earlier. The 10-year yield rose 5.3 basis points on Friday to 5.156 percent, its highest level in almost a month, according to data from the dpa-AFX wire, capping a 29-basis-point climb over the week.

The catalyst was the producer price index, which rose 0.3 percent in October from the previous month and 6.5 percent from a year earlier, the largest annual increase since November 2022. Economists had expected a 0.4 percent monthly rise, but the yearly figure was the shock: goods prices rose 0.5 percent on the month and services prices rose 0.2 percent, extending a fifth consecutive month of increases in services, while food prices jumped and energy prices edged lower. September's monthly reading was revised up to 1.4 percent.

The report followed Tuesday's consumer price index, which rose 0.4 percent on the month with core inflation up 0.3 percent, though the Bureau of Labor Statistics did not publish 12-month changes for most of the October index because it could not collect October 2025 data during the federal funding lapse. Taken together, the two reports told the same story: price pressures are not fading, and the cost of producing goods and services is rising faster than at any point in four years, which is why traders moved so quickly to price in a December hike.

Traders responded by pricing in a December hike almost as a certainty. Futures markets tracked by the CME FedWatch tool put the chance of a quarter-point increase at the Federal Reserve's December 8-9 meeting at about 90 percent, with roughly a 10 percent chance of a larger half-point move. The federal funds rate currently stands at 3.75 to 4 percent, and at the Fed's October 28 meeting two policymakers, Lisa Cook and Beth Hammack, dissented because they wanted a quarter-point hike then. Another quarter-point increase in January is now increasingly likely, according to dpa-AFX, and LPL Financial chief economist Jeffrey Roach warned that a larger 50-basis-point increase in December "could pose unnecessary risks" to the economy.

The move rippled across the whole yield curve. The 2-year Treasury yield, which is most sensitive to Fed policy, rose to 4.88 percent from 4.51 percent a week earlier, while the 30-year yield climbed to 5.47 percent and the 3-month bill stood at 4.32 percent. The 10-year's October high was 5.31 percent on October 5, so Friday's close leaves the benchmark within reach of its recent peak. For borrowers, the implications are direct: the average 30-year fixed mortgage rate fell for a fourth straight week to 7.11 percent on Thursday, according to Freddie Mac, but a 10-year yield at 5.15 percent limits how much further mortgage rates can fall, since lenders price home loans off the benchmark.

The inflation data also hit stocks, which have been caught between strong earnings and rising borrowing costs. "For equities, 6.5% producer inflation matters because companies can get squeezed from both sides," said Chris Osmond, chief investment officer at Fifth Third Wealth Advisors. "Persistent input costs pressure margins, while higher bond yields raise the discount rate investors apply to those earnings." The squeeze is not theoretical: a $10,000 bond fund with a six-year duration loses about 1.7 percent, or roughly $174, on a 0.29-point rise in yields, the same move the 10-year made this week. Investors who bought Treasuries for safety have watched their holdings fall as yields climbed, a reminder that in an inflationary environment even government bonds can lose money.

The week's inflation reports land at a sensitive moment for the Fed, with producer-side inflation running well above its 2 percent target. The December meeting will be the last of the year, and a hike there would signal that the Fed is willing to tighten even as the economy shows signs of slowing. The market's near-certainty about December, combined with growing expectations for January, suggests traders believe the Fed has decided that inflation is the bigger risk, and that it will act accordingly even if growth softens.

For households and businesses, the practical consequence of a 5.15 percent 10-year yield is that borrowing stays expensive for longer. Mortgage rates near 7 percent, corporate borrowing costs tied to the benchmark, and the discount rate applied to future earnings all point in the same direction: the era of cheap money is not returning soon. The December Fed meeting on December 8 and 9 will be the next test, and the question is no longer whether the Fed will hike, but by how much, and whether January brings another one.

Why it matters: A 5.15 percent 10-year yield matters because it is the price of money for the whole economy: mortgages, corporate borrowing and the valuation of every stock are all priced off it. The producer inflation reading of 6.5 percent a year, the largest in four years, has convinced markets that the Fed will hike in December and probably again in January, which means borrowing stays expensive well into 2027. The squeeze on companies is real and two-sided, with input costs rising while higher yields cut the value of future earnings, and the bond market is now pricing in a Fed that has decided inflation is the bigger risk. What matters next is the December 8-9 meeting, where the only question left is the size of the hike.
