Stacy Keach, 'Mike Hammer' Star and Acclaimed Stage Actor, Dies at 85

Stacy Keach, the Golden Globe winner who played private eye Mike Hammer on television and was counted among America's finest Shakespearean actors, died Tuesday at his family home in Poland.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 12, 2026 · 5 min readFacebookX

Stacy Keach, the Golden Globe-winning actor who portrayed private investigator Mike Hammer on television and was regarded as one of America's finest Shakespearean actors, has died. He was 85. Keach died Tuesday, November 10, at his family home in Magdalenka, Poland, his wife Malgosia Keach wrote in a statement shared by the actor's representative.

“After a long and difficult battle with a serious heart condition and heart failure, Stacy peacefully departed this world,” she said. “He fought for so long, with extraordinary courage, determination and strength. He was a true warrior, facing his illness with the same remarkable spirit and resilience that defined his life.” She described the Blue Bloods star as “not only a legendary actor, admired and loved by audiences the world over, but also my beloved husband, my companion and the heart of our family.”

Keach made his mark on television as Mike Hammer, the hard-boiled detective from Mickey Spillane's novels, which he had read when he was young. He first took on the role in the 1983 TV movie “Murder Me, Murder You.” He reprised the character in several TV movies and series, including “The New Mike Hammer” and “Mike Hammer, Private Eye,” over the next 15 years. In the 1980s and '90s, his fedora and signature mustache — which covered a scar from cleft palate operations as a child — served him well in the role.

His range went far beyond the trench coat. He played outlaw Frank James in the Western “The Long Riders.” He was the bumbling Sgt. Stedenko in the Cheech and Chong comedies “Up in Smoke” and “Nice Dreams,” boxer Tully in 1972's “Fat City” and the antagonistic patriarch Ken in the Fox sitcom “Titus.” He narrated numerous documentaries for National Geographic and PBS, and late in his career appeared in prestige films such as Alexander Payne's “Nebraska” in 2013.

His first love, though, was the stage. He was recognized as one of the preeminent American Shakespearean actors, having performed in productions of “King Lear,” “Hamlet,” “Henry V” and “Coriolanus,” and he received a Tony nomination in 1970 for playing Buffalo Bill in the Broadway production of “Indians.” He said his toughest role was Macbeth: “Of all the major tragic roles in the canon, it's probably the most difficult,” he told NPR in 1995. He also won a Golden Globe for his portrayal of Ernest Hemingway in a television miniseries.

Keach was born in 1941 in Savannah, Georgia, and grew up in Southern California. He was a University of California, Berkeley, graduate who received a Master of Fine Arts degree at the Yale School of Drama and studied on a Fulbright scholarship at the London Academy of Music and Dramatic Art. He was the son of the late actor Stacy Keach Sr. and Mary Cain Peckham Keach, and acted alongside his younger brother James Keach on screen, including in “The Long Riders.”

His career was interrupted in 1984, when he was arrested at Heathrow Airport for bringing cocaine into Britain. He served six months in prison, which, he said, gave his subsequent roles more depth. “Anybody who has an experience like that certainly carries it with them,” he told NPR's Scott Simon in 1986. “And from an artistic point of view, utilizes it — or hopefully utilizes it — in a way that's going to be positive and productive. I think that the most important thing from my point of view was not taking freedom for granted.”

Nearly a decade ago, his health faltered on stage. He had a heart attack onstage in 2017 during the opening night of “Pamplona,” in which he played Hemingway. “After 11 successful previews, on opening night the unthinkable occurred. I had a mild heart attack onstage,” he wrote in a 2018 New York Times essay. When he saw a doctor the next day, tests showed seriously clogged arteries, and he underwent a six-hour triple bypass operation. By 2018 he found his health had never been better, his recovery aided by diet and exercise.

Keach is survived by his wife Malgosia Keach, their children Karolina and Shannon Keach and their grandchildren. His career spanned more than half a century across stage and screen, from Shakespeare's great tragic roles to a fedora-wearing private eye — a range few actors of his generation matched.

Why it matters: Keach's death closes a career that bridged two acting worlds that rarely meet: the classical stage, where he was counted among America's best Shakespeareans, and the populist detective series that made him a household name. The Mike Hammer role could have typecast him; instead he kept returning to Hamlet, Lear and Macbeth between television jobs, and the stage work kept the screen work honest. His 1984 imprisonment and his onstage heart attack became part of the story he told about himself — a performer who treated setbacks as material. What endures is the range itself: a career that ran from Buffalo Bill to Hemingway to a fedora in a dark office, and never settled for just one of them.
