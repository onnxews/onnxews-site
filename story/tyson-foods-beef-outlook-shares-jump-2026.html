Tyson Shares Jump as the Meatpacker Sees Its Beef Losses Shrinking

Tyson Foods led the S&P 500 on Monday after reporting a fourth-quarter beat and forecasting that its loss-making beef business could approach break-even in fiscal 2027 — even as it warned of lower sales and weaker chicken profits.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 17, 2026 · 5 min readFacebookX

Tyson Foods was one of Monday's few big gainers, with shares climbing as much as 11 percent in early trading and closing up nearly 9 percent to lead S&P 500 advancers, after the meatpacker reported better-than-expected quarterly earnings and said there were signs conditions were improving in its battered beef business. The rally stood out on a day when the broader market fell sharply, with the Nasdaq dropping 2.5 percent.

The trigger was Tyson's fiscal fourth-quarter report, released before the bell on Monday. The company reported adjusted earnings of 61 cents a share for the quarter ended October 3, above the 58-cent estimate cited by Reuters, and sales of $14.59 billion, up 5.2 percent from $13.86 billion a year earlier and ahead of the roughly $14.04 billion expected, helped by a 53rd week in the quarter. The stock opened at $60.90, climbed to $65.34 at 9:50 a.m. — 11.1 percent above Friday's $58.82 close — and then gave back more than a third of the gain as investors read the rest of the outlook.

The quarter itself was weaker than it looked. Adjusted operating income fell 41 percent to $361 million, from $608 million a year earlier, and GAAP earnings were a loss of 22 cents a share. The beef segment reported a quarterly adjusted operating loss of $171 million, compared with a loss of $52 million a year earlier, and chicken income fell to $389 million from $562 million. Excluding the extra week and legal accruals, sales fell 4.0 percent. Legal contingency accruals cut fourth-quarter GAAP operating income to just $1 million.

What moved the stock was not the quarter but the outlook for beef. Tyson forecast a fiscal 2027 result for the segment of between an adjusted loss of $300 million and an adjusted income of $100 million, compared with a loss of $654 million in fiscal 2026 — a midpoint improvement of about $550 million, and a swing of more than $750 million at the top of the range. 'There are early signs industry economics are improving,' Chief Operating Officer Wes Morris said on a conference call. 'As cattle costs ease and wholesale prices hold, packer margins are moving up from deeply negative levels.'

Beef has been the problem for two years. Tyson shuttered beef plants this year and laid off thousands of workers as soaring costs for cattle outpaced gains from record-high beef prices, and the company said its cattle costs jumped about $2.1 billion in 2026 from 2025. Executives said the closures would benefit the company over the next year, amid signs that U.S. ranchers are starting to rebuild a cattle herd decimated by drought, and Washington has resumed imports of Mexican cattle, which it halted last year in an attempt to keep out a flesh-eating livestock pest. 'The phased reopening of the Mexican border is a tailwind for fiscal 2027 and beyond, although the start has been relatively slow,' Morris said.

The rest of the outlook was weaker, which is why the stock faded from its highs. Tyson forecast total adjusted operating income of $1.7 billion to $2.15 billion in fiscal 2027, compared with $1.98 billion in fiscal 2026, and estimated revenue will decrease 3.5 percent to 5.5 percent as plant closures and lower cattle supplies limit beef sales volumes — a steeper drop than the roughly 1 percent decline analysts had expected. Chicken, which earned $1.86 billion in fiscal 2026 and carried the company, is guided to $1.3 billion to $1.6 billion as the company expects higher feed ingredient costs. The pork unit is projected to earn $75 million to $175 million, down from $228 million in 2026, as processors struggle with weak demand.

The balance sheet gave investors something to hold onto. For the full year, sales rose 3.6 percent to $56.42 billion, while adjusted operating income fell 14 percent to $1.977 billion and adjusted earnings fell 17 percent to $3.44 a share. Free cash flow rose $486 million to $1.66 billion, the company repaid $1.08 billion of debt and bought back 7.8 million shares for $427 million, and the board raised the quarterly dividend about 2 percent, to $0.52 a Class A share. Prepared foods delivered annual operating income of $1.33 billion.

Chief Financial Officer Curt Calaway sounded a note of caution about the year ahead. 'Market-driven headwinds, mainly in chicken and pork, could outweigh the controllable improvement we are making across the businesses,' he said. The U.S. Department of Agriculture projects beef production down about 2 percent in fiscal 2027, which keeps cattle expensive while Tyson restructures its beef network around three facilities in the central United States.

The market chose the beef story over the sales forecast, and the test of that choice comes in February, when Tyson reports its fiscal first-quarter results — the first under the new guidance, with a date not yet announced. Investors will be watching whether beef results are at or above break-even, whether chicken earns more than $400 million in the quarter, and whether the company raises the bottom of its $1.7 billion operating income range. Until then, the rally is a bet on a recovery that has not started yet.

Why it matters: Tyson's rally is a reminder that markets trade on the future, not the past: the quarter was weak across the board, but investors bought the idea that the worst of the cattle crisis is over. The risk is that the recovery depends on cattle supplies, border policy and a restructuring that has not yet delivered — while chicken and pork, the businesses that actually make money, are guided lower. If beef does not narrow its losses in the first quarter, the 11 percent intraday spike will look like a head fake; if it does, Tyson's two-year slump may finally be turning.
