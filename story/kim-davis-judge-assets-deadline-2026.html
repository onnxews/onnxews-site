Judge Orders Kim Davis to Explain Hidden Inheritance as $565,000 Judgment Deadline Hits

Former Kentucky county clerk Kim Davis faces a Monday deadline to tell a federal judge why she should not be sanctioned after the court found she gave false answers about an inheritance while owing $565,000 to the gay couple she refused to marry.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 16, 2026 · 4 min readFacebookX

A federal judge has ordered former Rowan County Clerk Kim Davis to explain why she did not disclose an inheritance that is now the subject of a state lawsuit, and gave her until Monday, November 16, to answer. The order, from U.S. Magistrate Judge Edward Adkins, stops short of holding Davis in contempt for now, but it puts her on notice that sanctions are on the table if her answers do not satisfy the court.

In a ruling on Friday, Adkins declined to hold Davis in contempt of court for allegedly hiding an inheritance, according to a filing in the U.S. Eastern District of Kentucky. Instead, he ordered her to respond to allegations she knowingly withheld information about an inheritance to avoid paying hundreds of thousands of dollars in outstanding damages. The order gives Davis's team a November 16 deadline to provide full and complete responses and to explain why she should not be sanctioned.

The dispute turns on what Davis told the court about her assets after a federal jury ruled she owed damages to David Moore and David Ermold, the couple she refused a marriage license in 2015. In post-judgment discovery, Davis said she owned no real estate, made no large transfers and was not a beneficiary of any estate. The couple's lawyers later found certified records in Rowan County showing she had inherited 50 percent of her mother's estate and signed deeds in 2025 transferring property to family members.

"Defendant does not deny the existence of these certified public records, does not address if or why these documents are outside of the scope of Plaintiffs' requests, and gives no reason for not producing complete responses and documents," the order reads. Despite finding the records "fall squarely" within the scope of discovery, Adkins declined to hold Davis in contempt.

The lawsuit says Davis and her brother, Darold Rayburn Bailey Jr., inherited a $735,230 property from their mother last year, and Davis transferred her share of the property to her brother, a sister-in-law, husband and son between April and May 2025. The cash value of Davis's share is more than $200,000, according to court filings. In a separate lawsuit filed earlier this year, the couple accused Davis in Rowan Circuit Court of lying under oath about what properties and assets she owned in order to avoid paying.

The money at stake has grown sharply since the original verdict. In 2023, a federal jury ruled Davis owed $100,000 in damages to Moore and Ermold. After attorneys' fees and court expenses, that amount has now ballooned to $565,285.91. The couple is asking the state court to void her property transfers, block further transfers of her assets, and apply her estate funds toward the payments she owes them.

Davis gained national attention in 2015 when she refused to issue marriage licenses to same-sex couples on the grounds it violated her religious beliefs, shortly after the Supreme Court's landmark Obergefell v. Hodges ruling legalized same-sex marriage nationwide. Her denials were ruled unconstitutional, and she was jailed for contempt after refusing to follow a court order to issue the licenses. In November 2025, the U.S. Supreme Court declined to hear an appeal in Davis's case, one that could have threatened Obergefell.

The couple's attorney, Michael Gartland, told WEKU he is not ruling out an appeal of the decision not to hold Davis in contempt. "I would have held her in contempt because this court already held her in contempt and put her in jail years ago for failing to issue the licenses after the court ordered her to do so," Gartland said. "So I thought there was a chance."

Davis's lawyers have argued that the proposed motions in federal court were premature and that an order could not be made until the outcome of the lawsuit in Rowan Circuit Court, where Ermold and Moore are looking to undo Davis's transfers. Mat Staver, Liberty Counsel chairman and Davis's attorney, told the Lexington Herald-Leader she did not lie under oath and answered specific questions with specific answers. A spokesperson for Liberty Counsel told WEKU they would comment only after they had responded to the judge's order.

Why it matters: This case has always been about more than one clerk and one couple: Davis's 2015 refusal became a national flashpoint over religious belief and civil rights, and the Supreme Court's decision last November not to hear her appeal left Obergefell intact. What is at stake now is narrower but still consequential — whether a court judgment can actually be collected when the person who owes it is accused of moving assets to family members to keep them out of reach. If the judge finds her answers unsatisfactory, sanctions or a contempt finding could follow, and the state lawsuit could unwind the transfers themselves.
