Apple Says Internal Update — Not a Cyberattack — Knocked Out iCloud, App Store and Apple Pay

A botched internal update disrupted Apple services for hours on Tuesday, signing some users out of their accounts and blocking purchases in stores, the company said.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 18, 2026 · 4 min readFacebookX

Apple said an internal update, not a cyberattack, caused a widespread outage on Tuesday that knocked out iCloud, the App Store, Apple Music and Apple Pay for hours and left some users signed out of their accounts. The company moved quickly to rule out an attack after reports of the disruption spread, telling Bloomberg and the Associated Press that its systems had not been targeted.

The outage hit a long list of services. Apple's status page showed problems with iCloud Account and Sign In, the App Store, Apple Music, AppleCare on Device, iCloud Mail, iCloud.com, Apple Pay, Apple Support and iPhone Activation, along with some business and education services including Apple Business Manager and Apple School Manager. Some users reported being signed out of their Apple Accounts and hitting "Failed to verify your identity" errors when they tried to sign back in.

The scale of the disruption was visible in user reports. On Downdetector, reports about Apple Pay spiked at about 11:40 a.m. Eastern time and peaked at about 2,300 reports in the United States around 12:20 p.m. Reports about iCloud peaked at about 1,800 around noon, while Apple Support reports crossed 2,100 and the App Store drew about 900 reports. The numbers are self-reported and do not measure the full number of people affected, but they show the outage hitting around midday on the East Coast.

Apple's statement was brief. "Many of our systems are back up and running after an internal update caused some outages this morning. We're working to get the remaining systems online as soon as possible," the company said. The App Store was resolved earlier in the afternoon, at about 1 p.m. Eastern time, while iCloud sign-in and AppleCare on Device were restored by about 4:45 p.m.

By about 5:24 p.m. Eastern time, Apple's System Status page showed all of the affected programs as available, and a company spokesperson told CBS News that services were "green across the board" at about 5:46 p.m. There were no unresolved incidents by 6 p.m. Eastern time, bringing the disruption to an end after most of the working day.

The outage reached into Apple's stores as well. Staff at Apple Store locations in New York and Chicago said internal software failures temporarily blocked purchases and appointment bookings, leaving customers unable to complete transactions while the systems were down. For a company whose stores are also its showrooms, the failure was visible in the most literal sense.

Tuesday's disruption followed a run of outages elsewhere. A banking outage on Monday affected Chase, Wells Fargo, Bank of America and the payments service Zelle, and last week brought a Microsoft 365 outage and a Federal Aviation Administration communications outage. The cluster has put the resilience of everyday digital infrastructure back in the spotlight.

What Apple has not said is what exactly went wrong. The company has not identified the specific system or deployment process that failed, and it has not said whether it will publish a fuller explanation of the incident. That leaves the central question — how an internal update could take down so many services at once — unanswered for now. USA Today reported the outage and Apple's account of it on Tuesday, and TechRepublic covered the scope of the affected services.

Why it matters: The outage matters less for how long it lasted — most services were back within hours — than for what it exposed. A single internal update took down the services that handle Apple's payments, its app store, its cloud storage and its customer support at the same time, and it signed users out of the accounts that tie all of those together. That is the risk of a tightly integrated ecosystem: when the plumbing fails, everything fails together. Apple has ruled out an attack, which is reassuring, but the absence of any explanation of what went wrong means customers and developers have no way to judge whether it will happen again.
