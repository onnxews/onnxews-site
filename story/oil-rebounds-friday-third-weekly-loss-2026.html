Oil Rebounds Friday on Gulf Attack Fears but Posts Third Straight Weekly Loss

Brent settled at $92.57 and WTI at $83.98 after Houthi strikes on Riyadh and tanker attacks in Hormuz, but both benchmarks still posted a third straight weekly loss.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 21, 2026 · 6 min readFacebookX

Oil prices rebounded late on Friday on supply fears after Houthi missiles were fired toward Riyadh and reports of tanker disruptions in the Strait of Hormuz, but both benchmarks still closed a third straight weekly loss. Brent crude for January settled up 59 cents, or 0.64 percent, at $92.57 a barrel, while U.S. West Texas Intermediate for December gained 77 cents, or 0.93 percent, to $83.98 on its expiry day. The more active January WTI contract settled at $83.41, up 97 cents. The Friday bounce came despite a week in which the dominant story was recovering Gulf flows and a looming supply surplus.

For the week, Brent fell $3.27, or 3.41 percent, from $95.84, and WTI fell $4.58, or 5.17 percent, from $88.56. Both benchmarks are on track for November declines of about 20 percent. Brent had traded at $92.22 in early Friday Asian trade, up 24 cents, and WTI at $83.40, up 19 cents. Both benchmarks settled little changed on Thursday — Brent added only 3 cents to end at $91.98, while WTI lost 5 cents to finish at $83.21, its weakest close since late August — before the Friday headlines lifted them.

The Friday bounce was driven by fresh security concerns in the Middle East. The Iran-aligned Houthi movement fired missiles at Riyadh, the latest blow to the kingdom in its war with the Houthis, with damage reported from missile debris in the capital, including the financial district. At the same time, Iran’s Revolutionary Guards Navy said two oil tankers had struck naval mines in the Strait of Hormuz and caught fire, a claim carried by Iranian state media that Reuters said it could not independently verify. The UK Maritime Trade Operations agency reported that a commercial tanker outbound from the Persian Gulf was struck on its port bow by an unidentified projectile 8 nautical miles north of Khasab, Oman, resulting in an onboard fire. The agency has reported six attacks in the past week.

The tanker attacks are the latest in a running series of incidents in the world’s most important oil chokepoint. ADNOC Logistics & Services, a unit of Abu Dhabi’s national oil company, confirmed on Wednesday that one of its vessels was attacked while transiting the Strait of Hormuz on the evening of November 17, and that one crew member remains unaccounted for, with search and rescue operations ongoing. The company did not identify the vessel, its cargo or who carried out the attack. Iran’s Revolutionary Guards said on Tuesday that a ship carrying liquefied natural gas and two oil tankers had caught fire in the waterway, a claim Reuters said it could not independently verify, and crude shipments through Hormuz have since climbed back close to where they stood before the war — a recovery that has undercut prices all month.

That recovery is the reason the Friday bounce did not last into a weekly gain. Traders are still expecting a supply surplus because crude flows through the Hormuz waterway have returned to roughly pre-war levels, and expectations of a surplus plus the U.S. stockpile build dominated the week. The International Energy Agency sees a supply surplus even as Hormuz attacks continue. Brent is pacing for a 3.5 percent weekly decline and the December WTI contract for a roughly 6 percent fall, marking a third consecutive weekly drop for both benchmarks. The Friday bounce matters because it shows how sensitive oil still is to Middle East headlines — missiles fired toward Riyadh and disruption reports in Hormuz were enough to lift both benchmarks late in the session, even after a week in which fundamentals ground prices down day after day.

What to watch next is which story wins the coming week: the supply-fear headlines — the Riyadh strike, the Hormuz tanker disruption reports — or the fundamentals that dominated this week, recovering Gulf flows, an expected supply surplus and the U.S. stockpile build. Another important development for the energy market is the upcoming OPEC+ meeting scheduled for November 29, 2026, where producers will decide whether to adjust output in the face of falling prices. For now, the market has decided there is too much oil, not too little — and a one-day bounce on war headlines has not changed that verdict.

The mechanics of Friday’s session also mattered. The December WTI contract expired on Friday, which often brings choppy trading as positions roll into the next month, and the more active January contract — the one most traders now watch — settled at $83.41, up 97 cents. Brent’s January contract, the global benchmark, settled at $92.57. Dubai crude, the Middle East benchmark, was quoted at $91.47 a barrel. The spreads between the benchmarks remain narrow, a sign that the market sees the Gulf disruption as a regional shipping problem rather than a global supply shock — for now.

The attacks themselves show no sign of stopping. The UK maritime agency has reported six attacks in the past week, and on the morning of Saturday, November 21, monitors confirmed a second kinetic impact against a tanker 11 nautical miles north of Khasab, with a projectile striking the vessel’s stern near the engine room. Iran’s Revolutionary Guard Corps also claimed to have shot down a U.S. MQ-9 Reaper drone over the Strait of Hormuz on Saturday, according to Iranian state media. With an Iranian flotilla of more than a dozen laden crude tankers reported to be departing Kharg Island for East Asia, the strait — through which a fifth of the world’s oil passes — is now functionally contested, and every tanker transit is a fresh test of whether the Friday rebound was a one-day scare or the start of a risk premium that sticks.

Why it matters: Oil’s Friday rebound is a reminder that the market now runs on two different stories at once. The fear story — missiles over Riyadh, tankers hit near Hormuz, a crew member missing — can lift prices in an afternoon. The fundamentals story — Hormuz flows back to pre-war levels, a looming surplus, a U.S. stockpile build — grinds them down all week, and it has now produced three straight weekly losses and a November decline of about 20 percent. The OPEC+ meeting on November 29 is where those two stories collide: producers must decide whether to defend prices by cutting output, or accept that the market has decided there is simply too much oil.
