October Inflation Rose 0.4%, Hotter Than Expected, With No Annual Rate to Compare

Consumer prices rose 0.4 percent in October, beating forecasts, in a report with no annual inflation rate because last year's shutdown wiped out the comparison data.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 10, 2026 · 3 min readFacebookX

Consumer prices rose 0.4 percent in October from September, the Labor Department reported on Tuesday, a slower monthly pace than September's 0.6 percent jump but hotter than economists expected, and the last inflation reading before the Federal Reserve's December meeting.

Core prices, which leave out food and energy, rose 0.3 percent for a third straight month. Both the headline and core figures came in 0.1 percentage point above the consensus forecasts of 0.3 percent and 0.2 percent, and above the Federal Reserve Bank of Cleveland's Nowcast projection of a 0.24 percent rise.

The report arrived with an unusual hole in it: there is no official 12-month inflation rate. The Bureau of Labor Statistics did not collect survey data for October 2025 because of last year's government shutdown, so it cannot publish annual percent changes for October 2026 for most series. The last available annual reading, for September, showed prices up 3.7 percent over 12 months.

Inside the monthly numbers, food prices rose 0.5 percent, with groceries up 0.4 percent and restaurant meals up 0.5 percent. Energy prices rose 0.7 percent, slowing sharply from September's 5.2 percent surge, and gasoline rose 1.0 percent after jumping 9.1 percent the month before. Shelter costs rose 0.3 percent and accounted for more than 30 percent of the monthly increase, the bureau said.

The missing annual comparison does not mean prices stopped rising, only that the yardstick is broken for one month. Forecasters will have to judge the report on its monthly pace alone, and a 0.4 percent monthly rise, if sustained, would still be too fast for comfort: compounded over a year, it implies inflation running well above the Federal Reserve's 2 percent target.

That is why the report matters for December. The Federal Reserve meets on December 9, and futures markets have been pricing in another interest rate increase, with CME Group's FedWatch tool putting the odds above 80 percent. A hotter-than-expected reading strengthens the case for a hike; a cooler one would have given the doves on the committee something to work with. Tuesday's numbers gave them little.

The report is also one of the first hard snapshots of the economy since the midterm elections, in which voters cited prices and the cost of living as a central grievance. Average hourly earnings for employees on private nonfarm payrolls were up 0.4 percent last month, according to the department's November 6 estimate, meaning pay roughly kept pace with prices in October but did not get ahead of them.

Markets took the number in stride in early trading. Dow futures were up about 0.4 percent and S&P 500 futures little changed before the release, with Nasdaq-100 futures down about 0.4 percent, and the reaction after the 8:30 a.m. release will set the tone for a session that also features a 10-year Treasury note auction and several Federal Reserve speakers.

The next inflation reading, covering November, is scheduled for December 10, one day after the Fed's meeting, which means Tuesday's report is the last hard data policymakers will see before they decide. With core prices rising 0.3 percent for three months running and no annual rate to soften the picture, the central bank heads into that meeting with inflation still running hotter than it wants, and with little in the data to argue for patience.
