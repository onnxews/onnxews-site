Fred Nile, New South Wales' Longest-Serving Parliamentarian, Dies at 92

The Reverend Fred Nile, who spent more than four decades in the New South Wales Parliament as the state's most recognisable voice of conservative Christian politics, has died at the age of 92.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 22, 2026 · 5 min readFacebookX

The longest-serving parliamentarian in New South Wales history, conservative stalwart Fred Nile, has died. The Christian Democratic Party founder passed away peacefully on Saturday at the age of 92, his family confirmed. His death closes a political career that spanned more than four decades in the state's upper house and made him one of the most recognisable — and most divisive — figures in Australian public life.

Frederick John Nile was born in Sydney on Sept. 15, 1934, volunteering to serve in the army in 1954 before studying theology and becoming ordained as a minister in 1964. He worked as national director of the Australian Christian Endeavour Movement and was involved with the Billy Graham crusade before being appointed NSW director of the Festival of Light, an organisation dedicated to promoting Christian family values. His move into politics came as the founder of the Call to Australia (Fred Nile Group) party, when he was elected to the NSW Legislative Council in 1981.

Nile was re-elected at the state elections of 1991, 1999, 2007 and 2015, and served as assistant president of the Legislative Council from 2007 to 2019. He briefly resigned his seat in 2004 to stand for the federal Senate, but failed to secure a seat and was reappointed to his vacant post in state parliament a few months later. By the time he lost his seat at the 2023 New South Wales state election, he had served in the parliament for more than 40 years, making him the longest-serving member of the New South Wales Parliament at that point. He had been considering retiring from politics but his new wife convinced him to stand again, and he was elected for his final eight-year term in 2015.

His wife Elaine followed him into the party and also served in the Legislative Council from 1988 to 2002, when she retired due to ill health. After his wife Elaine's death from cancer in 2011, Nile remarried in 2013, to Silvana Nero, 24 years his junior. While studying, Nile met and married Elaine Crealy in 1958, and the pair had three sons and a daughter. "Revd Nile is survived by his wife, children, grandchildren and great-grandchildren," his family said in a statement.

Nile was known for his deep Christian values and his staunch opposition to drug use, gay marriage, abortion and Sydney's Gay and Lesbian Mardi Gras, becoming a feature of the celebrations by praying for rain each year at the start of the parade. He campaigned against same-sex marriage and adoption by same-sex couples, voted against the 2010 bill that opened adoption to same-sex couples, and spoke against same-sex marriage in a parliamentary debate in 2012. He was a standard-bearer for Christian and social conservative politics since long before the formation of the Australian Christian Lobby and other faith-based political movements, and the party he led for decades frequently took positions against drug use, abortion and homosexuality.

Yet his record also contains a less expected chapter. Nile supported a 2014 bill to extinguish certain historical convictions for homosexual offences, and it was that work his critics remembered in their tributes. Independent NSW MP Alex Greenwich, who is gay, paid tribute to Nile and called for a state service to be held in his honour. Greenwich said that while they often disagreed, they did so in an "adult way." "(He) played the ball, not the person," he said. "His opposition to many of the reforms I delivered came from his faith and he didn't engage in the personalised vitriol that comes from many of today's right-wing politicians." Greenwich added that Nile played a key role in passing legislation for convictions for homosexuality to be expunged, "landmark laws that cleared the names of hundreds of gay men."

NSW Opposition Leader Kellie Sloane said the reverend was "a man of deep Christian faith and strong convictions" who was never afraid to stand up for what he believed in. "While people held different views on the issues he championed, there can be no doubting his commitment to his beliefs and his extraordinary contribution to parliamentary life in NSW," she said. Sydney MP Alex Greenwich has since called for a state funeral for Nile in honour of his service to New South Wales.

His family said details of a service or memorial would be shared later. Nile's death comes three years after he left the parliament he had served since 1981, and it closes a chapter in New South Wales politics that began when a Christian minister decided that faith belonged in the chamber as well as the pulpit. Whether remembered as a conscience of the parliament or as an obstacle to reform, he was, for more than four decades, impossible to ignore — and the tributes from his fiercest opponents suggest that even those who fought him respected the way he fought.

Why it matters: Fred Nile's death closes the longest parliamentary career in New South Wales history, and the tributes say as much about Australian politics as about the man. That his warmest praise came from Alex Greenwich, the gay independent MP whose reforms Nile opposed for decades, speaks to a style of politics that has grown rare: fierce disagreement without personal hatred. Nile spent forty years arguing that faith should shape public life, and his opponents spent forty years arguing the opposite — yet both sides now agree he played the ball, not the person. His passing marks the end of an era in which a single backbencher with deep convictions could hold the balance of power in a state parliament for a generation.
