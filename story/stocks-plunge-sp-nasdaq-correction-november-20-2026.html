Stocks Plunge as AI Sell-Off Pushes S&P 500 and Dow Into Correction

The S&P 500 fell 3.7 percent and the Nasdaq plunged 5.3 percent on Friday, wiping out the year's gains and putting both the S&P and the Dow in correction territory after their worst week since April 2025.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 21, 2026 · 6 min readFacebookX

U.S. stocks plunged on Friday as an AI sell-off gained momentum, sending the Nasdaq Composite into negative territory for the year and wiping out nearly all the 2026 gains for the other two major indexes. The S&P 500 declined 3.7 percent, losing ground for a seventh straight session, while the Dow Jones Industrial Average fell 1.9 percent, or more than 900 points. The Nasdaq Composite plunged 5.2 percent, or more than 1,200 points, the second-largest one-day points drop in history for the tech-heavy index, and both the S&P 500 and the Dow moved into correction territory, each down more than 10 percent from their recent highs.

The closing numbers were stark. The S&P 500 lost 265.44 points to close at 6,910.39, its seventh consecutive daily decline and its longest losing streak since February 2020. The Nasdaq Composite tumbled 1,261.47 points, or 5.28 percent, to 22,617.73, erasing its gains for the year. The Dow Jones Industrial Average fell 949.73 points, or 1.93 percent, to 48,147.33. The Russell 2000 dropped 2.76 percent to 2,581.79. The S&P 500 closed 11.6 percent below its October 6 record, and the Dow ended 11.4 percent below its August 5 record. The Nasdaq is now down nearly 3 percent for 2026, while the other two major indexes are up less than 1 percent.

The week was worse still. The stock market posted its worst weekly performance since the first week of April 2025, when President Donald Trump’s announcement of reciprocal tariffs sent the market into a tailspin. The Dow finished the week down 8 percent, while the S&P 500 declined 8.6 percent and the Nasdaq tumbled 10 percent. Just six weeks ago, the S&P 500 and Nasdaq were trading at record-high levels. The Cboe Volatility Index, often referred to as Wall Street’s fear gauge, jumped 26 percent on Friday to near 36, its highest mark since last year’s tariff turmoil.

Chip stocks, which have been hit particularly hard in recent weeks, tumbled again on Friday. Shares of AI bellwether Nvidia fell 14 percent, leading Dow decliners, and have now lost one-third of their value in the past two weeks as investors assess the impact of Jensen Huang’s departure from the chief executive post. Shares of Advanced Micro Devices dropped 13 percent, while Broadcom, Marvell Technology and Micron each posted double-digit declines. The iShares Semiconductor ETF lost 11 percent and the Roundhill Memory ETF fell 13 percent. Among memory makers, SanDisk fell 16 percent, while Seagate and Western Digital both dropped 12 percent. Shares of Nvidia-backed companies Coherent and Nebius plunged 13 percent and 16 percent respectively.

The selling spread well beyond chips. Shares of SpaceX, which carried out its own massive IPO in June, fell 8 percent. Among other mega-cap stocks, Meta Platforms and Tesla declined 6 percent and 8 percent respectively, while Amazon, Apple and Microsoft were also down sharply. Not even the blockbuster success this week of Anthropic’s IPO — the biggest on record, which immediately catapulted the AI lab into the ranks of the world’s largest companies by market capitalisation — was enough to revive a market battered by fears that an AI bubble could burst. Anthropic shares fell nearly 9 percent on Friday to around $89. The Dow was the least scathed of the major indexes, helped by a 1.5 percent rise in Walmart and a 3.0 percent gain in UnitedHealth after a TD Cowen upgrade.

The fear gauge was not the only warning light. In other markets, bitcoin was at $59,000 on Friday, trading at its lowest level since June, as investors steer clear of risky assets. Gold futures fell 4 percent to $3,540 an ounce, their lowest point since September 2025, while WTI oil futures rose 1.1 percent to $84.10 per barrel. The yield on the 10-year Treasury, which affects interest rates on all sorts of loans, was at 4.94 percent, down from Thursday’s close of 5.05 percent. The yield had surged earlier in the month but has now fallen for four straight days as investors seek safety in government bonds.

What drove the rout is a collision of doubts. Investors are questioning whether the enormous spending on AI will ever pay off, whether valuations stretched too far during the boom, and whether the Federal Reserve will raise interest rates again in December — minutes from the Fed’s October meeting, released on Wednesday, showed the decision to hold rates was a “close call” and that nearly all officials expect a hike next month. Technology shares fell 7.3 percent on Friday alone. Lower bond yields would normally support equities, particularly growth companies, but Friday’s losses suggest concerns about AI earnings, positioning and market liquidity outweighed relief from falling borrowing costs.

Next week brings a short trading week in America: markets are closed on Thursday for Thanksgiving and shut early on Friday. U.S. inflation figures and Black Friday spending data arrive next week, and the Fed decides on December 9. Corrections happen fairly regularly — the question is whether this one settles down or deepens. For now, the market that spent the autumn at record highs has given back its year in a week, and the AI trade that carried it there is the trade investors are abandoning fastest.

Why it matters: A correction is defined as a fall of more than 10 percent from a peak, and as of Friday both the S&P 500 and the Dow meet it — the first time the broad market has been there since the tariff turmoil of April 2025. What makes this sell-off different is its cause: not a recession scare or a policy shock, but a loss of faith in the AI trade itself, the same trade that drove the market to records six weeks ago. When Nvidia can lose a third of its value in two weeks and the year’s biggest IPO can slide 9 percent the day after listing, the market is repricing the whole AI story at once. The next test is not another earnings report but Black Friday spending and the Fed’s December decision — and whether investors believe the AI boom was a bubble, or merely a boom that got ahead of itself.
