America Marks Veterans Day With Arlington Observance and Fifth Avenue Parade

The 73rd annual National Veterans Day Observance at Arlington National Cemetery anchors a day of ceremonies across the country, with astronaut Suni Williams leading New York's parade.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 11, 2026 · 4 min readFacebookX

The United States marks Veterans Day on Wednesday, November 11, honoring all who have served in the armed forces in war and peacetime. The day's national focal point is at Arlington National Cemetery in Virginia, where the 73rd annual National Veterans Day Observance takes place in the Memorial Amphitheater, and New York City's Veterans Day Parade returns to Fifth Avenue in the afternoon.

The Arlington program begins with a musical prelude at 10:30 a.m. Eastern time by the U.S. Coast Guard Band and the U.S. Air Force Singing Sergeants. A wreath-laying at the Tomb of the Unknown Soldier follows, honoring the day's significance at one of the nation's most symbolic sites, before the national observance convenes in the amphitheater at 11 a.m. with a parade of colors and remarks from dignitaries.

The ceremony is free and open to the public, with amphitheater seating on a first-come, first-served basis, according to Arlington National Cemetery. This year, attendance at the wreath-laying at the Tomb of the Unknown Soldier is limited to official participants, a change from past years when the public could watch the ceremony up close.

The 11 a.m. start time is itself part of the tradition. Veterans Day traces its roots to Armistice Day, marking the end of fighting in World War I at the 11th hour of the 11th day of the 11th month of 1918. It became a legal holiday on May 13, 1938, and was later expanded to honor all American veterans, not only those who served in the First World War.

In New York City, the Veterans Day Parade returns to Fifth Avenue, running north from East 26th Street to East 48th Street from about 12:30 p.m. to 3:30 p.m. The United War Veterans Council named NASA astronaut and retired Navy Capt. Sunita 'Suni' Williams as grand marshal for the 2026 march, and organizers say the parade is rain or shine, with free public viewing anywhere along the route.

Williams is a fitting choice for the honor. A retired Navy captain who flew combat helicopters before joining NASA, she has spent more than 600 days in space across her career and became one of the most recognizable faces of American spaceflight. Her selection links the parade's military roots to the newer generations of service members whose careers have taken them from the cockpit to orbit.

Beyond Arlington and New York, the Department of Veterans Affairs and the Veterans Day National Committee selected 48 regional sites in 24 states for official Veterans Day observances in 2026, from small-town memorial parks to city halls. Communities across the country are holding their own parades, ceremonies, luncheons and flag raisings, many of them at 11 a.m. local time in keeping with the Armistice tradition.

The White House issued a presidential proclamation ahead of the holiday, and Veterans Day is a federal holiday, with government offices closed and many schools and businesses shuttered. For veterans' organizations, the day is also a moment to press for attention to the issues facing those who served, from health care and housing to the transition back to civilian life.

This year's observance comes at a charged moment for the military community. The country is winding down a war with Iran that has dominated the political debate for months, the House voted on Tuesday on whether to impeach the defense secretary over that war, and the Pentagon has told Congress the conflict has cost 47.1 billion dollars so far. The ceremonies today are officially nonpartisan, but the backdrop is impossible to ignore for the veterans in attendance.

For the families gathered at Arlington and along Fifth Avenue, the day is simpler than the politics around it. Veterans Day honors the living, unlike Memorial Day, which honors the fallen, and its message is one of gratitude to the roughly 18 million veterans in the United States. The wreath at the Tomb of the Unknown Soldier, the parade of colors and the march up Fifth Avenue all say the same thing: the country remembers who served.
