Appeals Court Blocks Trump's $73.5 Million Highway Funding Cut to New York

The Second Circuit ruled the Transportation Department overstepped its authority by punishing New York for licenses issued under rules that never required expiration dates to match drivers' immigration documents.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 14, 2026 · 5 min readFacebookX

A federal appeals court on Friday invalidated the Trump administration's April decision to withhold $73.5 million in highway funding from New York over the state's refusal to revoke some commercial driver licenses issued to foreign drivers. The U.S. Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit ruled that the Transportation Department's decision went beyond its legal authority, in a case brought by New York Attorney General Letitia James.

The court said the department's decision "runs afoul of fair notice principles, departs from the regulatory text, and underestimates the complexity of its supposedly 'common sense' requirement." The ruling, in State of New York v. U.S. Department of Transportation, 2d Cir., No. 26-1097, sets aside a penalty that threatened road repairs, bridge maintenance and highway safety projects across the state.

The Transportation Department had withheld the money after an audit by the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration found New York had been routinely issuing commercial driver licenses to foreign drivers illegally. The audit, conducted in 2025, found that more than half of 200 sampled licenses issued by the state to nondomiciled individuals carried expiration dates running as much as eight years past the period for which the license holders could demonstrate they were lawfully present in the United States.

New York argued that the federal government never established an expiration rule for nonresident commercial licenses, and that the administration's decision was arbitrary, capricious and contrary to law. The state's lawyers told the court the regulations in force during the relevant period required applicants to present an unexpired employment authorization document or an unexpired foreign passport with an approved I-94 form showing lawful presence, but said nothing about matching the license's expiration date to those documents.

Judge Alison J. Nathan, writing for the court, agreed. The agency "itself concedes, the plain text of the federal regulations in place during the relevant period did not contain an expiration-matching requirement. The regulatory structure and history confirm that conclusion," she wrote. She rejected the agency's argument that the court should focus on the intent of the provision rather than its actual text, noting that the plain text of the regulation gives states freedom to issue licenses to nonresidents for up to eight years.

Nathan added that the regulatory history confirmed the meaning of the text. The safety administration, back in 2008, considered and declined to adopt an expiration-matching requirement for the regulation at issue. The administration has since issued a final rule, which took effect in March, that bars certain immigrants with work authorization from receiving commercial driver's licenses and requires a license to expire within one year of issuance or at the same time as the "admit until" date on the nonresident's I-94 form, whichever is sooner. Nathan said that amendment strengthened the court's conclusion that an expiration-matching rule was never a matter of common sense. That final rule is itself being challenged in the D.C. Circuit Court of Appeals by the American Federation of State, County and Municipal Employees and the American Federation of Teachers, with oral arguments held in September.

Judge Sarah A. L. Merriam signed on to Nathan's opinion. Judge Matthew A. Schwartz wrote a separate opinion agreeing with the result but chiding the majority for commenting "gratuitously on regulatory structure and history in ways that could affect other cases." "I do not believe the majority's approach is appropriate and so concur only in the judgment," Schwartz said.

The Transportation Department, which houses the safety administration, said in an emailed statement that it "doesn't make sense for any state to 'give unvetted, unqualified foreign drivers trucking licenses that stretch years beyond their lawful status.'" The agency said the administration "will continue to use every resource at our disposal to stop illegal immigrants who can't properly operate a truck from terrorizing America's roads." James's office did not immediately respond to a request for comment, and spokespeople for Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy and New York Governor Kathy Hochul did not immediately comment.

The stakes went beyond the $73.5 million at issue in the ruling. New York said previously that losing the funding would put New Yorkers at risk, while the department also threatened to withhold an additional $147 million a year. The state said revoking the licenses "would disrupt key industries that rely on commercial drivers and could lead to bus driver shortages affecting schools and families."

The case is the latest in a series of lawsuits in which New York, California and other states have accused the administration of withholding transportation funds for political reasons. A court earlier this year required the Transportation Department to continue making payments for the $16 billion Hudson Tunnel rail project connecting New York and New Jersey, and another court blocked the department's efforts to stop New York City's congestion pricing program. In March, a judge ordered the department to unfreeze about $3 billion in funding for rail projects in Chicago after it canceled at least $9.5 million in reimbursements since October from grants previously approved under former President Joe Biden.

Why it matters: The ruling is the latest courtroom defeat for the administration's practice of using federal funding as leverage over states that resist its policies. The Second Circuit's reasoning matters beyond New York: it holds that an agency cannot punish a state for violating a requirement its own regulations never stated, a fair-notice principle that could shape the other funding fights now working through the courts, even as the administration's stricter new licensing rule takes effect.
