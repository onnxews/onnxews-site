Eight Dead, 19 Hurt as Brooklyn Building Collapses Into Construction Pit

A three-story building in Flatbush fell into an excavation pit next door on Wednesday afternoon. As of Thursday morning, eight people were confirmed dead and 19 injured, and rescuers warned the toll could still rise.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 12, 2026 · 5 min readFacebookX

Eight people were confirmed dead by Thursday morning after a three-story building in Brooklyn collapsed into an open construction site next door, and rescue crews were still digging through the rubble for anyone trapped beneath it. The building at 1321 Flatbush Avenue, in the Flatbush neighborhood, came down shortly after 4 p.m. on Wednesday, November 11, falling into an excavation pit at 2625 Bedford Avenue where work was under way. Nineteen other people were injured, officials said.

The dead included six people whose bodies were pulled from the rubble and two more who died after being taken to a hospital, according to the Fire Department. At least 19 others were wounded, four of them critically, and five more had serious but non-life-threatening injuries. Seven children were among the wounded, and at least three firefighters were also hurt. None of the dead had been publicly identified by officials as of Thursday morning.

One victim was identified by his family. Jose Luis Caguana, a 26-year-old construction worker, was named by his brother Carlos, who said Caguana had a young child and a wife who was heavily pregnant with their second child. 'Her husband has passed away; he was the sole provider for the family,' Carlos said. 'Now that he's gone, she is going through an immense amount of pain.'

The building, constructed in 1930, was a three-story walk-up with four residential units and a commercial space at street level. It fell into the adjacent lot, where excavation work was under way. A neighboring building was also damaged, leaving part of its interior exposed. The cause of the collapse remained under investigation on Thursday, and officials had not determined whether the digging next door contributed to it.

The first emergency call came in shortly after 4 p.m., and a nearby fire company returning from another emergency arrived in less than a minute, fire officials said. More than 250 firefighters and emergency medical personnel responded, with 69 units sent to the scene. Crews worked through the night and into Thursday, using search dogs, drones, buckets and vacuum trucks to pick through the wreckage.

Fire officials described the search as slow and dangerous. The collapse left unstable wreckage and voids beneath the debris, and crews warned that portions of the adjoining structure could still come down. 'It's a very unstable situation,' Fire Chief John Esposito said. 'The adjoining building that remains partially intact is always a danger, where the main concern is a secondary collapse.' Esposito said the search was 'very reminiscent' of the searches after the World Trade Center attacks, with crews crawling in and around the collapse looking for void spaces.

Fire Commissioner Lillian Bonsignore said crews did not know how many people might still be in the collapse zone. 'We do not know how many people are in the collapse zone, and this will be a prolonged operation,' she said. Esposito said the operation could last at least three days, and crews were expected to remain at the scene until the entire collapse zone had been searched.

Mayor Zohran Mamdani said rescue workers had 'spent the night searching the site of yesterday's building collapse, working under extremely dangerous conditions to find and save anyone who may still be trapped.' He added: 'There are no words for a loss of this magnitude. My heart is with the families and loved ones of those we have lost, those who are injured and everyone grieving today.' Governor Kathy Hochul said New York would provide its full support to the investigation.

The city's Department of Buildings has opened a full investigation into the collapse with its law enforcement partners. 'We have immediately launched a full investigation into how exactly this happened,' Buildings Commissioner Ahmed Tigani said, 'and we will be looking into all aspects of the building and the neighboring construction site.' Council member Farah N. Louis, who represents Flatbush, said what happened was 'completely unacceptable and raises serious questions that our community deserves clear answers to.' The city activated its Unified Victim Identification System on Wednesday night, and a reception center for affected residents opened at 1401 Flatbush Avenue. Anyone unable to contact someone who may have been affected can call 311.

Why it matters: The Flatbush collapse is a reminder that construction-site safety in a dense city is a life-or-death matter, not a paperwork exercise: a building constructed in 1930 came down beside an active excavation, and the questions now facing investigators go to the heart of how closely the city watches digging next to occupied homes. The toll is already the deadliest building collapse in New York in years, and with the search expected to run for days, the final count may still rise. What investigators find about the excavation, the permits and the inspections will shape not only accountability for this disaster but how the city oversees the next construction pit dug beside people's homes.
