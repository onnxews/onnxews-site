Apple Launches Health AI Study of Up to 200,000 iPhone Users With OpenEvidence

Apple has opened the Apple Health AI Usage Study to up to 200,000 iPhone users in the United States, working with clinical AI company OpenEvidence to examine how people trust, understand and act on AI health answers.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 16, 2026 · 5 min readFacebookX

Apple has launched a large research study to learn how people actually use, understand and trust AI answers about their health, recruiting up to 200,000 iPhone users in the United States. The company announced the Apple Health AI Usage Study on November 15, 2026, describing it as a virtual research study with a dedicated app designed to advance understanding of how people interact with conversational health AI tools. The study is being conducted in collaboration with OpenEvidence, a clinical AI platform widely used by healthcare professionals, and it aims to inform the responsible design of health AI experiences for Apple and the wider industry. Apple

Participants use a dedicated Apple Health AI Study App, separate from the Apple Health and Research apps, to ask everyday health questions and explore complex medical information written in approachable language. Answers come with links to peer-reviewed clinical citations that ground each response, and participants can rate the AI's answers and say what was useful or lacking. The study will examine what drives user trust, the quality of responses, comprehension and everyday health actions, and it will also explore how health AI can help participants prepare for visits with their own doctors, including tools for appointment preparation and surveys afterwards.

The answers participants receive do not come from Apple's own model. They are generated using OpenEvidence technology, which draws on medical information from established sources such as The New England Journal of Medicine, the Journal of the American Medical Association and Nature, as well as Cochrane and the National Comprehensive Cancer Network. Apple said it selected OpenEvidence as a partner for its high-quality medical sources, robust privacy controls and advanced search models. The idea is to test the kind of tool doctors already use, placed in patients' hands, rather than a general-purpose chatbot with no clinical grounding.

Enrollment is open to up to 200,000 eligible participants aged 21 and older across the United States, and participants may remain in the study for up to 12 months. To join, participants must have an Apple Intelligence-enabled iPhone, an iPhone 15 Pro or later, running iOS 26 or later, and they can download the study app to review the research information and enroll. The study is open only in the United States, and Apple has announced no expansion to other countries.

Sharing personal health data is optional, but it is a central part of what the study wants to learn. Participants can choose to share HealthKit data types such as resting heart rate, activity, sleep or lab results, and if they have imported clinical records into the Health app and separately agree to share them, the app can incorporate that information, primarily lab results. Apple wants to explore how personal data integration affects the relevance of answers and how well people understand them, a question that matters because a generic answer and a personalised one can feel very different even when both cite the same research.

Privacy protections are built into the study's design. Apple said study data will not be used to train AI models, and participants retain control over whether to share any HealthKit metrics or survey responses. Queries are first processed on the iPhone to remove identifying information, then flow to a secure study environment managed by a third-party study support contractor, where they are de-linked from individual participant IDs and screened again before being sent to OpenEvidence. The study is overseen by an independent review board, participants complete a mandatory onboarding process that includes informed consent, and the app includes safety guardrails intended to detect certain concerning questions and suggest following up with a provider.

Apple is careful to say what the study is not. The app is not meant to diagnose or treat any condition, or to replace the advice of medical professionals, and participants should consult a doctor to address health concerns or verify medical information. The company also acknowledges that consumer health technologies powered by large language models may at times offer responses that are incomplete or unclear, which is why research is critical to presenting health information to users with greater clarity.

The study arrives as health chatbots are already widely used. Roughly one in three adults in the United States have turned to an AI chatbot for health information, according to a KFF survey cited in coverage of the launch, with use even more prevalent among younger adults. Apple has run health research before, launching its Research app in 2019 with three medical studies on mobility, cardiovascular health, hearing and women's health, and starting the longitudinal Apple Health Study last year. Sumbul Desai, Apple's vice president of Health and Fitness, said conducting a dedicated research study is the most thoughtful way to understand how people engage with, comprehend and evaluate health AI in everyday life.

Why it matters: This study matters because it is the first time a company with Apple's reach is testing health AI at population scale before shipping it as a product, measuring trust and comprehension rather than only technical accuracy. Roughly one in three American adults already ask chatbots about their health, often with tools never designed for the job, so the gap between what an AI gets right and what a patient safely understands is a live public health question. The findings could shape how health answers are presented, cited and personalised across the industry, and whether a clinically grounded chatbot ever reaches the Health app or Siri. With participants enrolled for up to 12 months, the earliest full results are a year away, so the study is a long bet that rigorous evidence should come before a health AI product, not after it.
