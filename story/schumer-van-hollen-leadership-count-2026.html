Schumer Allies Say He Has the Votes as Van Hollen's Leadership Challenge Tests Senate Democrats

The first contested Senate Democratic leadership race in more than three decades will be decided by secret ballot on December 1, and both camps are counting — in public, at least — while most of the caucus stays quiet.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 16, 2026 · 5 min readFacebookX

Senator Chuck Schumer's allies say he has the votes to survive the first contested Senate Democratic leadership race in more than three decades, even as Senator Chris Van Hollen's challenge forces the caucus to choose between the leader who delivered its midterm majority and a progressive who says voters demanded change. The secret ballot is set for December 1.

Van Hollen, a 67-year-old Maryland Democrat, made his run official on November 12 in an interview on MS NOW, days after saying he was “not looking” to challenge Schumer. Van Hollen wrote on social media after his announcement that the election had called on Democrats to unrig an economy rigged against working people, and that delivering on that demand required new leadership.

Schumer, 75, who has led Senate Democrats since 2017, has said from the start that he has the support to stay. “I'm proud of what we've accomplished together — 55 seats and counting is an amazing result — and I have the votes to continue serving as Democratic Leader,” he said in a statement after Van Hollen's announcement. His allies have gone further: they told The Hill they expect him to clear more than 40 votes in the caucus contest, a figure that would settle the race comfortably.

The arithmetic favours the incumbent, but only just. Whoever wins will need a majority of the 55- to 56-member caucus — roughly 28 votes, possibly 29 depending on the outcome of Alaska's still-uncalled Senate race. Van Hollen has said he has the support of more than 10 of the 55 Democratic senators, and he has acknowledged his position plainly. “Am I the underdog? Yes,” he told MS NOW host Jen Psaki on Thursday night. “You're talking about an incumbent who uses the power of incumbency to, you know, provide deals to people.”

The public commitments so far tell the story of a race that is mostly being fought in private. As of Friday afternoon, Van Hollen could list only four public backers: Senators Bernie Sanders and Jeff Merkley, plus Senators-elect Abdul El-Sayed of Michigan and Peggy Flanagan. Sanders, an independent who caucuses with Democrats, said the midterms showed the party needed to move in a new and bold direction, and added that while he has known Schumer for three decades, the time had come for change.

The most important undecided voices are the ones that have stopped short of endorsing Van Hollen while refusing to back Schumer. Senator Elissa Slotkin of Michigan, who days earlier would not rule out challenging Schumer herself, said she was not voting for him, telling reporters she had yet to figure out what the Democrats' plan was in her two years in the Senate, adding that a leader had to be able to lay out where the party was going. Senator-elect Adam Hamilton of Kansas said in a November 8 interview that he would not support Schumer, calling him a polarizing figure in his state, though he has not formally backed Van Hollen either.

Schumer's camp, by contrast, has been collecting public endorsements from the start. Senators Kirsten Gillibrand of New York and Ruben Gallego of Arizona voiced their support at Schumer's November 10 press conference, where Gallego said that when a manager wins a World Series, you give them another contract. Senator Angus King, the Maine independent who caucuses with Democrats, wrote on November 12 that he stands with Schumer, asking, “Why would you fire the manager when you just won the World Series?” Senator Catherine Cortez Masto of Nevada said Schumer was instrumental in Democrats flipping eight Senate seats, soon to be nine, and securing a historic majority.

Senator John Fetterman of Pennsylvania was blunter still, telling USA TODAY on November 12 that it was bizarre for anyone to challenge Schumer, and adding that Democrats should not be arguing among themselves now that they are about to hold the majority. Retiring Minnesota Senator Tina Smith has backed Van Hollen, though she cannot vote in the leadership election.

The contest is about more than personalities. Van Hollen has framed it as a choice about whether the party will govern differently with its new majority, arguing that the caucus “needs to be open to new ideas” and “a different way of governing.” His allies have cast the bid as a push to change how the Senate works, including on the filibuster, while Schumer's supporters describe it as a distraction from the work of the majority. Senate Democrats have not held a contested leadership election since 1994, when Tom Daschle of South Dakota defeated Chris Dodd of Connecticut by a single vote.

Van Hollen has also shelved a 2028 presidential run to make the bid. Last week he told Fox 5 DC that he was “kicking the tires” on a White House campaign, but on Thursday he said he now believes the caucus can make the biggest difference by responding to the opportunity the voters have given it in the Senate. The vote on December 1 will show whether the caucus agrees — and how many of its members were willing to say so in public first.

Why it matters: This is a sequential update on the leadership fight Orbit covered on November 13, when Van Hollen announced his challenge: the new development is the whip count taking shape over the weekend, with Schumer's allies claiming more than 40 votes while Van Hollen's public support sits at four names. It matters because the winner will lead a 55- or 56-seat majority through the last two years of Trump's presidency and into the 2028 cycle, and because the race is the first real test of whether the midterm landslide changed the party or merely changed its numbers. The secret ballot on December 1 is the next hard date, and most senators are still declining to say how they will vote.
