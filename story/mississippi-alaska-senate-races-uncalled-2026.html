Mississippi and Alaska Senate Races Still Uncalled as Tuesday Deadline Hits

Tuesday is the mail-ballot deadline in Mississippi, where Scott Colom leads Cindy Hyde-Smith by about 2,500 votes, while Alaska's count waits for its November 18 ranked-choice tabulation.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 10, 2026 · 4 min readFacebookX

A week after Election Day, two U.S. Senate races remain uncalled, and Tuesday is a deadline day in one of them: in Mississippi, mail ballots postmarked by Election Day must arrive by 5 p.m., and voters who cast affidavit ballots without photo identification have until the end of the day to show ID and save their votes.

In Mississippi, Democrat Scott Colom, a district attorney, leads Republican Sen. Cindy Hyde-Smith by about 2,500 votes, 49.1 percent to 48.8 percent, with 99 percent of the expected vote counted, according to USA Today's tally as of November 9. The Associated Press has not called the race, and neither candidate has conceded or claimed victory.

Thousands of ballots are still in play. Secretary of State Michael Watson said on Sunday night that 75 of the state's 82 counties have posted updated unofficial results, that more than 2,800 mail ballots are still out, and that 12,767 affidavit ballots have been processed, with about 77 percent accepted. Republicans estimate the outstanding pile is far larger: state GOP chairman Mike Hurst has said about 17,000 affidavit ballots and more than 3,000 mail-in ballots remain, roughly 20,000 in all, and he called the affidavit total 'very abnormal' in the state's history.

Affidavit ballots are Mississippi's version of provisional ballots, cast when a voter's name is not on the poll book or they lack an acceptable photo ID. County election commissioners review each one before it counts. Watson has not confirmed the Republicans' estimate of how many remain in total, and the Colom campaign has estimated 10,000 to 15,000 are still uncounted.

Mississippi law makes the fight over those ballots especially consequential, because the state has no recount provision. The contest will be decided by which ballots the counties accept, not by a second count. Counties must certify their results by Friday, November 13, and the Hyde-Smith campaign has already written to counties asking them to preserve ballots and records that could be needed for an election contest or a proceeding before the U.S. Senate.

In Alaska, the picture is clearer but the race is no closer to being called. Former Democratic Rep. Mary Peltola leads incumbent Republican Sen. Dan Sullivan by 2,517 votes in the latest official count from the Alaska Division of Elections, 106,321 votes to 103,804, or 48.47 percent to 47.33 percent, with all 401 precincts reporting. A second candidate named Dan Sullivan, retired schoolteacher Daniel J. Sullivan Jr., running with no party affiliation, has 3,651 votes, or 1.66 percent.

Alaska's result will not be settled until November 18 at the earliest. State law accepts absentee ballots from overseas voters for up to 15 days after Election Day, and the state uses ranked-choice voting: if neither Peltola nor Sullivan tops 50 percent once every ballot is counted, the ranked-choice tabulation scheduled for November 18 will redistribute votes from lower-placed candidates. The state has until November 25 to certify a winner.

The Alaska count has already survived a legal challenge. Judge Sharon L. Gleason dismissed the Justice Department's civil lawsuit over the state's handling of ballots on November 1, declining to order ballots segregated or certification delayed, and the department has appealed to the Ninth Circuit. A separate criminal investigation into the election continues.

The two races are the last loose ends of a midterm that has already remade Congress. Democrats hold 54 Senate seats with these two still to be decided, and the final tally will determine whether their majority is 54, 55 or 56. In Mississippi the answer starts to take shape on Tuesday, when the mail-ballot window closes; in Alaska, voters will wait another eight days for the ranked-choice count that finally decides it.
