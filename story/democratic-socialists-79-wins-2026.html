Democratic Socialists Won 79 Races on November 3, From City Halls to Statehouses

Candidates endorsed by the Democratic Socialists of America won 79 of the 164 races they contested on November 3, including the mayor's offices in Los Angeles and Washington, D.C., and eight seats in the U.S. House.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 12, 2026 · 5 min readFacebookX

Candidates endorsed by the Democratic Socialists of America won 79 elections on November 3, according to a tally published by Ballotpedia on Wednesday, November 11, in the clearest measure yet of how far the democratic socialist movement advanced in the midterm elections. The national DSA and its local chapters endorsed 164 candidates in 32 states and the District of Columbia: one for the U.S. Senate, 10 for the U.S. House of Representatives, 64 for state legislatures, and 89 for local offices. As of November 10, 79 of those candidates had won and 24 had lost, with results for the remaining 61 yet to be called.

The headline wins came in the country's biggest cities. In Los Angeles, DSA-endorsed Nithya Raman defeated incumbent Mayor Karen Bass, and in Washington, D.C., DSA-endorsed Janeese Lewis George won the open mayor's office. Raman, a Los Angeles city councilwoman and DSA member, defeated Bass, a more moderate Democrat who had served as mayor since 2022, in the first successful challenge to a sitting Los Angeles mayor in more than two decades. Once she takes office, the mayors of New York City, Los Angeles, and Washington, D.C., will all be members of the Democratic Socialists of America, and Seattle's mayor also identifies as a democratic socialist.

The movement's gains extended well beyond city hall. Eight DSA-endorsed candidates won U.S. House elections on November 3: two incumbents and six new members. Once the new members take office, that raises the number of DSA-endorsed members of Congress from two to eight. All six new DSA-endorsed members won in congressional districts that Democrats represented before the election, and three of them defeated Democratic incumbents in primaries. No DSA-endorsed candidate won a Republican-held congressional district.

In state legislatures, the results were even stronger. Of the 64 DSA-endorsed state legislative candidates, 51 won and 11 lost, with two races not yet called. Thirteen DSA-endorsed candidates won in New York, more than any other state, including 11 in the New York Assembly and two in the New York Senate. Twenty of the 21 DSA-endorsed state legislative incumbents won re-election. Five DSA-endorsed candidates defeated Republican incumbents, and 26 won open seats previously held by Democrats, 11 of them after defeating Democratic incumbents in primaries.

The local results filled out the picture. DSA-endorsed candidates won two of the three mayoral races in large cities where the group had endorsed, losing only in Louisville, where incumbent Mayor Craig Greenberg defeated DSA-endorsed Shameka Parrish-Wright. In Providence, DSA member David Morales, a Rhode Island state lawmaker and former professional wrestler, was elected mayor after campaigning to limit rent increases, turn vacant properties into affordable housing, and require corporations to contribute more toward city services and public schools.

The DSA's positions also prevailed on 44 of the 72 ballot measures voters decided on November 3, with five not yet called. At the statewide level, the group's side won 17 measures and lost 11, including the defeat of California Proposition 40, a one-time wealth tax the DSA supported. At the local level, the DSA's position won 27 measures and lost 12. Voters approved California Proposition 3, which renews a state income tax increase for education, and all three Virginia constitutional amendments the group supported.

The new mayors share a policy agenda built around affordability. Their priorities include building more affordable housing, expanding child care and public transportation, strengthening renter protections, and raising taxes on corporations and wealthy residents to help pay for public services, according to USA Today. Lewis George campaigned on universal child care, city-owned social housing, and stronger tenant protections, and has also called for lowering rental costs for small businesses. Raman, an urban planner by training, campaigned on building more housing, changing zoning rules, and improving public transportation.

President Donald Trump weighed in after Raman's race was called, predicting that voters would sour on democratic socialist policies. "It's going to be a disaster," Trump told reporters on November 4 in the Oval Office. "Unfortunately, usually that happens so late that you'll never be able to bring back a city, or a country or a state, when it goes too far." The warning has not slowed the movement's momentum: the DSA describes itself as "the largest socialist organization in the United States" and its endorsed candidates will now hold offices from Congress down to city councils and school boards.

What the tally shows is a movement that has learned to win inside the Democratic Party rather than outside it. Nearly all of the DSA's congressional and state legislative wins came in Democratic-held seats, often by defeating Democratic incumbents in primaries, and its mayoral wins came in deep-blue cities. The test now is governing: the new mayors take office in the coming weeks and months, and their promises on housing, child care, and rents will be measured against budgets and councils that may not share their politics. The 79 wins are a record for the organization. Whether they become a record of delivery is the next question.

Why it matters: The 79-win tally matters because it shows the midterm story was not only a Democratic wave but a leftward shift inside the Democratic Party itself. The DSA did not win by flipping Republican seats; it won by taking Democratic ones, in primaries and in open races, and by capturing the mayor's offices of the country's second-largest city and its capital. With eight DSA-endorsed members heading to Congress and democratic socialists now running four of America's largest cities, the movement has moved from the margins of the party to a governing force in its biggest strongholds. The open question is whether voters in those cities will judge the experiment by its promises on affordability or by the Trump administration's prediction of disaster.
