Nvidia Sued by Shareholders as Huang Probe Widens Ahead of Earnings

Shareholders sued Nvidia on Monday as new reports tied the Jensen Huang probe to a halted chip-smuggling investigation and H200 sales to China.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 10, 2026 · 4 min readFacebookX

Nvidia and chief executive Jensen Huang were sued on Monday in a proposed securities class action accusing them of defrauding shareholders by concealing Huang's ties to a suspected Chinese spy, according to Reuters, adding a legal threat to a board investigation that has already rattled the world's most valuable chipmaker.

The lawsuit landed on a day of cascading headlines for the company. The New York Times reported on Monday that the Commerce Department had cleared roughly two dozen Chinese companies to buy or handle Nvidia's H200 chips, the older but still powerful AI chip that President Donald Trump allowed to be sold to China in late 2025 with a 25 percent cut going to the U.S. government.

The same newspaper reported that a Commerce Department investigation into suspected Nvidia chip smuggling to China, centered on Singapore-based Megaspeed, was halted and transferred to the Justice Department after Nvidia's legal team contacted investigators. Bloomberg reported that Nvidia general counsel Tim Teter called Commerce Under Secretary Jeffrey Kessler in late summer 2025 about a Bureau of Industry and Security probe into Megaspeed, asking whether the bureau played a role in Singapore's detention of Megaspeed executive Alice Huang.

The reports tie the board's probe to the export-control question, turning a governance story into a regulatory one. Nvidia's board announced on Sunday that a committee of independent directors, working with outside counsel, will investigate reports that Huang was targeted by a foreign intelligence effort. The board said it has no reason to believe that national security or company secrets were compromised, and it expressed confidence in Huang and his leadership team. It gave no timetable for the review.

The underlying reports center on Alice Huang, a former executive of Megaspeed, a Singapore-based company that reportedly bought more than $4.8 billion of Nvidia chips over two years. The New York Times reported on October 29 that U.S. authorities had concluded she was a Chinese intelligence asset. She is unrelated to Jensen Huang despite the shared surname. China's Foreign Ministry rejected the espionage claims on Monday, calling them smears, and Reuters said it could not independently verify them.

Huang himself has acknowledged he was unfaithful to his wife, apologizing to his family, colleagues and shareholders in a message to employees on Sunday and saying he would cooperate fully with the inquiry. The affair is something Huang has admitted; the espionage allegation is a report the board is investigating, and the board's own statement says it has no reason to believe harm occurred.

The market has sold first and asked questions later. Nvidia shares closed on Monday at $178.67, down about 4.3 percent from Friday's $186.64 close, after opening at $173.04 and hitting a low near $170.24. The stock fell as much as 8 percent during the session and more than 6 percent in Frankfurt trading, putting about $270 billion of market value at risk. The shares had already given back all of their 2026 gains before the probe was announced, and they are now roughly 22 percent below their record.

Congress is getting involved. Three chairs of U.S. House committees have asked for a closed-door briefing with Huang, proposing November 18 and asking him to accept that date or offer another by November 11. The request adds to the case in Congress for tighter China chip controls in the same week lawmakers want answers from Huang directly.

The next test comes on November 17, when Nvidia reports fiscal third-quarter results. The last reported quarter, released August 26, showed revenue of $96.2 billion, up 106 percent from a year earlier, with data-center sales up 117 percent to $89 billion, and the company guided third-quarter revenue to $108 billion, plus or minus 2 percent. Analysts will ask about the probe as much as about data-center demand, and if orders and margins hold, sentiment could snap back quickly. If they do not, the stock that carried the AI rally may have further to fall.
