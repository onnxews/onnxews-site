Spain Heads to Snap Election With Far Right Poised to Enter Government for First Time Since Franco

Spain votes on November 29 in a snap election that could end eight years of socialist rule and bring the far-right Vox party into national government for the first time since the return to democracy. Polls put the People's Party first, but only a deal with Vox gives it a majority.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 16, 2026 · 5 min readFacebookX

Spaniards head to the polls on November 29 for the country's sixth general election in 11 years, and the vote could end eight years of socialist-led rule and bring the far right into government for the first time since Spain's return to democracy after the death of General Franco. Polls put the conservative People's Party first but short of a majority, meaning it would need the support of the far-right Vox party to govern — a deal that would reshape Spanish politics and send a signal across Europe.

Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez, Spain's socialist prime minister, called the snap election on October 5 after the conservative People's Party, the far-right Vox party and the rightwing, pro-independence Catalan party Junts voted down emergency measures the government had put together to tackle a housing crisis. His minority coalition government has not passed a budget since 2023, and his administration has faced corruption allegations and fierce criticism over its handling of the Ceuta crisis, where about 80,000 people crossed from Morocco in 48 hours.

The polls have barely moved since the vote was called. According to the polls, the People's Party could win about 130 seats in the 350-seat Congress, the Socialists about 110 and Vox about 63. Barring a far better than expected performance from the Socialists, the People's Party is set to win and would need Vox's support to govern. Spain's Congress has 350 seats, meaning any party or coalition needs at least 176 seats to secure an outright majority.

The latest survey sharpens the picture. A SocioMétrica poll for El Español published on November 15 put the People's Party at 31.6 percent of the vote and 130 deputies, the Socialists at 27.8 percent and 110, and Vox at 19.1 percent and 67 seats. The result maintains an absolute majority for the right: the People's Party and Vox would add up to 197 deputies, 21 above the 176 needed, and 199 with the two deputies of the Navarrese People's Union. Vox would more than double its representation from the 33 seats it won in July 2023, when it took 12.4 percent of the vote.

Housing is the issue that has defined the campaign. The lack of affordable housing has sparked large protests across Spain, and more than 3 million people took part in a general strike last Wednesday over soaring prices — the country's first general strike in 14 years. Sánchez has billed the election as a stark choice between his progressive government and a Spanish right that would abandon hard-won social advances. "You, the men and women of Spain, must decide whether you want to join the far-right wave that rolls back progress wherever it governs, or to ensure that Spain remains a bastion of openness, social justice and environmental responsibility," he said when announcing the snap vote.

The campaign has also been overshadowed by a high-speed rail crash on October 28 that killed 69 people, nine months after 46 died in another rail crash on the same line. Transport Minister Óscar Puente bowed to pressure and resigned days after the latest crash, saying: "Neither the country, nor I, can bear 100 deaths in less than a year." The People's Party leader, Alberto Núñez Feijóo, accused the government of failing to keep Spaniards safe, while Vox attacked the government's "incompetence, corruption, cronyism and a lack of investment in maintenance and infrastructure improvements".

What Vox would demand in government is the most contested question of the campaign. Vox leader Santiago Abascal has said his party stands for "putting nationals first, deregulation, tax relief, and the return of those who should not be in Spain." One of its main policies is "national priority", giving Spaniards precedence over others for subsidised housing, social rentals, grants and benefits. In July last year, Vox floated the idea of deporting up to 8 million people of foreign origin, arguing that "it's very difficult for them to get used to our customs". It later backtracked, saying "remigration" would apply only to those who enter illegally, commit crimes, or "come to Spain to live off welfare benefits".

Feijóo has tried to keep the door open to a deal while setting limits. He told El País: "National priority is based on rootedness in Spain, not nationality, and on respect for the constitution and European treaties. Nothing can be agreed that falls outside the constitution or those treaties." Six years ago the People's Party repudiated Vox as practising politics based on "fear, anger, resentment and revenge", but it has since adopted a friendlier tone, and the two parties already jointly govern four Spanish regions, setting a precedent for deals.

Feijóo has promised "a return to decency" as a basic principle if his party wins, while Sánchez's opponents seek to frame the election as a referendum on what they call the self-serving politics of "sanchismo". The SocioMétrica survey shows the right's majority holding even as the gap between the two main parties narrows: the People's Party lost six seats from the previous survey and the Socialists gained two, cutting the distance between them from 28 seats to 20. After November 29, the new parliament must confirm a prime minister, so the size of the right bloc's majority — and whether the People's Party and Vox can agree on terms — will decide how quickly Spain gets its next government.

Why it matters: Spain has been here before in one sense — a snap election called by a prime minister betting that a campaign will save him — but the stakes are different this time because the likely winner cannot govern alone. If the polls are right, the far right will hold the balance of power in Madrid for the first time since the dictatorship ended, and the price of its support will be policies on immigration and housing that test both the Spanish constitution and European law. The vote on November 29 will settle who governs; the negotiations after it will settle what kind of government Spain gets, and Europe will be watching both.
