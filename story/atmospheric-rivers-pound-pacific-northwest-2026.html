Back-to-Back Atmospheric Rivers Bring Heavy Rain and Monster Surf to the Pacific Northwest

Two more rivers in the sky will soak Oregon and Washington this weekend after two earlier storms dumped inches of rain, with 20-foot swells and king tides raising the flood risk.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 14, 2026 · 6 min readFacebookX

Two atmospheric rivers, bringing heavy rain, will pound parts of Oregon and Washington state back-to-back on Friday and Saturday, November 13 and 14. Two other atmospheric rivers already dropped multiple inches of rain on both states earlier this week, and forecasters say this year's atmospheric river season, which runs from October to March, is off to a fast start. The storms are hitting as heavy rain, high tides and a rising sea level batter the West Coast, with more storms on the way.

The rain totals are already substantial. Parts of Washington's Outer Coast recorded over 8 inches of rain between Tuesday and Thursday, and portions of the Northwest coast could see another 2 to 3 inches of rain through Monday. Meanwhile, the heaviest rain will begin to shift south on Friday, with parts of Northern California expected to pick up 3 to 5 inches of rain through Monday. Parts of the northern Sierra could see up to 4 feet of snow by Monday, while Washington and Oregon will see another round of intense rain and wind.

The greatest threat may be along the coastline, where massive ocean swells are expected to generate destructive surf and life-threatening sneaker waves. This weekend, Washington's coast could be hit by significant 20-foot swells, according to the National Weather Service in Seattle, and conditions along the Oregon and Washington coasts have been hazardous all week, with 15- to 20-foot westerly swells dominating the waters. Along the coast, massive 20- to 25-foot waves and offshore seas exceeding 30 feet are expected to slam the shoreline, and large waves will pound much of the West Coast this weekend, including Southern California, with destructive surf and coastal flooding possible in some areas around high tide.

High Surf Advisories are now in place along the Washington, Oregon and California coasts. The city of Santa Cruz announced it will close all beaches and the Santa Cruz Wharf through the weekend because of the dangerous high surf. "Destructive waves may wash over beaches, jetties, and other structures unexpectedly," the National Weather Service said. "Extreme caution should be used if you plan to visit beaches this week." The service's Los Angeles office warned that large breaking waves can cause injury, wash people off beaches and rocks, and capsize small boats near shore, and that large wave runup may dislodge logs that have historically killed or seriously injured unsuspecting beachgoers.

Strong winds are adding to the danger. Wind gusts could reach 60 to 80 mph along the coast and 40 mph across the Interstate 5 corridor, and strong gusts could take down power lines, particularly where the ground is already saturated. High Wind Warnings have been issued across coastal Oregon and northern California through Saturday. In Oregon, two people and a dog came dangerously close to being swallowed by sneaker waves at Winchester Bay, in a reminder of how quickly the surf can turn deadly. Colder air is expected to arrive late Sunday into Monday, dropping snow levels to around 3,500 to 4,000 feet and bringing another round of snow to the Cascade and Sierra passes.

An atmospheric river is a narrow band of water vapor pushed by the wind. Loaded with moisture, it dumps rain or snow, and these rivers can be up to 2,000 miles long and 600 miles wide, said F. Martin Ralph, the director of the Center for Western Weather and Water Extremes at UC San Diego's Scripps Institution of Oceanography. About 50 to 60 percent of rain in California comes from atmospheric rivers, and people may know them by nicknames such as Pineapple Express or Rum Runner Express, which describe where the moisture originates. A Pineapple Express that came across the Pacific from Hawaii in late October caused devastating flooding and multiple deaths in the Southwest's Four Corners region.

"What's really problematic is when you get compounding impacts from multiple atmospheric rivers," said Josh Weiss, a meteorologist with the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration's Weather Prediction Center. Those impacts include flash flooding, urban flooding and even landslides, he said. The rivers are hitting as El Nino's warm ocean waters contribute to rising sea levels and fuel storm systems, and right now these natural weather patterns are coinciding with annual high tides, known as king tides, which leaves coastal and inland waterways along the West Coast at risk for flooding. According to 40 years of data from the Federal Emergency Management Agency, 90 percent or more of all flood damage in the Western United States is from atmospheric rivers.

The relentless pattern shows no signs of letting up. Three more storms are expected to blast the region over the next two weeks, including potentially threatening Thanksgiving plans. A powerful storm will sweep across the Pacific Northwest and Northern California beginning Friday and last through the weekend, due to the relentless atmospheric river pattern. Winter Storm Warnings are in effect across the eastern slopes of the Sierra, and even lower elevations there could pick up anywhere between 8 inches and two feet of snow through Monday.

For residents, the advice from forecasters is consistent: stay far away from the water's edge, expect power outages where saturated ground meets high winds, and check forecasts before traveling. The combination of high astronomical tides and persistent onshore wave energy is also expected to produce minor tidal overflow and significant beach erosion along the coast. "We've got to keep our eyes" out for atmospheric rivers, Ralph said, "whether it's an El Niño year or a La Niña year or a No Niño year."

Why it matters: Four atmospheric rivers in a single week is the kind of compounding event forecasters warn about: each storm on its own is manageable, but back-to-back systems saturate the ground, swell the rivers and leave nowhere for the next round of rain to go. With three more storms expected before Thanksgiving and king tides amplifying the surf, the Pacific Northwest is facing a long, wet stretch in which the main risks are not the rain itself but the flooding, landslides and sneaker waves that come with it — and the pattern is a reminder that the region's flood risk is driven less by any single storm than by how many arrive in a row.
