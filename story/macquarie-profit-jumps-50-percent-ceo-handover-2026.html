Macquarie's Profit Jumps 50% as Outgoing CEO Bows Out on a High

Australia's biggest investment bank posted a 50 percent jump in half-year profit to A$2.48 billion, powered by commodities trading — but a A$100 million regulatory capital overlay and a sliding share price took the shine off the farewell result.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 8, 2026 · 3 min readFacebookX

Macquarie Group, Australia's biggest investment bank, has delivered a record half-year result to mark the final day of Shemara Wikramanayake's eight years as chief executive. Net profit jumped 50 percent to 2.478 billion Australian dollars for the six months to September 30, beating the average analyst forecast of 2.32 billion dollars and closing the book on a tenure defined by the bank's transformation into a global markets powerhouse.

The result was powered less by a uniformly stronger franchise than by a particularly forceful showing from the bank's Commodities and Global Markets division, whose net profit contribution rose 83 percent to 2.034 billion dollars. Commodity prices swung sharply after the United States and Israel attacked Iran in February, and the resulting volatility drove strong earnings from client hedging across global gas and power markets, inventory management and oil trading.

The rest of the bank was more mixed. Net operating income rose 10 percent to 9.568 billion dollars, and the Banking and Financial Services division grew its home loan book to about 200 billion dollars, up 24 percent from a year earlier, lifting its net profit contribution 6 percent to 841 million dollars. But Macquarie Capital, the deal-making unit, saw its profit contribution fall 61 percent, a reminder that the bank's earnings now come from trading rather than transactions.

Shareholders will be rewarded for the strong half. The board declared an interim dividend of 3.25 dollars per share, up from 2.80 dollars a year earlier, representing a 50 percent payout ratio. The record date for the dividend is November 12 and the payment date is December 17. Earnings per share rose 49 percent to 6.52 dollars.

The market's reaction was less celebratory. Macquarie shares fell as much as 5.5 percent to 243.70 dollars on Friday, weighed down by a second announcement on the same day: Australia's banking regulator said the bank had miscalculated some non-bank capital requirements in 2019, and imposed a new 100 million dollar governance overlay on top of an existing 500 million dollar overlay added in 2021.

The result also came with a reminder that trading windfalls are uneven. Profit was 22 percent below the previous six-month period, showing how lumpy the bank's earnings can be when they depend on market volatility rather than steady client activity. The next result will test not simply whether profit remains high, but how much of it can be reproduced through ordinary business.

Wikramanayake's departure closes a significant chapter. Her retirement was announced in July, and she hands over to Greg Ward, who assumes the managing director and chief executive roles on November 7. Ward, for his part, has said that volatility in AI and tech stocks can be healthy, a view that will be tested quickly given the market turmoil of the past week.

Macquarie itself retained a cautious outlook, pointing to interest rates, geopolitical volatility, foreign exchange, transaction timing and regulatory change as factors that could swing future results. The bank enters the Ward era with strong capital, growing deposits and a larger dividend, but the durability of its trading income matters more than the headline profit growth.

For global investors, the result is a barometer of where the money is being made in 2026: not in deal-making, which has slowed, but in commodities trading, where war, tariffs and volatile energy markets have created exactly the conditions in which Macquarie's trading engine thrives. Whether that engine can keep running at this pace is the question that will define Ward's first year in charge.
