Trump Says He Wants a 'Permanent' Deal to Keep Iran From a Nuclear Weapon as Talks Stall

In a Saturday phone interview, the president said any agreement with Tehran must bind future administrations — while Iran insists there will be no talks until the U.S. blockade ends and its frozen assets are released.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 22, 2026 · 5 min readFacebookX

President Donald Trump said on Saturday that he is seeking a permanent agreement with Iran that would guarantee Tehran never acquires a nuclear weapon — one that no future president could undo. The remarks, made in a phone interview with Fox News, came as negotiations remain stalled over Tehran's demands on sanctions, frozen assets and the Strait of Hormuz.

'If we have an incompetent president in four years, or 15 years, or 20 years, I'm going to make a deal that is going to be permanent. I'm not going to let somebody allow them to have a nuclear weapon,' Trump told Fox News anchor Kayleigh McEnany on 'Saturday in America.' He said he was 'not rushing' the negotiations and left open the possibility of further military action if diplomacy fails.

The comments followed a White House press conference on Friday, held after Trump's return from China, in which he said the Iranian regime wants a deal 'but not a deal that's acceptable to me.' 'We want to make a real deal, not a fake deal,' he said in the West Wing, with Vice President JD Vance and Secretary of State Marco Rubio standing behind him. 'They've been bullshitting people for 51 years.'

Trump also defended Vance, who drew attention after the midterms by writing on Nov. 4 that 'the reason we lost is the war,' referring to the conflict with Iran that began on Feb. 28. Asked about the post on Friday, Trump said he thought Vance's statement was 'fine' and 'accurate,' adding that he would have added one thing if asked whether he would order the strikes again: 'absolutely the answer is yes, because we cannot allow Iran to have a nuclear weapon.' Vance, for his part, said the goal of keeping Iran from a nuclear weapon 'is absolutely what we need to be doing.'

Tehran's position, set out by Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi on Saturday, is that Iran has no plans for talks with the United States until its conditions are met. Those conditions include an end to the war on all fronts, the lifting of the economic blockade and the release of frozen Iranian assets, along with recognition of a new shipping route through the Strait of Hormuz worked out with Oman. Araghchi confirmed that messages continue to pass through mediators, but maintained that keeping enriched uranium inside Iran remains a 'red line.'

Mediation is still moving. A Qatari delegation arrived in Tehran on Friday evening for about two hours of consultations, according to Iranian Deputy Foreign Minister Kazem Gharibabadi, who said Iran's 'main condition is an end to the war on all fronts.' U.S. special envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner met Qatar's prime minister in Berlin on Nov. 19 about the mediation, according to Axios. Araghchi said regional negotiations should be 'given a chance.'

The military option has not gone away. CBS News reported on Friday that the United States and Israel are considering launching a military offensive against Iran in the near future, citing sources familiar with the discussions. According to the report, Jerusalem is increasingly concerned about Tehran's efforts to rebuild its ballistic missile production capabilities and move military and nuclear infrastructure underground. Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu reportedly favors a joint operation with Washington, although Trump has yet to decide whether to authorize renewed strikes.

Trump told Israel's Channel 12 on Thursday that he was 'not rushing into any decision,' adding, 'We will see what happens after the beginning of the year.' The report follows meetings in Washington last week between Mossad Director Roman Gofman and senior U.S. officials, including CIA Director John Ratcliffe. U.S. Central Command chief Adm. Brad Cooper also met with senior Israeli defense officials on Wednesday for what the Israeli military described as a joint assessment of the security situation.

The standoff is playing out on the water as well. U.S. Central Command on Saturday marked 130 consecutive days of its forces' blockade of Iran, saying American service members were preventing Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps from exporting billions of dollars in crude oil. The command previously estimated the blockade had prevented more than $25 billion in Iranian crude exports. On Saturday, British maritime agency UKMTO reported that two tankers transiting the Strait of Hormuz came under attack in separate incidents, one struck in its engine room and the second on its stern, each by an unknown projectile. A third tanker was struck the previous day, starting a fire on board. No injuries were reported in any of the three incidents.

Iran, meanwhile, claimed on Saturday that it had shot down a U.S. drone over Qeshm Island in the strait, and separately claimed its air defenses had damaged an American F-16 over the Persian Gulf. Central Command rejected the F-16 claim outright, stating: 'Iran did not attack any F-16 yesterday. All American fighter jets in the Middle East are safe.' Mohsen Rezaei, secretary of Iran's Supreme National Security Council, threatened retaliatory attacks against Israeli infrastructure if Iranian facilities were targeted. And in a joint statement on Thursday, the United States, Britain, France and Germany called on Iran to restore international inspections of its nuclear facilities, warning that the whereabouts of its highly enriched uranium stockpile remain unverified after a year and a half without full monitoring.

Why it matters: The gap between the two sides is now explicit and structural: Trump wants a deal that outlives his presidency and permanently bars Iran from a weapon, while Tehran wants the blockade lifted and its money back before it will even sit down — and will not give up its enriched uranium. With the U.S. and Israel openly weighing renewed strikes, Qatar mediating, and tankers still being hit in the Strait of Hormuz, the diplomacy and the military pressure are running on parallel tracks. The next signal that matters is whether the Berlin mediation channel produces a meeting, or whether the new year brings the offensive Trump says he has not yet decided on.
