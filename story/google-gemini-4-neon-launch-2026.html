Google Launches Gemini 4 Neon, Its Fourth Gemini 4 Model in Seven Weeks

The new model sits between Google's heaviest and fastest AI systems, with a one-million-token context window and pricing aimed at developers building agentic software.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 19, 2026 · 4 min readFacebookX

Google launched Gemini 4 Neon on Tuesday, November 18, the fourth model in its Gemini 4 family in seven weeks. The pace is striking: Argon arrived on September 30, Carbon on October 28, Hydrogen on November 12, and now Neon, a cadence that shows how quickly Google is iterating on its flagship AI systems.

Neon sits between Carbon and Hydrogen in the lineup. Carbon is the heaviest and most capable model in the family, while Hydrogen is the fast, everyday model. Neon was adapted from Carbon, and Google says it keeps much of Carbon's capability while running more cheaply, which makes it a middle option for developers who need more power than Hydrogen but do not want to pay Carbon's price.

The model comes with a one-million-token context window, which means it can take in very long documents, codebases, or conversations in a single request. It also has four thinking levels, so developers can choose how much reasoning the model does for a task. At its default high setting, Google says Neon costs 32% less per task than Carbon.

Pricing is where Neon makes its case. The model costs $3 per million input tokens and $15 per million output tokens, with cached input priced at $0.15 per million. Hydrogen costs $1.50 and $7.50 for the same volumes, while Carbon and Argon cost $2 and $10 through November 30 as an introductory offer, rising to $4 and $20 from December 1. From December 1, Neon will be 25% cheaper than Carbon and Argon.

On benchmarks, Neon looks strong but not quite at the top. It scored 61 on Artificial Analysis's Intelligence Index at its highest setting, placing third behind Claude Fable 5.2 at 65 and GPT-6.2 Astra at 62. It did better in coding, where it ranked first on the Coding Agent Index with 58.6, and it scored 65.2% on Terminal-Bench 4.1, a test of real-world software engineering work.

Google says Neon has the lowest rate of making things up of any Gemini model yet, based on its own internal tests, and that it writes more secure code than Carbon. The company is also planning a version of Neon for cyber defence, to be released through its Fairwind Program, which gives security researchers early access to its models.

The model is available now through the paid Gemini API in AI Studio and Android Studio, through Gemini for business, and through Antigravity, Google's coding platform. Subscribers to Google AI Pro and Ultra can use it in the Gemini app as well. The launch post was signed by Koray Kavukcuoglu, a senior vice president at Google DeepMind.

There are caveats. Artificial Analysis, which runs the benchmarks, noted that at its highest thinking setting Neon is notably slow and very verbose, taking an average of 21.5 minutes per task against a median of 12.4 minutes, and waiting 56 seconds before its first word against a median of 13 seconds. The full safety report for Neon is also still coming, and Google says it did not run the full set of critical-capability evaluations for this model.

Neon also has a knowledge cutoff of March 2026, which means it has no built-in knowledge of events after that date. For developers, the choice is now about trade-offs: Carbon for the hardest problems, Hydrogen for speed and cost, and Neon for the middle ground, where a task needs real reasoning but does not justify the most expensive model.

Why it matters: Google is releasing flagship AI models at a pace that would have been unthinkable a year ago, and Neon is the clearest sign yet that the race is now about price and positioning as much as raw capability. By carving out a middle tier between its heaviest and fastest models, Google is betting that developers want choice, not just power. The benchmark results show Neon is genuinely competitive in coding, where it leads the field, but the slowness at its highest setting is a real trade-off. The bigger question is whether the market needs four flagship models in seven weeks, or whether this is a company shipping fast to stay ahead of rivals who are doing the same.
