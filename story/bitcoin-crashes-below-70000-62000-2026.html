Bitcoin Crashes Below $70,000 and Sinks Toward $62,000 in Brutal Weekend Sell-Off

The cryptocurrency fell below $70,000 on Friday and hit a low of $62,081 on Sunday, down about 23 percent from its November 4 high, as ETF outflows and forced selling piled up.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 15, 2026 · 5 min readFacebookX

Bitcoin fell below $70,000 on Friday and sank toward $62,000 over the weekend, capping a brutal sell-off that has wiped out roughly a quarter of the cryptocurrency's value in less than two weeks. The world's largest cryptocurrency fell below the $70,000 mark on Friday, November 13, and dropped to a low of $62,081 on Sunday, November 15, before recovering toward $65,400. The slide left Bitcoin down about 23 percent from its roughly $81,000 high on November 4, and it has now fallen for most of the past ten days as investors pulled money from crypto funds and leveraged traders were forced out of their positions.

The selling has been relentless in the exchange-traded fund market, where U.S. spot Bitcoin ETFs recorded $1.91 billion in outflows over seven consecutive sessions from November 5 to 13, according to data from Farside Investors. Friday alone brought $185.4 million in outflows, including $143.5 million from BlackRock's iShares Bitcoin Trust and $41.9 million from Fidelity's Wise Origin Bitcoin Fund. BlackRock's fund has now seen more than $2 billion in outflows over nine sessions through Wednesday, and the week of November 9 to 13 brought $1.065 billion in net withdrawals.

Leveraged traders have borne the worst of the rout. Around $1.96 billion in crypto positions were liquidated over the past 24 hours, according to data from CoinGlass, with Bitcoin accounting for roughly $750 million of that total, including about $600 million in long positions wiped out as prices fell through key levels. The Kobeissi Letter, a market commentary service, noted that Bitcoin had fallen about $5,000 in a day and about $11,000 over three days, and that digital assets as a whole have shed roughly $800 billion in value since October 6. The Crypto Fear & Greed Index stood at 20 on Friday, its lowest reading in more than four months and deep in "extreme fear" territory, a level last seen on July 8.

The trigger for the latest leg down was a hotter-than-expected inflation report that changed the outlook for interest rates. Consumer prices rose 0.4 percent in October, with core inflation up 0.3 percent, and futures markets are now pricing in 64 basis points of Federal Reserve interest rate hikes over the next year, up from 37 basis points at the start of November, according to Coinbase Institutional. Higher rates are bad news for risk assets, because they raise the return on safe cash and make speculative holdings harder to justify. "Investors should avoid chasing short-term dips or using excessive leverage," said Vikram Subburaj, CEO of the crypto exchange Giottus, warning that volatility was likely to remain elevated.

Bitcoin's slide has been part of a broader retreat from risk that began with a sharp sell-off in technology stocks on November 5, when the Nasdaq fell 5.5 percent and semiconductor shares dropped 12 percent. Research firm 10x Research noted that Bitcoin fell 5.9 percent between November 5 and 12 while the Nasdaq 100 gained 6.4 percent over the same stretch, a divergence that undercut the argument that crypto was simply tracking stocks. Gold fell 5.5 percent over the same period. The tech sell-off intensified on November 12, when Nvidia chief executive Jensen Huang resigned and the chipmaker's shares fell 12 percent at the open, dragging Bitcoin below $72,000.

Corporate holders have added to the pressure. MARA Holdings, one of the largest publicly traded Bitcoin miners, disclosed that it sold 13,597 Bitcoin for roughly $1.1 billion after reporting third-quarter results, using $750 million of the proceeds to repay Bitcoin-backed loans. Strategy, the software company that has become the largest corporate holder of Bitcoin, was confirmed by index provider MSCI on November 11 to be removed from its major equity indexes after the close on November 30, a decision that could force index-tracking funds to sell the stock. The supply of major stablecoins, including Tether, USD Coin and DAI, has contracted by $11.5 billion over the past 30 days, the largest recorded decline, suggesting that cash is leaving the crypto ecosystem rather than waiting on the sidelines.

Market structure has also deteriorated. Bitcoin's order-book depth, a measure of how much buying and selling interest sits near the current price, fell to $72 million on November 9, about 33 percent below normal levels, according to trading platform Talos, meaning that even modest sell orders can move the price sharply. The thin liquidity helps explain why the decline has been so violent: with fewer buyers standing ready, each wave of selling pushes the price further before finding support. Talos senior research associate Tanay Ved warned that "liquidity remains thin while leverage stays elevated, leaving the market vulnerable to sharp moves in either direction."

For now, the market's mood is one of fear rather than capitulation. But the combination of sticky inflation, rising rate expectations, heavy ETF redemptions and thinning liquidity has broken the momentum that carried the cryptocurrency to $81,000 less than two weeks ago. Whether the $62,081 low marks a bottom or merely a pause in a deeper correction will depend on whether the selling in ETFs eases and whether the Federal Reserve's December decision gives risk assets a reason to rally again.

Why it matters: This matters because the sell-off is no longer a single bad day but a structural unwind. ETF outflows of $1.91 billion in seven sessions, nearly $2 billion in liquidations in a day, and the largest stablecoin contraction on record all point to money leaving crypto rather than rotating within it, and the trigger was a shift in the interest-rate outlook that hits every risk asset at once. The 23 percent drop from the November 4 high has broken the momentum that defined Bitcoin's year, and the thin order books mean the next move could be just as violent. What matters next is whether the ETF selling eases and whether the Fed's December meeting gives risk assets a reason to stop falling.
