Passenger Charged With Attempted Murder After Somali Uber Driver Is Strangled and Run Over in Minnesota

Mohamed Yusuf, 51, is in critical condition with broken ribs, a broken leg, spinal injuries and collapsed lungs. Prosecutors say they cannot prove the attack was motivated by bias.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 18, 2026 · 4 min readFacebookX

A passenger has been charged with attempted murder in a weekend attack on a Somali American Uber driver near Minneapolis that prosecutors described as an incredibly brutal assault. Samit Apurva Patel, 25, was charged on Tuesday with attempted second-degree murder and carjacking after the driver was strangled with a seatbelt and run over by his own car in the city of Hopkins.

Mohamed Yusuf, 51, is in critical condition after the attack early Saturday, according to court documents. He has broken ribs, a broken leg, spinal injuries, collapsed lungs and significant internal bleeding, and his prognosis is poor, the criminal complaint says. His family said he was in a coma.

Yusuf was on the phone with his brother, who is also an Uber driver, when Yusuf picked up the passenger just before 1 a.m. Saturday. Yusuf's brother says he heard the passenger tell Yusuf he didn't like the name Mohamed, the court document says. He later heard Yusuf screaming in Somali that he was being choked with a belt and run over by the car, according to the filing.

Officers found Yusuf in a patch of burs in a ditch off the side of the road, using location data from his smartwatch and a drone with thermal imaging. Patel's parents had called for a wellness check after Patel arrived home appearing intoxicated and exhibiting signs of emotional distress and belligerent behavior, according to the court filing. When officers arrived, they found Yusuf's car in the driveway with the exterior and driver's seatbelt damaged, and the phone call with Yusuf's brother was still active through the car's system.

Officers said Patel told them he had been drinking and admitted he took an Uber home, but he claimed not to know where his Uber driver was, the filing says. Patel was taken into custody on Saturday. He made his first court appearance on Tuesday afternoon and is being held on $1 million bail. Online court records do not list an attorney for Patel.

Hennepin County Attorney Mary Moriarty announced the charges at a news conference on Tuesday. "It was an incredibly brutal attack," Moriarty said, saying Yusuf was strangled with his seat belt and apparently pulled out of the car and driven over, possibly more than once. Moriarty said the motive remains unknown.

Prosecutors said there was insufficient evidence to prove to a jury beyond a reasonable doubt that a specific bias was the motivation for Patel's actions, and they did not bring a bias-related enhancement. That decision has drawn anguish from Yusuf's family and from Somali community leaders, who had demanded a hate crime investigation after the attack.

Moriarty said the evidence could not prove beyond a reasonable doubt that Patel's behavior was motivated by bias, though that "does not diminish the pain that the family and the Somali community are feeling right now". She also said the environment for Somali neighbors in Minnesota is absolutely frightening, and that it is worsened by comments made by the president and others who demonize an entire community.

Uber said in a statement that it was horrified by the attack, has been in touch with the driver's family and has banned the rider from the platform. Dozens of community members have been stopping by the hospital to support Yusuf's family, and people demonstrated in support of the driver outside the Hennepin County Medical Center on Monday.

Why it matters: This case lands in a Minnesota Somali community that is already on edge, and the charging decision captures the tension at the heart of it: prosecutors call the attack incredibly brutal and have charged the most serious counts available, yet say they cannot prove bias beyond a reasonable doubt, the standard a jury would require. That gap between what a community experiences as a hate attack and what a prosecutor can prove in court is where the anger now sits. For Uber drivers, the case is also a reminder of how exposed the job is: the driver was alone, at night, with a stranger, and the only reason he was found in time was a smartwatch and a phone call that stayed connected.
