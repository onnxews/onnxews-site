IAEA Says It Cannot Locate Iran's Uninspected Isfahan Enrichment Plant or Its Uranium

The UN nuclear watchdog says it does not know where the Isfahan Fuel Enrichment Plant is, whether it holds nuclear material, or the fate of Iran's stockpile of near-weapons-grade uranium.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 14, 2026 · 6 min readFacebookX

The UN nuclear watchdog is "increasingly concerned" about a uranium-enrichment plant at Isfahan that Iran declared to it last year but has still not let the watchdog inspect, it said in a confidential report on Iran on Friday, November 13, seen by Reuters. The International Atomic Energy Agency's warning, in a confidential quarterly report circulated to member states, is the starkest admission yet of how little the agency can now see inside Iran's nuclear programme.

The plant at the center of the report is the Isfahan Fuel Enrichment Plant, known as IFEP. Iran declared the facility to the agency on June 12, 2025. The IAEA had planned to inspect the plant on June 13, 2025, but the bombing campaign started that day, making it impossible. The report said the Agency "does not know" IFEP's "exact location or whether it contains nuclear material," adding that it was "increasingly concerned" about the plant, which Iran has not permitted the Agency to inspect. The targets of the June 2025 bombing included a tunnel network at Isfahan where diplomats have said they believe IFEP is located, though a senior diplomat said that its whereabouts and status remained unknown.

Iran has not let International Atomic Energy Agency inspectors return to any of its nuclear facilities bombed by Israel and the United States in the 12-day campaign in June last year, which destroyed or badly damaged Iran's other uranium enrichment plants, the heart of its nuclear programme. The only facility inspected in Iran by the IAEA since last June is the Bushehr nuclear power plant, where inspectors conducted an inspection on September 12 to 15. The reactor currently running at Bushehr uses uranium from Russia enriched to 4.5 percent, a low level needed for power generation.

The report also reiterated that Iran should inform the IAEA of the status of its enrichment plants as well as its stock of highly enriched uranium, which includes material close to weapons grade, and let inspectors return to the bombed sites and account for the enriched uranium. According to the IAEA, Iran maintains a stockpile of 440.9 kilograms (972 pounds) of uranium enriched up to 60 percent purity, a short, technical step away from weapons-grade levels of 90 percent. IAEA chief Rafael Grossi said in March that his agency believed a significant amount of Iran's most highly enriched uranium was in the tunnel complex at Isfahan. Grossi warned in an interview last year that the stockpile could allow Iran to build as many as 10 nuclear bombs, should it decide to weaponize its programme, while adding that it does not mean Iran has such a weapon.

Iran told the UN nuclear watchdog this month that discussions on nuclear monitoring cannot proceed while it is under threat from military attacks. According to the confidential report, circulated to member states on Friday and seen by The Associated Press, Iran insisted discussions on resuming inspections in the country cannot resume amid "a series of persistent, explicit, and public threats by the President of the United States of America ... to commit further acts of armed aggression" against Iran, "particularly targeting its nuclear facilities." Iran said the situation will not resolve "until the root causes of the present situation are addressed and the essential requirements for normal safeguards implementation are restored," the report added.

Iran nevertheless reaffirmed its "firm commitment to resume full implementation of its safeguards obligations as soon as the relevant legal and actual conditions permit it." Tehran also expressed concern in a letter to the IAEA about a recent U.S. offer of a reward of up to $20 million in exchange for information on Iran's nuclear programme, stressing the importance of maintaining the confidentiality of the IAEA's activities. The IAEA encouraged Iran to "consider technical and gradual measures" to allow for a return of inspectors to Iran, and said it is "fully prepared to resume its safeguards activities in Iran at short notice."

The watchdog said the lack of information about Iran's atomic sites and nuclear material is "a matter of proliferation concern" that "needs to be rectified with the utmost urgency." Such highly enriched nuclear material should normally be verified every month, according to the IAEA's guidelines, and the agency also said it cannot conclude that no nuclear material has been diverted from peaceful activities. The gap matters because the material in question is the part of Iran's programme closest to weapons use, and because the agency's inability to verify it has now lasted more than a year.

The monitoring breakdown is a legacy of the war that began in June 2025, when Israel and the United States launched a 12-day campaign of heavy airstrikes against Iran's nuclear sites, leaving much of Iran's nuclear programme in ruins even as the full extent of the damage remains unclear. The United States and Israel launched further military strikes against Iran on February 28, with attacks that killed Iran's Supreme Leader Ayatollah Ali Khamenei and other top officials, targeting Revolutionary Guard command facilities, air defence systems, missile and drone launch sites and military airfields. Iran has long insisted its programme is peaceful, but the IAEA and Western nations say Tehran had an organised nuclear weapons program up until 2003.

What happens next is unclear. The IAEA's report goes to its member states with no inspection date set and no agreement on how monitoring resumes. Iran says the root causes must be addressed first; the agency says the lack of access must be rectified with the utmost urgency. Until one side moves, the watchdog's picture of Iran's nuclear programme will keep getting darker, and the world's uncertainty about where 440.9 kilograms of near-weapons-grade uranium sits will keep growing.

Why it matters: This report is the nuclear shadow behind the military story Orbit covered on November 13, when Iran claimed it hit a U.S. radar in Bahrain and a tanker struck a mine in the Strait of Hormuz. The fighting and the inspections failure are now feeding each other: Iran says it cannot let inspectors in while it is under threat of attack, and the longer inspectors stay out, the less anyone outside Tehran knows about a uranium stockpile that the IAEA itself says could fuel as many as 10 bombs. That uncertainty is itself a driver of the crisis, and it will not be resolved by airstrikes or by statements — only by inspectors getting back through the door.
