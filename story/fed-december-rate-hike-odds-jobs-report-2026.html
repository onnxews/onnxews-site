Strong Jobs Report Pushes December Fed Hike Odds to About 80 Percent

Employers added 165,000 jobs in October, nearly triple forecasts, and traders now expect the Fed to raise rates at its December meeting.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 7, 2026 · 4 min readFacebookX

The American economy added 165,000 jobs in October, nearly triple what economists expected, and the unemployment rate slipped to 4.1 percent. The surprisingly strong report, released by the Bureau of Labor Statistics on Friday morning, immediately pushed up the odds that the Federal Reserve will raise interest rates again in December.

Futures markets moved quickly after the release. Traders priced about a 78 to 80 percent chance of a quarter-point hike at the Fed's December 8-9 meeting, up from about 67 percent the day before, according to CME FedWatch data cited by Reuters. Some measures put the odds as high as 85 percent in the immediate aftermath of the report.

The jobs numbers beat expectations on almost every front. The Reuters consensus forecast was for about 65,000 new jobs, and the actual figure of 165,000 was more than three times the 48,000 monthly average of the previous 12 months. Revisions added another 84,000 jobs to the earlier counts, with August revised up by 53,000 to 186,000 and September revised up by 31,000 to 60,000.

Wage growth also came in hot. Average hourly earnings rose 0.4 percent in October, accelerating from a 0.1 percent increase in September, and wages were up 3.1 percent from a year earlier, rather than slowing below 3 percent as some economists had anticipated. The labor force participation rate rose to 61.9 percent.

Treasury yields rose on the data. The two-year yield, which tracks Fed expectations closely, climbed about 5 basis points to 4.52 percent after the release. The benchmark 10-year yield rose to about 4.87 percent. Higher yields reflect the market's judgment that the Fed will have to keep policy tighter for longer to cool an economy that is still running hot.

The report lands at a delicate moment for the central bank. The Fed held its target range at 3.75 to 4.00 percent on October 28, but two officials already voted to raise rates at that meeting, and most policymakers projected at least one more quarter-point hike by the end of 2026. A December move would take the range to 4.00 to 4.25 percent.

Not every signal in the report pointed the same way. The broad U6 unemployment measure, which counts underemployment as well as unemployment, rose from 7.6 percent to 7.9 percent. The number of people working part time for economic reasons rose by 339,000 to 4.84 million, a reminder that the labor market's strength is not evenly shared.

The Fed's preferred inflation gauge, the PCE price index, was up 3.6 percent from a year earlier in September, well above the central bank's 2 percent target. That combination of sticky inflation and strong hiring is exactly what pushes policymakers toward another hike, even as the stock market's technology selloff argues for caution about tightening into a falling market.

Canada offered a sharp contrast on the same day. Canadian employment fell by 4,700 jobs against expectations of a 13,600 gain, and unemployment there rose from 6.5 percent to 6.7 percent. Markets put the chance of a second Bank of Canada hike at just 35 percent, after the bank raised its rate by 25 basis points to 2.5 percent on October 28. The two countries' central banks now look headed in different directions.

Two data points stand between now and the Fed's decision. The October inflation report is due on November 10, and the November jobs report arrives on December 4, just days before the December 8-9 meeting. If inflation stays high and hiring holds up, a December hike will look close to certain. If either cracks, the Fed could still pause, and the market's 80 percent bet would unravel fast.
