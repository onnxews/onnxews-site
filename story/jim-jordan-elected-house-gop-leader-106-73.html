Jim Jordan Elected House Republican Leader, Beating Scalise 106-73

House Republicans chose the Ohio firebrand to lead them into the minority after their midterm wipeout.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 11, 2026 · 4 min readFacebookX

House Republicans elected Representative Jim Jordan of Ohio as their next leader on Tuesday, handing the combative Judiciary Committee chairman the top job in the party's diminished conference by a vote of 106 to 73 over Majority Leader Steve Scalise of Louisiana. The closed-door secret ballot, held a week after Democrats swept Republicans from power in the midterm elections, puts one of President Donald Trump's closest allies in Congress at the head of the party as it prepares to move into the minority.

Jordan, 62, will take over as minority leader when the 120th Congress convenes in January 2027, succeeding Speaker Mike Johnson, who said the day after the election that he would not seek the post. Jordan has represented Ohio in the House since 2007 and co-founded the hard-right House Freedom Caucus, the bloc that has pushed Republican leaders to the right for a decade. His election signals that the shrunken conference intends to fight the incoming Democratic majority head-on rather than rebuild quietly.

The contest was a choice between two very different styles of leadership. Scalise, who has served in senior Republican leadership since 2014 and held the number-two job since 2019, ran on his record as a fundraiser and operator, telling colleagues he had raised 67.9 million dollars for the party's midterm effort. Jordan ran as a fighter, arguing in a letter to colleagues the day after the election that Republicans needed a leader ready to take on Democratic investigations.

Johnson's decision not to run opened the race. The speaker, who had led the conference for three years, told colleagues on November 4 that he would step aside, and on Tuesday he called both candidates his brothers and declined to say how he voted. He did tell reporters he would take the oath of office in January, though he would not commit to serving a full term, a remark that underscored how unsettled the party's future remains.

Jordan wasted no time framing his mission. In the November 4 letter, he warned that each Democrat-led committee would turn its full attention to investigating every facet of the administration and private entities across the country. After the vote, he took no questions from reporters, saying only that over the next two years House Republicans will fight for American families, according to CBS News' live coverage.

Republicans also filled out the rest of their leadership team on Tuesday. Representative Tom Emmer of Minnesota was elected minority whip, the party's number-two post, beating Representative August Pfluger of Texas by 103 to 76, while Representative Lisa McClain of Michigan held off two challengers to keep her job as conference chair.

The scale of the defeat Jordan inherits is stark. Democrats had won 253 House seats to 167 for Republicans, with 15 races still uncalled, according to NBC News' count on Tuesday, a net gain of 39 seats for Democrats over the 214 they held before the election, and NBC's model projects a final margin of 259 to 176. It is the biggest House majority Democrats have held in decades, and it leaves Jordan leading a conference that is a shadow of the one that held the gavel.

Jordan's first test will come before he even takes the new job. The House voted on Tuesday evening on a Republican motion to send Representative Thomas Massie's eight articles of impeachment against Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth to the Judiciary Committee, the panel Jordan chairs, a procedural move that effectively shelved the effort for this Congress.

Across the aisle, Democrats are preparing their own transition. Representative Hakeem Jeffries, who is expected to take the speaker's gavel in January, has already flipped to a firm yes on impeaching Hegseth, and House Democrats are scheduled to hold their leadership elections on November 18 and 19. Jordan will be the Republican answering Jeffries across the aisle for the next two years.

The choice of Jordan also says something about where the Republican Party sees itself after a punishing election. Rather than turning to an institutionalist in the mold of Scalise, the conference picked its most prominent television-era brawler, a man who built his reputation on confrontational hearings and close alignment with Trump. Whether that posture wins back voters or deepens the party's losses will be the central question of the next two years, and the answer starts in January.

Correction (Nov. 11, 2026): An earlier version of this article described the Democrats' House gains as a net gain of 41 seats. Democrats won 253 seats, a net gain of 39 over the 214 they held before the election. The figure of 41 was the Associated Press's count of seats gained, before offsetting losses.
