Krakow Gunman Visited Websites About Mass Killer Breivik, Polish Minister Says

As Poland mourns the six people killed in the Main Market Square shooting, investigators say the gunman showed signs of far-right influence, and the government has ordered an urgent review of gun licensing.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 10, 2026 · 5 min readFacebookX

The gunman who killed six people in Krakow's historic Main Market Square visited websites about Norwegian mass killer Anders Behring Breivik, Poland's justice minister said on Tuesday, adding that there were signs he may have been influenced by right-wing extremism. The disclosure, made by Waldemar Zurek on Radio Krakow, deepened the political dispute over the motive for the deadliest mass shooting in Poland's modern history, even as the country continued to mourn the victims of Saturday's attack.

The suspect, a 24-year-old factory worker named Michal Oksinski, died in hospital on Monday after being shot and arrested by police minutes after they said he opened fire from a car in the square, one of Poland's busiest tourist sites. Shortly after 3:00 in the afternoon on Saturday, November 7, Oksinski, masked and dressed in military-style clothing including a bulletproof vest, drove onto the Main Market Square and began firing at random people with a semi-automatic rifle. Six people died and sixteen were wounded, two of them police officers. One of the wounded officers remains in a coma, while the other has regained consciousness.

The bodies of Oksinski's father, mother and sister were found in their family home in Ostrowiec Swietokrzyski, about 150 kilometers northeast of Krakow, on Saturday. The father was 53, the mother 52 and the sister 21, and investigators have preliminarily identified gunshot wounds as the cause of death. Prosecutors have opened a separate murder case over their deaths, and the number of murder charges in the investigation could rise from six to nine if the deaths at the home are attributed to him. It is alleged that he shot them before driving some 200 kilometers south to continue the killing in Krakow.

Polish prosecutors opened a terrorism investigation on Sunday, saying the shooting constitutes a terrorist offence because the gunman acted to intimidate many people. It is the first investigation in Poland formally filed as terrorism, and it has landed four days before the most flammable date on the country's political calendar, the November 11 Independence Day march. Prosecutor Tomasz Waszczuk confirmed that six murder charges were being prepared, with the figure able to rise to nine. The arsenal justified the severity: when Oksinski's car was searched, four firearms and hundreds of rounds of ammunition were found, and investigators recovered eleven firearms in total, seven of them seized from the family home.

The motive has become the subject of a bitter political dispute. Interior Minister Marcin Kierwinski said on Saturday, just hours after the incident, that the gunman had been influenced by far-right ideology. The national prosecutor's office now says it is too soon to say he was inspired by any ideology. Kierwinski told public radio on Tuesday that he was not taking back anything he had said, but that he was waiting to see investigators' findings. Zurek said on Tuesday that it is clear Oksinski visited websites concerning Anders Breivik, and that a silencer had been found in the car used by the gunman.

Investigators are examining Oksinski's electronic devices, including his phone, and a number of badges he had attached to his military-style outfit. The prosecutor's office says a yellow Gadsden flag with a coiled snake symbol was displayed in his bedroom. Police have also detained five people whose online posts praised the attack and could pose a threat. The gunman and his father were both members of the Swit Starachowice sports club, where there is a shooting range, and all of the firearms recovered were owned legally by Oksinski and his father. He had passed all of the psychological tests required to hold a license, a fact that has brought scrutiny to the checks over who qualifies to handle weapons.

The government announced on Monday an urgent review of gun licensing rules. The review will examine how Oksinski was able to accumulate an arsenal of eleven firearms and hundreds of rounds of ammunition despite the checks that are supposed to prevent exactly that. For a country with some of the strictest gun laws in Europe, the attack has raised difficult questions about whether the system is working as intended, and whether the psychological screening that Oksinski passed is rigorous enough to catch someone planning an attack.

On Monday night, thousands of people joined a silent march through central Krakow in memory of those killed and injured. The crowd was led by Poland's president and prime minister, bitter political rivals who put on a rare show of unity after the country's deadliest attack in modern times. As they reached the historic Market Square, a bugler played from the tower. One of the six victims has been identified as a Polish painter in her 50s, a mother of four who was in town with friends for an exhibition opening. Four of the six people killed were Polish nationals.

President Karol Nawrocki declared November 8 and 9 days of national mourning, while Krakow authorities declared mourning in the city until November 11. The suspect's death ends any charges against him, but prosecutors say the terrorism investigation will continue, and the inquiry into his motive and possible links goes on. For Poland, the coming days will bring both the Independence Day march and the funerals of the victims, and the question of what drove a 24-year-old factory worker to kill his own family and then open fire on strangers in one of Europe's most beautiful squares remains unanswered.
