GDC Survey: 40% of Game Professionals Now Use Generative AI, but 55% Say It Hurts the Industry

A survey of more than 2,600 game industry professionals finds 40 percent now use generative AI at work, up from 36 percent, while 55 percent say it is hurting the industry.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 16, 2026 · 5 min readFacebookX

Generative AI is now part of daily work for a large share of the people who make video games, even though most of them believe it is harming their industry. That is the central finding of the Game Developers Conference's 15th State of the Game Industry report, released on November 12, 2026, and based on responses from more than 2,600 game industry professionals across 69 countries. The survey found that 40 percent of respondents use generative AI tools as part of their jobs, up from 36 percent in the previous survey, while 55 percent said the technology has a negative impact on games. GamesBeat

Adoption is rising, but it is uneven across the industry. Among those who use generative AI, 57 percent use it every day, making it routine rather than occasional for its adopters. Use is highest among people working in business operations and services, at 71 percent, and among workers at mobile-only studios, at 70 percent, and those working on free-to-play games, at 58 percent. Programmers report usage of 48 percent, and older professionals are more likely to use the tools, with 54 percent of those over 45 saying they do. Indie developers and people working on premium games report the lowest rates, at 29 percent and 39 percent.

What people actually use the tools for is narrower than the hype suggests. Among adopters, nearly three-quarters turn to generative AI for research and brainstorming, and 56 percent use it for code generation and debugging. Only 22 percent use it for concept art and visual ideation, and just 7 percent use it for player-facing features, the content players actually see and hear in a finished game. The pattern is consistent: the tools are welcomed for background work, research and early prototyping, and resisted where they would touch the creative work itself.

Sentiment has barely moved even as use has grown. The 55 percent who say generative AI has a negative impact on the industry compares with 54 percent in the previous survey, while 35 percent described the impact as mixed and only 8 percent called it positive. Beth Elderkin, content marketing manager at GDC Festival of Gaming, told GamesBeat this was the first year in which the negative figure did not change, after years in which it rose each time the question was asked, suggesting that sentiment around the technology has settled now that people know enough about it to have formed a view.

The written responses show why the two numbers point in different directions. Respondents described practical value in repetitive tasks, research and early experiments, while raising concerns about employment, legal exposure, environmental costs and the erosion of skills. A new worry appeared this year: skill atrophy, the fear that over-reliance on generative tools will stop people, junior developers in particular, from developing skills of their own. About 10 percent of open responses indicated full opposition to using generative AI tools at all, but a large majority saw value in uses that happen in the background and take a long time without taking much effort.

Workplace policy is split almost as sharply as opinion. Twenty-seven percent of workers at large AAA studios said they feel encouraged or pressured to use generative AI, compared with just 9 percent at indie studios. Across all respondents, 23 percent said AI is banned at their studio and 11 percent said it is discouraged, while 17 percent are encouraged to use it, 12 percent can use it at their discretion and 1 percent are required to. Some respondents told the survey they do not like generative AI but are forced to use it at work, a reminder that the 40 percent adoption figure measures behaviour, not enthusiasm.

The survey was conducted against a harsh jobs backdrop. One in three respondents, 34 percent, said they had been laid off in the past two years, rising to 39 percent for respondents in the United States, and more than half of those laid off, 54 percent, had not found new employment. GDC itself cautioned that the survey is likely weighted toward people affected by layoffs, who may have been more willing to respond, so the figures describe the survey's respondents rather than a verified layoff rate for the whole industry. Even with that caveat, 21 percent of all respondents were unemployed, up from 13 percent in the previous report.

The report also captures where development itself is heading. About 10 percent of respondents are developing for the next PlayStation and 8 percent for Xbox's Project Helix, while PC remains the dominant development platform at 81 percent. Interest in developing for Nintendo Switch 2 has risen to 47 percent, while only 3 percent of developers reported working on a virtual, augmented or mixed reality game, down from 8 percent in the previous survey. On engines, Unreal remains the most popular at 35 percent, ahead of Unity at 28 percent, with proprietary and in-house engines climbing to 23 percent.

Why it matters: The survey matters because it separates two questions the games industry usually conflates: whether people use generative AI, and whether they think it is good for games. Use is rising, concentrated in business roles, mobile and free-to-play studios, while the creative core of development remains largely off limits and sentiment has stopped getting worse rather than improving. That split points to the fights ahead, over studio policies that pressure staff to use tools they distrust, over junior developers whose skills may never form, and over disclosure to players. The next survey will show whether the plateau in negative sentiment holds once AI use spreads further into production, or whether the 55 percent was only a pause.
