Two Senate Races Still Uncalled as Alaska and Mississippi Counts Drag On

Democrats already hold the Senate, but razor-thin leads in Alaska and Mississippi will take days or weeks to settle.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 6, 2026 · 2 min readFacebookX

Two days after the midterm elections, the last two Senate races remain too close to call, with Democrats leading both by fewer than 2,000 votes. The outcomes will not change which party controls the chamber, since Democrats have already secured at least 54 seats, but they will decide the size of the new majority.

In Mississippi, Democratic challenger Scott Colom led Republican incumbent Senator Cindy Hyde-Smith by just over 1,800 votes late Thursday, with about 96 percent of ballots counted, according to the Associated Press. Colom had 466,775 votes to Hyde-Smith's 464,962. Several thousand provisional and mail-in ballots still need to be counted, and voters who cast affidavit ballots because they lacked photo identification have until November 10 to show it. Counties must submit certified results by November 13.

In Alaska, former Representative Mary Peltola led Republican incumbent Senator Dan Sullivan by 637 votes, with roughly three-quarters of ballots counted. Peltola had 102,962 votes to Sullivan's 102,325. Alaska accepts mailed ballots for up to 10 days after Election Day, and longer for ballots posted from abroad, so the initial count is far from finished. If no candidate wins a majority, the state's ranked-choice tabulation is scheduled for November 18.

The uncertainty has drawn heavy attention from prediction markets, with nearly $37 million wagered on the Mississippi race alone, including about $32 million on Kalshi. Betting markets heavily favored Colom, but Mississippi has no automatic recount provision, so the final certified count will be decisive.

Dozens of House contests also remain uncalled as counting continues across the country. Democrats had flipped seven Senate seats and more than 30 House seats as of early Thursday, with the House majority already projected to reach levels not seen since the Obama era.

Reporting: USA Today
