Peltola's Lead Nearly Doubles in Alaska as Friday Count Puts Her at 49.84%

Mary Peltola added more than 13,000 votes in Friday's update, stretching her lead over Sen. Dan Sullivan to 10,412 votes — but she remains just short of the majority that would settle the race outright.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 14, 2026 · 4 min readFacebookX

Democrat Mary Peltola's lead over Republican Sen. Dan Sullivan nearly doubled on Friday, as Alaska released another large batch of ballots in a Senate race that remains uncalled nearly two weeks after Election Day. Peltola added 13,083 votes in the update, stretching her advantage to 10,412 votes as of Friday night, up from 5,800 after Thursday's count.

The latest unofficial report from the Alaska Division of Elections, timestamped 7:14 p.m. Friday, showed 279,334 ballots counted statewide, an increase of 22,511 from Thursday's report. Peltola has 138,240 votes to Sullivan's 127,828. Republican Gerald Heikes has 5,885 votes, Daniel J. Sullivan Jr. has 4,560, and write-ins account for another 855.

Peltola took about 58 percent of the newly counted ballots. Sullivan added 8,471 votes in the update, and her lead grew by 4,612 votes in a single day. The count is moving in one direction, and it is moving fast.

The count leaves Peltola tantalizingly close to the outright majority that would end the race without further rounds. The state's candidate dashboard shows her at 50.0 percent, but that display leaves write-in votes out of the total. The detailed summary report, which includes write-ins, puts her at 49.84 percent, and among the four named candidates her share is about 49.996 percent — still below half of all votes counted so far.

Under Alaska law, a candidate who tops 50 percent of first-choice votes when counting ends wins without ranked-choice rounds. If Peltola falls short, the race goes to ranked-choice tabulation, which under state law would not start until November 18. The Associated Press has not called the race.

The stakes reach beyond Alaska. A Peltola victory would give Democrats 56 Senate seats, their largest majority since the late 2000s, according to USA Today. The Alaska contest is one of the few major races still unresolved nearly two weeks after Election Day, after a Democratic wave flipped both chambers of Congress.

The Senate race was not the only Alaska contest to move on Friday. In the race for the state's lone House seat, Republican Rep. Nick Begich continues to lead with 132,053 votes, or 47.83 percent, but his advantage over nonpartisan Bill Hill narrowed to 16,833 votes, down from 18,781 on Thursday. Hill has 115,220 votes, or 41.73 percent, and also remains below a first-choice majority.

In the governor's race, Jonathan Kreiss-Tomkins and running mate Zac Johnson strengthened their majority, reaching 139,293 votes and 50.81 percent when write-ins are included, up from 50.05 percent on Thursday. And in the state House, the closest contest in the state tightened further: Republican Rep. Sarah Vance's lead in House District 6 dwindled to just 13 votes over Brent Johnson, down from 164 on Thursday.

The Division of Elections' report lists all 401 precincts as reporting, but not every ballot has been counted. The count will continue toward the November 18 ranked-choice tabulation, which will decide the race if no candidate finishes above 50 percent of first-choice votes.

The count has moved Peltola's way on each of the last two days: her lead was 5,800 votes after Thursday's report and 10,412 after Friday's. Whether the remaining ballots lift her over 50 percent, or leave the race to ranked-choice tabulation, will be decided by the next updates from the Division of Elections.

Why it matters: Friday's update is the second straight day the count has widened Peltola's lead: it stood at 5,800 after Thursday's update and 10,412 after Friday's. The open question is arithmetic, not momentum — whether the remaining ballots push her over 50 percent or send Alaska to its first ranked-choice Senate tabulation. Either way, the seat will decide whether Democrats hold 55 or 56 Senate seats when the new Congress convenes in January, and with it how much room the new majority has to move its agenda.
