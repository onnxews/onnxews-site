Baby Reindeer Defamation Trial Set for May 18, 2027

A federal judge in Los Angeles set the date for Fiona Harvey's lawsuit against Netflix, rejecting both sides' request for a later start.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 17, 2026 · 4 min readFacebookX

The defamation lawsuit over Baby Reindeer will go to trial on May 18, 2027, after a federal judge in Los Angeles set the date and rejected both sides' request for more time. US District Judge R. Gary Klausner set the jury trial date in the Central District of California, turning down a joint request from Fiona Harvey and Netflix to start on July 27, 2027. Harvey's lawyer said his client was delighted with the earlier date.

Harvey sued Netflix in June 2024, claiming the hit series falsely portrayed her as Martha, a twice-convicted stalker who was jailed for stalking and sexually assaulted the show's creator, Richard Gadd. Harvey says she was never convicted of stalking, and she is seeking more than $170 million in damages. Her US lawyer, Richard Roth, said: "We've just learned that the trial... is going to be on May 18, 2027... Both sides submitted a document which provided for more time, but the judge said, 'no, you're trying this in May.'" He described Harvey as "elated" and "delighted."

The case has already been through a long procedural fight. The trial was originally scheduled for May 2025, but it was paused in September 2024 when Netflix appealed Judge Klausner's refusal to dismiss the case under California's anti-SLAPP law, which protects speech on matters of public concern. The Ninth Circuit dismissed that appeal on jurisdictional grounds in October 2026, clearing the way for the case to proceed.

Harvey has since narrowed her claims. On November 3, 2026, she filed a First Amended Complaint that reduced the case from six causes of action to three and seeks more than $50 million in profits from the series, alongside the broader damages claim. Her bid for punitive damages, which Judge Klausner struck down in 2024, has been renewed in the amended filing. Discovery in the case must be completed by February, setting up a compressed timetable before the May trial.

Netflix denies that it defamed Harvey and has vowed to defend the case vigorously. The company argues that the events depicted in the series are substantially true and that Harvey has not identified a provably false statement of fact about her. Gadd, who is not a defendant in the case, said in a 2024 declaration: "Martha Scott is not Fiona Harvey." The series itself was presented as a true story, with a title card describing it as based on real events — a claim that is now at the heart of the dispute.

The stakes are high for both sides, and for the industry. Baby Reindeer premiered in April 2024 and became one of Netflix's biggest hits, drawing more than 100 million views and winning six Emmy Awards, including acting prizes for Gadd and Jessica Gunning, who played Martha. A verdict against Netflix would raise difficult questions about how streamers label true-story dramas and how much they must verify before presenting a real person's life as entertainment.

The case also tests the limits of dramatisation. Gadd has always said the series was based on his own experience of being stalked, and that he changed details to protect the identity of the person involved. Harvey's claim is that the changes were not enough — that viewers identified her, and that the series presented her as a convicted criminal when she is not. The jury will have to decide where the line falls between a creator's right to tell his story and a real person's right not to be defamed by it.

With the trial date now set, the next six months will be dominated by discovery and pre-trial motions. Both sides will be deposing witnesses, exchanging documents and arguing over what evidence the jury should hear. The trial itself, in May 2027, will be one of the most closely watched court cases in entertainment history — a reckoning, more than three years after the series premiered, over what really happened and who gets to say so.

Why it matters: This trial will matter far beyond Netflix. Baby Reindeer was sold as a true story, and its success was built on the audience's belief that what they were watching really happened. If a jury finds that the series defamed a real person, every streamer will have to rethink how it labels true-story dramas and how much verification it does before putting a real person's life on screen. The case asks a question the industry has avoided for years: when a show says 'this is a true story,' who is responsible when it is not quite true? The answer, in May 2027, will reshape how these stories are told.
