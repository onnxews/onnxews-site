Ariana Grande Brings the Focker Franchise Into 2026 as Focker In-Law Opens Wednesday

Grande joins Ben Stiller and Robert De Niro for the fourth Meet the Parents film, which opens the day before Thanksgiving, 16 years after the last one, with critics split on whether the legacy sequel earns its return.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 22, 2026 · 5 min readFacebookX

It's been a tough year for Ariana Grande. In September, she opted to take a brief step back from the public eye at the end of her global concert tour after drawing scrutiny for her shrinking frame. She also dropped out of a musical with Jonathan Bailey, prompting the entire production to fall apart. And her love life continues to draw outsize attention and criticism. Yet she's ending the year on a high note.

In Focker In-Law, Grande joins Ben Stiller and Robert De Niro for the latest film in the 26-year-old Meet the Parents franchise, and more than holds her own against the industry icons. Her casting is a masterstroke, at once refreshing the series for new fans and enlivening it with her endless on-screen charisma. The film opens the day before Thanksgiving, on Wednesday, November 25.

In Focker In-Law, 33-year-old Grande plays Olivia Jones, the new girlfriend of Henry Focker, played by Skyler Gisondo. In a twist on the first film in the series, it is now Henry's turn to bring home his romantic partner to meet his parents, played by Ben Stiller and Teri Polo, and grandparents, played by Robert De Niro and Blythe Danner. The family is gathering on Long Island to celebrate Jack's 80th birthday, but Greg takes an almost instant dislike to Olivia, believing she is manipulating his 26-year-old baby boy.

Olivia is a former FBI hostage negotiator turned corporate adviser, and her background in American intelligence agencies is part of what wins over the family. What feels particularly unfair to Greg, who had to battle over three previous films to win the begrudged respect and love of his father-in-law, is that Jack seems to be under Olivia's spell too.

Olivia is both the love interest audiences should be rooting for and the main antagonist to Greg. They should root for her, while also not fully trusting her, a tough assignment for any actor. But Grande weaponizes her diminutive stature to great effect, with a firecracker confidence that makes clear neither she nor her character should be underestimated. "She's got to play all these sides. I think she really does deep work on the arc of the character, and then you combine that with great comic timing," said John Hamburg, the film's writer and director. "All that adds up to like this complex character who I think can be romantic, be funny and be a little scary as well."

"Ari is slight of stature, but she is a powerhouse," Hamburg said. Grande threw herself into the project with ease, according to Hamburg, despite it being just her second leading feature film role. He recalled that on the first day or so of filming, she had to shoot a pivotal moment that revealed her character's potentially sociopathic side, a tough scene in which her character takes twisted glee in inventing a story about someone plummeting from a bridge. "She just went for it," Hamburg said. "And I was like, 'Oh, she's gonna make my job easy.'"

Hamburg, who co-wrote the first two films and directed the third movie, 2010's Little Fockers, said that when he began kicking around ideas for a return to the franchise, he knew he needed to do something different than just having Greg and Jack at odds with one another again. He liked the idea of Greg having aged into Jack's role. "It will be a different dynamic than we've seen before," he said of his ultimate decision to introduce Olivia, and instead of being insecure and anxious like Greg was, "she's incredibly confident and very good at many things. And most importantly, and most threatening to Greg, Jack Byrnes instantly loves her."

Not every critic is convinced. Little White Lies, reviewing the film, wrote that 16 years after Little Fockers, the new film "struggles to justify its own existence beyond Hollywood's apparent determination to scrape the bottom of the intellectual property barrel." The review found a few laughs and said Grande "manages to hold her own as a comedic co-lead, especially in scenes that center the dynamic between her, Stiller, and De Niro," but concluded that the film's overlit, unblemished lifelessness makes it near-indistinguishable from most forgettable straight-to-streaming movies.

The split verdict is familiar territory for the franchise. With this type of mid-budget, star-led comedy now mostly relegated to streaming services, the new movie feels both fresh to 2026 and a nostalgic reminder of the sort of comedy of errors that made the originals such successes. All told, the three prior films netted more than $1.1 billion at the global box office. Both Stiller and De Niro returned for this fourth movie, although Stiller's character is now older than De Niro's was in the original movie, a fact Greg marvels at with some horror.

Grande's own star power is the film's biggest asset heading into the holiday weekend. Her performance is a powerful reminder of what makes her such a consummate entertainer and comic actor, building on the comedy skills she honed via her Oscar-nominated turn in Wicked and her three stints hosting Saturday Night Live. Whether audiences come for the nostalgia or for Grande, the Fockers are back, and the Thanksgiving box office will deliver the verdict.

Why it matters: Focker In-Law is a test of two things at once: whether a 26-year-old comedy franchise can still fill theaters, and whether Ariana Grande can carry a film as a leading lady rather than a pop star making a cameo. The early split between CNN's praise and Little White Lies' dismissal is exactly the divide legacy sequels always face, and the box office will settle it. For Grande, the film caps a difficult year on a high note and could be the performance that moves her from singer-who-acts to actor in her own right.
