Producer Prices Rose 0.3% in October as Annual Inflation Hit a Four-Year High of 6.5%

The monthly rise was smaller than expected, but the year-over-year rate climbed to its highest since November 2022 — the last major inflation report before the Fed's December meeting.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 13, 2026 · 5 min readFacebookX

Wholesale prices rose 0.3 percent in October, the Bureau of Labor Statistics reported on Friday, a smaller monthly increase than economists expected — but the annual inflation rate still climbed to 6.5 percent, its highest level since November 2022. The report is the last major inflation reading before the Federal Reserve meets in December, and it complicates a decision that markets had treated as settled.

The headline producer price index, which measures the prices businesses receive for their goods and services, rose 0.3 percent in October against a consensus forecast of 0.4 percent. September's increase was revised up to 1.4 percent, from 1.3 percent originally reported. On a year-over-year basis, producer prices rose 6.5 percent, up from 6.3 percent in September and slightly below the 6.6 percent economists had expected.

The core figures tell a firmer story. Core producer prices, which exclude food and energy, rose 0.3 percent in the month against a 0.3 percent consensus, with September revised up to 0.8 percent from 0.7 percent. A broader underlying measure — excluding food, energy and trade services — rose 0.5 percent for a second consecutive month, and its annual rate stood at 4.9 percent.

The details show where the pressure is coming from. Goods prices rose 0.5 percent in October, a sharp slowdown from the 2.5 percent jump in September, while services rose 0.2 percent, down from 1.0 percent. Food prices rose 1.3 percent, but energy prices fell 0.1 percent after surging about 10 percent in September. Goods excluding food and energy rose 0.5 percent. Transportation and warehousing prices jumped 2.0 percent, driven by a 3.9 percent rise in truck freight, while trade services — the margins wholesalers and retailers earn — fell 0.2 percent.

Further up the supply chain, the pressure looks worse. Processed intermediate goods rose 0.4 percent in the month and were up 14.6 percent from a year earlier, while intermediate services rose 0.6 percent and were up 6.8 percent. Unprocessed goods fell 3.1 percent in October, led by a 10.3 percent drop in crude petroleum, which is the main reason the headline figure stayed moderate.

The timing is what makes the report matter. The Fed meets on December 8 and 9, and the producer price index is the last major inflation report it will see before deciding whether to raise interest rates. The report follows a hot consumer inflation report this week — October consumer prices rose 0.4 percent, hotter than expected — and Fed officials have opened the door to a half-point December hike. Markets now see a December hike as all but certain, with the debate over whether it will be a quarter point or a half point.

The odds tell the story of that debate. Futures pricing put the chance of a half-point move at about 12 percent and a quarter-point hike at about 90 percent, according to CME FedWatch data reported this week. The split reflects a market that believes the Fed will move in December but is not sure how aggressively — and the producer price report, with its hot annual rate and cooler monthly pace, gives both sides something to point to.

The report also lands in a week of bad inflation news. The consumer price report that preceded it was hot enough that Fed officials began publicly discussing a half-point move, a step the central bank usually reserves for moments when it believes inflation is getting away from it. The producer price data reinforces the same picture: prices are still rising at a pace well above the Fed's 2 percent target, even if the monthly pace has eased from September's surge.

For businesses, the figures have a direct meaning. Producer prices are the prices companies receive, and when they rise at 6.5 percent a year, companies either pass the costs on to consumers — which feeds consumer inflation — or absorb them, which squeezes margins. The 14.6 percent annual rise in processed intermediate goods suggests that cost pressure is building further up the supply chain, and that it has not yet fully reached consumers.

The Fed will have one more piece of data to consider before its meeting, but the producer price report is the last major inflation reading. If the central bank raises rates in December, as markets expect, it will be doing so against a backdrop of annual wholesale inflation at its highest in four years — and with the question of whether a quarter point is enough still open.

Why it matters: This is the new data release itself, distinct from Orbit's November 10 and 11 coverage of the hot consumer inflation report and Fed officials opening the door to a half-point hike. The producer price index is the last major inflation report before the Fed's December 8-9 meeting, and it matters because it shows the split that will decide the size of the hike: the monthly pace cooled, but the annual rate hit a four-year high. The 14.6 percent annual rise in processed intermediate goods is the number to watch, because it suggests cost pressure still building up the supply chain.
