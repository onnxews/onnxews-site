Five EU Countries Unveil Plans for a Rwanda Deportation Hub

Germany, Austria, Denmark, Greece and the Netherlands announced plans on Monday for a joint return hub in Rwanda for rejected asylum seekers, starting with a pilot of about 100 people in 2027. Rights groups condemned the plan, and its backers admit the point is the signal it sends.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 17, 2026 · 6 min readFacebookX

Five European countries unveiled plans on Monday to send rejected asylum seekers to a deportation hub in Rwanda, drawing condemnation from rights groups who said it would leave vulnerable people at risk of abuse. Germany, Austria, Greece, Denmark and the Netherlands announced the plan in Vienna, saying they have begun concrete negotiations with Kigali and aim for the first transfers to take place in 2027.

Under the planned scheme, which is still being negotiated with Rwanda, the five countries would send people to a center where the migrants could choose to return home, settle in Rwanda, or go somewhere else where they had a right to stay, Kigali said. The facility would be an open center where migrants would receive support and have access to training programs, employment opportunities, education and health centers, according to the project's promoters. The center, whose location has yet to be disclosed, could accommodate around 100 people at a time, about 20 from each country.

Danish Prime Minister Mette Frederiksen, a leading proponent of outsourcing migration for more than eight years, presented the plan in Vienna with Austrian Chancellor Christian Stocker and ministers from the countries involved. "I think it will be a game changer, making us able to confront the massive challenges of illegal migration," Frederiksen told a press conference. Le Monde reported that she repeated three times that the five countries want to "take back control of [their] borders."

Rwanda has positioned itself as a willing host. "Under this partnership, Rwanda would provide a second chance to people who no longer have the legal right to remain in the partner countries," Rwandan Interior Minister Vincent Biruta said on Monday. Rwanda was not looking to be paid for the arrangement, he added, but partner countries would meet the cost of relocation and the integration of anyone who chose to stay. Rwanda has committed to granting each person a residence permit and helping them integrate into the country.

Negotiations between the European capitals and Kigali are expected to wrap up in early 2027, with implementation set for later that year, according to German Interior Minister Alexander Dobrindt. The five countries intend to send adults, men or women, without dependent families. The project follows the approval in mid-October of the European Union's new Return Regulation, which sets rules for establishing return hubs in third countries and is designed to speed up deportations. Under the regulation, member states may only send people who have been denied asylum and who cannot return to their home country.

The scheme differs from Britain's abandoned Rwanda deportation plan in an important respect: it would only apply to migrants who have exhausted all legal avenues to remain in the European Union, rather than those whose asylum claims are still being processed. Britain's plan was abandoned after it was ruled unlawful by the country's Supreme Court in 2023, and Kigali sought a 50 million pound payment after the scheme was scrapped. The United States last year began transferring migrants to Rwanda under an agreement allowing it to deport up to 250 people.

Rights groups moved quickly to condemn the plan. In a joint statement, 47 aid, trade union and rights groups urged the five countries to abandon it. The plan "signals that Member States accept the risk of serious human rights violations against one of the most marginalized groups in society, in return for a deterrence goal that evidence clearly suggests they will not reach," read the statement from the International Rescue Committee, the Dutch Council for Refugees and other groups. Amnesty International said: "There is a real danger that the Rwanda deal will, with its limited chance for scrutiny, become a cruel black hole for human rights." Human Rights Watch said Rwanda does not meet the human-rights requirements of the EU's new return framework.

The scale of the pilot is tiny next to the problem it is meant to address. Almost 500,000 return decisions were made in 2025 by EU countries to third countries, and only 27 percent were carried out, according to Eurostat figures cited by public law professor Serge Slama. That leaves more than 350,000 people to deport each year, while the Rwanda project would move about 100. Frederiksen argued that the goal is not the number but the signal: "You can no longer enter the European Union illegally and think you can just stay around. That signal, I think, will prevent a lot and ensure that thousands of people will think it through before starting their journey to Europe."

The politics behind the plan are as important as its details. The five countries have been working toward a deal for several months, part of a broader push inside the European Union to increase deportations and curb irregular migration, with centrist governments toughening immigration policies to counter growing public support for far-right parties. The European Commission welcomed the initiative on Monday, even though its president, Ursula von der Leyen, had said during her first term that such innovative solutions were not feasible. Giorgia Meloni's Italy wanted to join the project but was not admitted, on the grounds that it does not comply with the migration and asylum pact, while France firmly refused to participate; President Emmanuel Macron said in June that such centers were "neither effective nor in line with our values." The group is already proposing to replicate the scheme with other third countries such as Uganda or Uzbekistan, and its backers expect legal challenges — which is why, one European diplomat said, they chose "a very small-scale pilot project to demonstrate that it can work."

Why it matters: The numbers give the game away: a pilot for about 100 people against more than 350,000 unexecuted return decisions a year. The five governments know it, and they are saying so openly — the point is the signal, not the throughput. That is what makes the announcement a turning point rather than a policy detail. Five centrist EU governments, with the European Commission's blessing, have now embraced a model that was, until recently, the preserve of the far right: paying a third country to take people Europe has decided it does not want. The legal challenges the backers expect will decide whether the pilot stays a symbol or becomes a template, and the list of countries already asking to copy it suggests the answer will matter far beyond Rwanda.
