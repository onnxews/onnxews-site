Microsoft 365 Outage Knocks Out Outlook, Teams and Copilot for a Second Day

A failure that began Thursday afternoon left millions unable to reach email, chat and cloud apps, and Microsoft said Friday morning that service was still degraded as engineers worked to restore capacity.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 13, 2026 · 4 min readFacebookX

A major Microsoft 365 outage that began on Thursday, November 12, stretched into a second day on Friday, leaving users around the world unable to reach Outlook, Teams, SharePoint and the company's Copilot assistant.

Microsoft said the problems started at 1:26 p.m. Eastern time on Thursday, when Exchange Online began having trouble in the Americas, and soon spread across the wider Microsoft 365 suite. The company is tracking the incident under the code MO1550821.

The affected services include Outlook, Exchange Online, Teams, SharePoint Online, Microsoft 365 Copilot and Defender XDR, with Planner and To Do also listed in updates. The outage hit businesses, schools, government offices and airlines, and disrupted connectors used by OpenAI and Anthropic. Japan's government confirmed its operations were affected.

The scale was visible in user reports. Downdetector, which tracks outage reports, recorded nearly 130,000 reports about Outlook around 1 p.m. Eastern on Thursday, according to the Associated Press, as reported by MacRumors.

Microsoft said the cause was not a surge in demand or a cyberattack. Instead, servers unexpectedly became unavailable, straining the remaining infrastructure, and a core process on the affected servers became overwhelmed, cutting the capacity available to handle requests. The company said there was no evidence of malicious activity.

Engineers paused new deployments, redistributed traffic, shifted work to alternate routes and began restarting servers, and said they were investigating whether to roll back recent configuration changes. Limited recovery was visible by 6:57 p.m. Eastern on Thursday, but Microsoft said the service was not fully restored.

By Friday morning, Microsoft was still reporting service degradation, with recovery uneven across regions. Repairs in Asia Pacific were improving access to mailboxes, but some users were still waiting for messages to arrive, and Microsoft extended the window for email delivery to 48 hours.

A separate, smaller Teams incident earlier on Thursday, tracked as TM1550488, ran from 9:45 to 10:53 a.m. Eastern and was resolved before the main outage began. Microsoft has not confirmed whether the two incidents were linked.

The disruption is the latest reminder of how much of daily work now runs through a handful of cloud services, and how a capacity problem inside one provider can ripple across companies, classrooms and government offices within hours.

For users still affected on Friday, the practical advice from Microsoft was patience: the company said it was working through the remaining capacity problems region by region, and that delayed messages should arrive within the extended 48-hour window.

The outage landed hardest on the services people check first in the morning. Outlook is the default email system for millions of businesses, and Exchange Online sits behind it, so when the mail servers faltered, calendars, meeting invites and shared inboxes went with them. Teams and SharePoint going down at the same time left many offices with no email, no chat and no shared documents — the three tools most workdays run on.

Microsoft's response followed the usual playbook for a capacity failure: stop making changes, spread the load, restart what has failed and look for a recent change to undo. The company has not said which configuration change it is examining, and the full picture will only emerge once the service is stable and engineers can publish a postmortem. Until then, the most useful number is the one that keeps moving: the share of users whose mail is flowing again.

Why it matters: The outage matters because it exposed how concentrated modern work has become: one provider's capacity problem took down email, chat, documents and AI assistants for millions of people at once, including government operations. Microsoft's explanation — servers dropping out and overwhelming what remained — points to an infrastructure resilience question rather than an attack, which makes the follow-up important: users and businesses will want a proper postmortem, not just a status update, and a clear answer on what changes so a single failure cannot cascade the same way again.
