Microsoft Says Global Microsoft 365 Outage Is Over After More Than a Day Offline

Microsoft said on Saturday that its Microsoft 365 services were fully restored after a worldwide outage that knocked out Outlook, Teams and Copilot for more than 24 hours, blaming a problem introduced during a network capacity upgrade and ruling out a cyberattack.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 15, 2026 · 5 min readFacebookX

Microsoft said on Saturday, November 14, that its Microsoft 365 services were fully restored after a global outage that disrupted email and workplace tools for more than a day. The company said it had completed its work on the underlying problem and restored service to the remaining affected customers and regions, closing an incident that had hit governments, courts, hospitals, airlines and banks across more than 70 countries.

The outage began around midday on Thursday, November 12, when users in the Americas first found they could not reach their Exchange Online mailboxes. Microsoft acknowledged the problem at 17:54 GMT on Thursday and soon warned that Microsoft 365 could be affected globally. Within about an hour, the tracking site Downdetector had received reports from users in 74 countries and territories, and it recorded almost 140,000 reports of Outlook problems at around 18:00 GMT on Thursday alone.

The scale of the disruption became clear through Friday. Outlook, Teams and the AI assistant Copilot were all interrupted, and the outage rippled into public services around the world. Government operations in Japan, Singapore and Australia were affected, a trial in Manchester was halted, and email problems in Britain's National Health Service were expected to delay some prescriptions. The Philippines Supreme Court was affected, Canada's national rail network warned passengers they might not receive email updates about their trips, and hospitals in Canada reported email problems of their own.

Businesses felt it too. Swiss luxury group Richemont delayed publication of its half-year results, while the American electric vehicle maker Workhorse Group postponed its quarterly earnings call until Monday, November 16. Airlines including India's IndiGo and Akasa Air reported delays to email support services, though neither reported disruption to flight operations, and the Japanese logistics company Sagawa Express suspended same-day reservations for luggage storage at railway stations and airports.

Microsoft restored service gradually, warning that the scale of the problem meant it had to bring systems back in stages to make sure they stayed stable. By 04:36 GMT on Friday it was reporting only a partial recovery in some parts of the world. By 13:32 GMT on Friday it said there had been broader improvements in North America, sustained improvements across much of Europe and encouraging recovery signals in the Asia-Pacific region. The final restoration for the remaining customers and regions came on Saturday.

The company also gave its first account of what went wrong. Microsoft said the incident was introduced by an issue during an earlier network capacity upgrade that surfaced following a recent network change, and it said the outage was not related to cyber or artificial intelligence agents. Charles Lamanna, Microsoft's executive vice-president of Copilot, Agents and Platform, had said on X that there was no evidence of a cyber incident causing the disruption, and the company apologised on Friday for the disruption.

In its final update, Microsoft said: "We have completed our efforts to address the underlying issue of the incident and restored service to the remaining impacted customers and regions," directing customers to incident MO1550821 in the Microsoft 365 admin center for further updates. The company said it would publish a full post-incident report setting out the root cause and remedial measures once it has completed a review.

The outage underlined how much of the world's daily work now runs through a single company's cloud. More than 430 million people use Microsoft 365 apps, according to Microsoft's website, and the company said in January that it had more than 450 million paid commercial subscriptions. When that system fails, the consequences show up within hours in courtrooms, hospitals, government offices and earnings calendars.

Microsoft has not yet said exactly which part of the network capacity upgrade failed or why the problem took more than a day to fix. The promised post-incident report will be the first detailed account, and it will be closely read by the governments and companies that lost a working day to the outage. Until then, the company has confirmed only the cause in outline and that the services are back.

Why it matters: This outage matters because it was one of the worst IT failures in years for a service that more than 430 million people rely on, and it struck at the heart of ordinary work: email, meetings and documents. The cause — a botched network capacity upgrade, not a hack — is in some ways more worrying than a cyberattack, because it means the failure came from inside Microsoft's own change process. The promised post-incident report will show whether the company's safeguards were adequate, and governments and big customers will want to know what changes before the next upgrade. SEQUENTIAL UPDATE: prior site coverage was the outage on its second day (Nov 13); this article reports the new Nov 14 development — full restoration, the confirmed cause, and the explicit exclusion of a cyberattack or AI agents.
