The Midterm Blitz That Saved Pete Buttigieg From Quitting Politics

The former transportation secretary campaigned in 38 states for 102 candidates, and now says a second presidential run is very much on his mind.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 18, 2026 · 5 min readFacebookX

Pete Buttigieg campaigned in 38 states during the midterms, appearing with 102 candidates, 91 of whom won, according to his advisers. The former transportation secretary, 44, was perhaps the most in-demand campaigner of any potential 2028 Democratic contender, and the blitz, CNN reported on Wednesday, is what saved him from quitting politics altogether.

Buttigieg had seriously considered leaving politics. In a June essay on Substack, he wrote about a fake tip that sent Child Protective Services to his house. He was briefly forbidden from being around his four-year-old twins unsupervised before the allegation was proved false. The episode left him questioning whether public life was worth the cost to his family, and some people close to him wondered if he was done for good.

He now sees the midterms as validation for a second presidential campaign that he is "very much thinking about launching," with a political operation already larger than most other possible contenders. He has not decided. When asked in Denver, he stuck with his standard answer: "Not anytime soon." His advisers anticipate a yes in weeks or a few months. His book, "Next," is out at the end of January 2027, almost eight years to the day he announced his exploratory committee for his first run.

Buttigieg closed the "What's Next" conference in Denver on Friday, November 13, a two-day gathering hosted by the group Majority Democrats. "What just happened, let us be clear, is an opening, not an answer. We did not win because America suddenly fell in love with the Democratic Party," he told the crowd. He opened with a joke about being at the hotel bar to help anyone rebook flights amid the national ground stop that had disrupted travel across the country.

Much of his speech was aimed at his successor as transportation secretary, Sean Duffy, and the administration's handling of the Federal Aviation Administration ground stop. Duffy's comments so far have been "puzzling" and incomplete at best, Buttigieg said. "They did not give us a clear picture... How could they reassure us it wouldn't happen again?" he asked. "It's the opposite of leadership to be in charge for two years and be unable to own what you're in charge of." Duffy and Trump have blamed Buttigieg for the FAA's problems, citing his diversity initiatives, with Trump saying, "Buttigieg ruined the system, he totally ruined it."

The feud spilled onto social media on Monday, when investor Ari Emanuel accused Buttigieg on X of having "made zero progress modernizing the FAA." Buttigieg fired back the same day, defending his record. The exchange had a family subplot: Emanuel's brother Rahm is planning his own 2028 presidential run. During a ground stop in 2023 that lasted about 90 minutes, Duffy at the time attributed it in part to Buttigieg having gone on paternity leave.

Buttigieg's agenda for the party is built around issues he says 70% of Americans and 99% of Democrats agree on: a higher minimum wage, reversing Medicaid cuts and tariffs, universal background checks on gun purchases, and banning gerrymandering. He avoids divisive debates like Medicare for All, though that put him at odds with Michigan Senator-elect Abdul El-Sayed, who called his win validation of Medicare for All. Buttigieg said El-Sayed's plan "kind of evolved to match" his 2020 "Medicare for All Who Want It" proposal, which would have created a government healthcare plan but not required all Americans to be on it. El-Sayed's spokesperson, Roxie Richner, disputed that, saying, "That is not a public option, that is a public guarantee."

His midterm tour was built on early, loyal picks. He endorsed Josh Turek, now senator-elect from Iowa, a month before the primary, and Turek said the endorsement was rivaled only by former Sen. Tom Harkin. In Orangeburg, South Carolina, Buttigieg was praised for helping deliver a $23 million federal grant to better connect two historically Black colleges to downtown. He campaigned from Montana to Mississippi to New Hampshire, and in 2025 he passed on runs for governor and Senate in Michigan, partly to spend time with his children, and thought about starting a podcast instead.

The Denver gathering was also a window into the wider 2028 field. Sen. Ruben Gallego warned the party that its midterm win was a "short-term loan" from voters, not a mandate, and Democrats at the conference set their sights on a 60-seat Senate supermajority and the White House in 2028. Van Hollen announced his Senate leadership challenge on the conference's first day, Rep. Ro Khanna backed Medicare for All, a $25 minimum wage, abolishing ICE, and ending aid to Israel, and Rep. James Talarico called the midterm results the most productive of any president in history. El-Sayed pushed his "Trumpcare" idea. Buttigieg's closing speech was the conference's final word, and his advisers are counting on it to be remembered when the 2028 race begins in earnest.

Why it matters: Buttigieg's 91-of-102 record is the strongest argument any Democrat can make for a 2028 run, and it explains why his name now sits at the center of the post-midterm conversation. A year ago he looked finished, bruised by a false accusation and out of office, and now he has the largest political operation of any potential candidate and a book coming in January. The question is whether the party wants a familiar face who can win the argument on competence, or a break from the past, and his clash with Duffy over the FAA keeps him in the news while he decides. The Denver speech was careful by design: he framed the midterms as an opening, not an answer, which is the language of a candidate who knows the party's voters are still angry and wants them to see him as the one who understands that.
