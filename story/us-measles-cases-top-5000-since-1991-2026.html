U.S. Measles Cases Top 5,000 for the First Time Since 1991 as Three Die in a Single Week

The CDC counted 5,182 cases this year, more than double last year's total, as health officials warn the country is at risk of losing its measles-free status.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 7, 2026 · 3 min readFacebookX

Measles cases in the United States have surged past 5,000 for the first time in 35 years, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention reported on Friday, in the worst year for the disease since 1991. The agency counted 5,182 confirmed cases as of November 5, more than double the 2,289 recorded in all of 2025.

The milestone arrived in a grim week. Three people died of measles in the space of seven days: an infant in Wisconsin, a child in Lewis County, New York, and a resident of Pennsylvania. The Wisconsin death was the state's first from measles since 1990, and the New York death was the state's first in more than 30 years.

Wisconsin health officials said the infant died amid an outbreak in the southwestern part of the state that has sickened 431 people across 13 counties, the state's highest total in more than 35 years. The coroner listed measles as the immediate cause of death. Nine patients in the state have been hospitalised.

Pennsylvania remains the hardest-hit state in the national outbreak, followed by South Carolina with 670 cases and Utah with 536, according to the CDC's figures. Cases have now been reported in 45 states and Washington, D.C., and the vast majority of infections, 96 percent, were among people who were unvaccinated or whose vaccination status was unknown.

The death toll itself has become a point of dispute. State health departments have reported eight measles-related deaths this year, while the CDC's official count stands at four. The gap opened after the agency, under Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr., switched from state health departments' figures to slower federal mortality records to track measles deaths.

The numbers carry a further consequence beyond the immediate toll. The Pan American Health Organization is scheduled to review U.S. transmission data on November 10, and if it finds that a single viral strain has circulated continuously for 12 months, the United States could lose the measles elimination status it has held since 2000.

The scale of the resurgence recalls the last comparable outbreak, from 1989 to 1991, which produced about 55,000 infections and 123 deaths before a major vaccination push brought the disease under control. Health officials say the current outbreak is driven by falling vaccination rates and by pockets of unvaccinated people in which the highly contagious virus can spread rapidly.

Measles spreads through the air and can linger in a room for up to two hours after an infected person leaves. It can cause pneumonia, brain swelling and death, and it is especially dangerous for infants too young to be vaccinated and for people with weakened immune systems. Two doses of the measles, mumps and rubella vaccine are about 97 percent effective at preventing infection.

Public health officials are urging parents to check their children's vaccination records and to catch up on missed doses. With cases still climbing and the elimination review days away, the coming weeks will determine whether the United States can contain an outbreak that has already become the worst in a generation, or whether the country formally loses a public health achievement it has held for a quarter of a century.
