Peltola's Lead Grows to 11,213 Votes in Alaska as Saturday Count Nears a Majority

Official results released Saturday show the Democrat at 49.94 percent, 157 votes short of a majority, with ranked-choice tabulation set for Wednesday.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 15, 2026 · 5 min readFacebookX

Democrat Mary Peltola's lead in Alaska's uncalled Senate race grew to 11,213 votes on Saturday as the state's vote count neared a majority, according to results released by the Alaska Division of Elections. As of Saturday afternoon, Peltola held 140,637 votes, or 49.94 percent, to Republican incumbent Dan Sullivan's 129,424 votes, or 45.96 percent, with about 96 percent of expected ballots counted. The Division's report, timestamped Saturday, November 14 at 3:15:40 p.m., also shows Republican Gerald L. Heikes with 6,002 votes, or 2.13 percent, Republican Dan J. Sullivan Jr. with 4,652 votes, or 1.65 percent, and write-in candidates with 872 votes, or 0.31 percent, for a total of 281,587 votes counted.

Saturday's count added 4,219 votes to the total since Friday, and Peltola won the majority of them. She gained 2,397 votes on the day, compared with 1,596 for Sullivan, taking about 56.8 percent of the newly counted ballots. Her lead, which stood at 10,412 votes on Friday, grew by 801 votes to 11,213, and her vote share rose from 49.84 percent to 49.94 percent. She now sits just 157 votes short of a majority of the votes counted so far: a majority of 281,587 votes would be 140,794, and she holds 140,637. The Associated Press has not yet called the race, and it remains the last uncalled Senate race of the 2026 midterms.

The count is now close enough to its end that the shape of the finish is coming into focus. Ranked-choice tabulation is scheduled for Wednesday, November 18, and the state is aiming to certify the results by November 25. If no candidate finishes above 50 percent in the first count, the ranked-choice process will redistribute the second preferences of voters who backed the eliminated candidates until someone crosses the threshold. Peltola's campaign said on Friday that there is "no mathematical path" for Sullivan to win, while Sullivan's campaign has continued to wait for every ballot to be counted. "We're still waiting for every ballot to be counted," Sullivan's spokesperson, Nate Adams, said.

The race has been shadowed by an extraordinary legal and political dispute. President Donald Trump ordered a lawsuit and federal and state criminal investigations into accusations that the Peltola campaign recruited Dan J. Sullivan Jr., a retired Petersburg teacher who shares the incumbent's name, to run as a spoiler candidate. The younger Sullivan has denied being recruited. The theory behind the complaint matters less with every update to the count: Peltola's lead of 11,213 votes is now more than double the 4,652 votes cast for Dan J. Sullivan Jr., which would leave the lawsuit largely moot even if its underlying claim were proved. Separately, a judge this week rejected the Peltola campaign's request for a list of rejected absentee ballots, a ruling the campaign had sought in order to contact voters whose ballots were set aside.

Elsewhere on the Alaska ballot, the governor's race has already been decided: Democrat Jonathan Kreiss-Tomkins was declared the winner with 141,623 votes, or 50.90 percent, in a result reported alongside Saturday's Senate figures. In the U.S. House race, Rep. Nick Begich held 133,707 votes, or 47.71 percent, to 116,932, or 41.72 percent, for independent Bill Hill. Voters also rejected a measure to repeal ranked-choice voting, with 53.62 percent voting against repeal, a result that preserves the system that will decide the Senate race on Wednesday if no candidate reaches a majority in the first count.

The remaining ballots are mostly absentee and early votes still being processed, and the Division of Elections has been updating its totals daily. With about 96 percent of expected ballots counted, the number of votes left to add is small, and the gap between the two leading candidates is large by the standards of Alaska statewide races. Sullivan would need to win the remaining ballots by a wide margin to close an 11,213-vote gap, and the trend of the count has moved in the other direction: Peltola has gained ground in each of the last several updates, taking a clear majority of the newly counted votes on Saturday as she did on Friday.

For the Senate, the stakes of the result are concrete. Democrats have already won 55 seats in the new Senate, and a Peltola victory would give them 56, widening their majority and changing the arithmetic of the leadership fight now under way in Washington. The ranked-choice tabulation on Wednesday will be the decisive moment if she remains below 50 percent, and the certification target of November 25 means the final answer should arrive before the end of the month. Until then, the count continues, and the only numbers that matter are the ones the Division of Elections publishes.

Saturday's update leaves the race in a familiar position for Alaska watchers: a Democrat ahead by a margin that keeps growing, a Republican incumbent waiting on a dwindling pile of uncounted ballots, and a ranked-choice process standing by in case the first count falls short of a majority. Peltola is 157 votes away from crossing 50 percent outright. Whether she gets there in the final count, or through the second preferences of Heikes and Sullivan Jr. voters on Wednesday, will decide the last Senate seat of the 2026 cycle.

Why it matters: This update matters because it is the last Senate race still uncalled, and the numbers are now moving in only one direction. Peltola has taken a majority of newly counted ballots in successive updates, her lead now exceeds twice the vote total of the alleged spoiler candidate, and she is 157 votes from an outright majority, which undercuts both the legal challenge to the result and the case for a ranked-choice upset. A 56th Democratic seat would also change the arithmetic of the Senate leadership fight playing out in Washington this week. The remaining question is narrow: whether the final count pushes her over 50 percent, or whether Wednesday's ranked-choice tabulation does it for her.
