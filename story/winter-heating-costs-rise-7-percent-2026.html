Winter Heating Bills Will Rise 7% Nationwide, With Heating Oil Costs Up 70%

A new forecast says the average household will pay $1,002 to heat its home this winter, and families in the Northeast who rely on heating oil face bills of nearly $3,000.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 14, 2026 · 5 min readFacebookX

American households are projected to spend an average of $1,002 on home heating this winter, a 7.1 percent increase from last winter, according to a new forecast from the National Energy Assistance Directors Association. The increase of $66 over last winter's average may sound modest, but it lands on families already struggling with rising food and gasoline prices, and for millions of households the jump will be far steeper than the national average.

Households that rely on heating oil face the sharpest increases by far. Nearly 5 million American households depend on the fuel, and their seasonal expenses could surge 70.2 percent, to approximately $2,977, up from $1,749 last winter. The Northeast, where heating oil is most common, faces the worst of it: residential customers there who rely on heating oil could see spikes as high as 70 percent, according to the report. Heating oil is essentially the same distillate as diesel, which is also used to make jet fuel, and refiners facing shortages have been forced to choose between producing jet fuel and heating oil.

Other heating methods will also become more expensive, though by less. Electricity-based heating costs are projected to increase about 6 percent, while average propane expenses could climb about 5 percent. Natural gas costs are expected to remain roughly the same as last winter. The report was released on November 10 by the association, known as NEADA, which represents state officials who seek federal energy funding.

The association blames the increases on disruptions to global fuel supplies linked to the war with Iran and the ongoing conflict in Ukraine. "Heating oil costs are a direct consequence of the Iran war," Joe Adamski, managing director of the supply chain consultancy ProcureAbility, told The Post. "We have been saying for months that come winter, heating oil was going to be at a premium due to Gulf events, and now we have arrived." National average residential heating oil prices fell to $6.14 a gallon, down from $6.23 a gallon, in the week ending November 2, according to the U.S. Energy Information Administration, but they are still roughly 69 percent higher than the same time last year.

Heating oil is primarily used in older homes built before 1990, which are largely concentrated in the Northeast. About 59 percent of households in Maine rely on heating oil, along with 43 percent in New Hampshire, 40 percent in Vermont, 39 percent in Connecticut, 29 percent in Rhode Island, 24 percent in Massachusetts, 19 percent in New York and 16 percent in Pennsylvania, according to U.S. Census Bureau data. Those states are where the projected increases will bite hardest, and where the gap between the national average and the local reality is widest.

The financial consequences will fall hardest on families with the least room to absorb them. About one in six U.S. households are behind on their utility payments, owing about $23 billion, NEADA said. "For higher-income households, these increases may be an inconvenience. For low- and middle-income families, they can be devastating," Mark Wolfe, the association's executive director, said in a statement. "Families are already struggling with rising costs for food, housing and other necessities. Adding hundreds or even thousands of dollars to their winter heating bills will push many of them further into debt." More than a quarter of U.S. families said they have reduced the heating and cooling in their homes to uncomfortably low levels to save money, and 19 percent said they cut back on groceries or switched to less expensive food options, according to a Consumer Reports survey from August.

There is one possible mitigating factor: experts are forecasting a milder start to the winter season, with a strong El Nino pushing the jet stream further south and east, which could reduce heating oil use and keep average costs lower. Oil prices have also fallen recently as President Trump signaled he is not planning to strike Iran anytime soon and is instead continuing negotiations, and gasoline prices fell below $4 a gallon for the first time since mid-July, according to AAA. But Wolfe warned families not to assume prices will keep falling. "While the recent decline in heating oil prices is welcome news, families should not assume that prices will continue to fall," he said. "The situation in the Strait of Hormuz remains highly volatile, and continued uncertainty about global oil supplies makes it difficult to predict where prices will go."

The association's advice to states is to prepare for the worst. "For planning purposes, we believe it is prudent to assume that heating oil prices will remain elevated throughout the winter heating season," Wolfe said. "We hope we're wrong, but states need to prepare for the possibility that prices will stay high." NEADA has called on Congress to provide financial assistance before winter heating expenses increase further, and it has urged emergency funding for the Low Income Home Energy Assistance Program, known as LIHEAP, which helps low-income families pay their heating and cooling bills. The president's fiscal year 2027 budget proposal would eliminate $4 billion in funding for the program, cutting off support to approximately 6 million low-income households that rely on it.

Why it matters: This forecast lands in a winter already defined by affordability pressure, and the heating numbers show the same squeeze reaching the thermostat. The 70 percent jump for heating-oil households is the sharpest edge of the story, and it is driven by a war thousands of miles away — which means the price Americans pay to stay warm this winter will depend as much on events in the Strait of Hormuz as on the weather outside their windows.
