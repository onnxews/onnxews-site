Russell Brand: From Prime-Time Fame to a Guilty Verdict — the Case That Took Three Years to Reach Court

A London jury convicted the 51-year-old comedian of rape and three counts of sexual assault. The verdict closes a trial three years in the making — but police say their investigations are not over.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 6, 2026 · 6 min readFacebookX

British comedian Russell Brand was found guilty of one count of rape and three counts of sexual assault by a jury at London's Southwark Crown Court on Thursday. He was acquitted of two other rape charges and one count of attempted indecent assault.

The charges involved six women, with alleged offences spanning from 1999 to 2009 in locations including a hotel room, a London bar, a radio station, a recording studio and Brand's own home. The rape conviction relates to an assault on a 21-year-old fan Brand met after a performance at the Hammersmith Apollo in 2009. Brand, 51, denied all the charges.

Judge Joel Bennathan refused Brand bail and remanded him in custody, telling him he should expect a significant custodial term. He will be sentenced on Monday, November 9. Rape carries a sentencing range of four to 19 years in custody, and legal experts have estimated Brand could receive around eight or nine years.

The trial, which began on October 12, lasted three weeks. Six complainants gave evidence, and prosecutors told the jury that the six women did not know one another. Prosecutor Riel Karmy-Jones KC told the jury Brand was a "ruthless, calculating, manipulative" sexual predator whose desire for contact was "indiscriminate and voracious." The jury convicted on four of the seven counts and acquitted on three — a split verdict that shows the jury weighed each allegation on its own evidence rather than accepting the prosecution's case wholesale.

The case began more than three years before the trial, with a media investigation. On September 16, 2023, Channel 4's Dispatches programme, "Russell Brand: In Plain Sight," and The Sunday Times published a joint investigation carrying allegations from four women, including rape, sexual assault and emotional abuse. The reporting described incidents spanning 2006 to 2013, including an account of a relationship that began when one woman was 16 and Brand was in his early thirties, and an alleged rape at a Los Angeles home. Brand posted a video the same day denying the claims, insisting that "the relationships I had were absolutely, always consensual" and calling the reporting a "coordinated attack." Within 48 hours, the Metropolitan Police had opened a formal investigation.

The road from that investigation to a courtroom was long. The Metropolitan Police interviewed Brand under caution on multiple occasions through 2023 and 2024, and it was not until April 4, 2025 that the Crown Prosecution Service authorized the first five charges, relating to four women. Two more charges were added on December 23, 2025, after reports from two additional women about incidents in 2009, bringing the total to seven charges involving six women. The 18-month gap between the start of the police investigation and the first charges reflects how difficult it is to build a prosecutable case on allegations from years earlier — and how high the bar is before a prosecutor will put them before a jury.

The six women whose evidence the jury heard were different from the four women who featured in the original 2023 investigation. The programme helped bring the six women forward, but their accounts were their own, and the jury was deciding what happened to them — not re-litigating the broadcast. That distinction matters, because it means the verdict rests on evidence the jury heard in court, from witnesses who testified under oath and were cross-examined, rather than on the media reporting that started the case.

Brand's fall has been as public as his rise. He rose to fame as a comedian and presenter on BBC radio and Channel 4, then crossed over to Hollywood films and became a tabloid fixture, including through his brief marriage to pop star Katy Perry. By 2013 he had largely left traditional entertainment for online video, reinventing himself as a podcast host and commentator. The 2023 investigation stripped away what remained of that career: his management dropped him, YouTube demonetized his channel, charity ties were severed, and the BBC published its own review of his conduct. A civil lawsuit was filed in the United States in November 2023 under New York's Adult Survivors Act.

For the criminal justice system, the case is a measure of how historical sexual offence cases are now handled. The offences the jury considered dated back as far as 1999 — 27 years before the verdict. The prosecution's case depended on the women's accounts, on contemporaneous messages and corroborating witnesses, and on the pattern across six complainants who did not know each other. The defence's case was that the encounters were consensual. The jury's split verdict — convictions on the 2009 Hammersmith Apollo rape and three sexual assaults, acquittals on the rest — suggests it believed some accounts and not others, which is how juries are supposed to work when the evidence is tested count by count.

The verdict is not the end of the legal story. After the verdict, London's Metropolitan Police confirmed Brand is under investigation for two further rape allegations made by two other women. The force's commissioner, Sir Mark Rowley, said he would not be surprised if more people came forward. Those investigations are separate from the trial, and they could lead to further charges — or to no charges, if the evidence does not meet the threshold for prosecution.

Sentencing on Monday will be the next moment that matters. The judge has already signaled that a significant custodial term is coming, and the range for rape alone runs from four to 19 years. Brand's legal team will have the opportunity to argue in mitigation, and the victims will have the opportunity to say how the offences affected them. Whatever sentence is imposed, Brand will have the right to appeal the convictions, and the process could run for months more.

The verdict closes one chapter: six women, three weeks of evidence, four convictions. But the police files are not closed, the sentencing has not happened, and the question the case has raised since September 2023 — how many more people will come forward now that a jury has believed the first six — is still open. For the women who gave evidence, the verdict is an answer. For everyone else watching, the case is not yet finished.
