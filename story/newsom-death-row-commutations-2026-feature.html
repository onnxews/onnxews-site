Newsom Emptied America's Largest Death Row. Why 382 Cases Still Hang in the Balance

California's governor commuted all 548 death sentences in the largest mass clemency in American history. Most of the cases still need the state Supreme Court's approval — and the death penalty itself survives.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 6, 2026 · 6 min readFacebookX

California Gov. Gavin Newsom announced Thursday that he will commute the death sentences of all 548 people on the state's death row to life in prison without the possibility of parole. The Death Penalty Information Center described the move as the largest mass commutation in American history.

In an open letter to Californians, Newsom wrote that he firmly believes state-sanctioned killing is incompatible with California's values and undermines the foundations of the legal system. The decision comes in his final weeks as governor after eight years in office, and follows the moratorium on executions he imposed shortly after taking office in 2019.

The commutations will not all take effect at once. Under the state constitution, the California Supreme Court must approve clemency for anyone convicted of more than one felony, which means the court will have to review 382 of the 548 cases, or roughly 70 percent, according to the governor's office. The remaining cases take effect immediately. That review is where the fate of most of the commutations will now be decided — the court must sign off on each case individually, and there is no timetable for when it will finish.

California has the largest death row population in the United States, holding more than a quarter of the nation's condemned prisoners. Among them are serial killers Randy Kraft, Charles Ng and Richard Allen Davis, who kidnapped and killed 12-year-old Polly Klaas in 1993. The state's last execution took place in 2006, and only 13 people have been executed in California since 1976. In practice, California's death row has for two decades been a place where sentences were imposed but almost never carried out — the commutations formalize a reality that has existed since the last execution nearly twenty years ago.

The move does not repeal the death penalty, which California voters chose to keep in 2012 and again in 2016. Prosecutors can still seek death sentences in new cases. Victims' advocates criticised the decision and said they would challenge it in court, while supporters of the move pointed to figures showing that 68 percent of the people on California's death row are defendants of color — a disparity that has long been at the center of the debate over whether the punishment is applied fairly.

Newsom's action has a precedent, and the comparison shows just how large it is. In January 2003, two days before leaving office, Illinois Governor George Ryan commuted the sentences of all 167 people on his state's death row, calling the system "fraught with error." Ryan, a Republican who had supported the death penalty for most of his career, had declared a moratorium on executions in 2000 — the first by any governor in American history — after a commission he appointed returned 85 recommendations to reduce the chance of executing an innocent person and the legislature passed only one of them. In Illinois, more death row inmates had been exonerated, 13, than executed, 12, since the state reinstated capital punishment. Ryan also pardoned four condemned men he concluded were innocent. His commutations paved the way for Illinois to abolish the death penalty entirely in 2011, and seven other states have abolished it in the years since.

More recent mass commutations have been far smaller. In December 2022, Oregon Governor Kate Brown commuted the sentences of the 17 prisoners on her state's death row to life without parole — then the second-largest mass commutation of the modern era. In December 2024, President Joe Biden commuted the sentences of 37 of the 40 people on federal death row. Newsom's 548 dwarfs all of them combined, and by more than three times the previous record.

What a commutation does is narrow and specific: it changes a sentence, it does not free anyone. The 548 people whose sentences Newsom commuted will remain in prison for the rest of their lives, without the possibility of parole. They will move from death row to the general prison population. What it does not do is change the law. The death penalty remains on California's books, district attorneys can still charge capital cases, and juries can still return death verdicts. The next governor could, in theory, resume executions — though no governor has carried one out in twenty years, and the machinery to do so, from the execution chamber to the lethal injection protocols, has been frozen since the moratorium.

The legal challenges will now take center stage. Victims' advocates have said they will challenge the commutations in court, and the California Supreme Court's review of the 382 multi-felony cases is itself a legal process in which prosecutors and victims' families can be heard. The court has the power to block individual commutations, and the governor's office has acknowledged that the review will take time. How the court handles the first cases will set the pattern for the rest.

The commutations also land in a national debate that has been moving in one direction for years. Executions in the United States have fallen sharply from their peak, the number of death sentences imposed each year has collapsed, and a growing number of states have abolished the punishment or stopped carrying it out. What California has done is different in kind, not just in scale: it is the first time the state with the largest death row in the country has emptied it in a single act, by a governor using the clemency power rather than by a legislature or a court.

What happens next will be decided in three places: the California Supreme Court, which must review 382 cases; the courts, where victims' advocates have promised challenges; and the governor's office after Newsom leaves, where his successor will inherit a death penalty that exists on paper but has no one left on death row. The 548 commutations are, for the people whose sentences were changed, final in their effect once they take hold — they will die in prison rather than by execution. But 382 of those cases still await review by the California Supreme Court, which has the power to block individual commutations, so for those cases that finality is not yet established. The question California voters will answer next, if the law itself is ever put to them again, is whether the punishment they chose to keep in 2012 and 2016 should survive a state that has now decided, in practice, not to use it.

The article has also been updated to make clear that 382 of the 548 commutations still await review by the California Supreme Court, so their final effect is not yet established.
