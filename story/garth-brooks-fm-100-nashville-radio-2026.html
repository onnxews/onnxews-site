Garth Brooks Buys Nashville's Lightning 100 Frequency to Launch Country Station FM-100

Garth Brooks' Sevens Network has agreed to buy the 100.1 FM frequency from Nashville's longest-running independent station — and turn it into a country station built around local news, female artists and public service.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 12, 2026 · 4 min readFacebookX

Country superstar Garth Brooks is buying the frequency of one of Nashville's most beloved radio stations — and promising to keep that station alive while he does it. Brooks' Sevens Network, LLC has agreed to acquire Lightning 100's 100.1 FM frequency from Tuned In Broadcasting, subject to Federal Communications Commission approval.

Under the deal, announced November 10, Brooks will turn 100.1 FM into a new country station called FM-100. Lightning 100, Nashville's longest-running independent radio station, will move its programming to Cromwell Media's 106.3 FM and 102.5 HD3 frequencies — and it will continue broadcasting on 100.1 FM until the FCC approves the sale.

“This deal moves the 100.1 FM frequency to Sevens Network, but keeps Lightning 100 on the air at a new place on the dial,” said Lester Turner, president of Tuned In Broadcasting and Lightning 100. “We are disappointed to leave our home at 100.1 FM but thrilled to have Cromwell Media Nashville as our partner in this transition. Listeners will need to tune to new frequencies but will continue to find the familiar Lightning 100 programming that they know and love.”

Brooks describes FM-100 as something different from a normal country station. He wants it to be the “Voice of Music City,” focusing on local and breaking news and the “entire family of country music” — the legends, the greats and the future greats. The station will prominently feature female country artists, including women who helped build the genre, alongside today's artists.

The station is also being built as a service to the city. The popular Nashville Severe Weather service will interrupt regular broadcasts to get critical weather information to listeners as needed, and FM-100 will help give tourists information while they visit. Brooks said the station will not compete with Nashville's existing country channels. “Our job is NOT to compete with the local channels in Nashville, but to PROMOTE what they have going on,” he said. “We are NOT a reporter, we are a SERVICE to Music City and its businesses.”

Brooks said he began the deal in an effort to help Lightning 100 through a difficult financial situation, and hopes to launch FM-100 as a way to give back to Nashville and the country music community. Beyond the music, the station is being positioned around local and breaking news, severe-weather interruptions and information for tourists visiting the city.

The purchase comes shortly after Brooks' Sevens Network ended its three-year partnership with TuneIn. The collaboration launched several online stations, including country outlet The BIG 615, in 2023; Brooks and Sevens formally exited the partnership in September. Brooks is also currently on his 2026 “Blame It All on My Roots Tour”.

For Nashville listeners, the deal changes one of the city's most familiar spots on the dial. Lightning 100 has been locally owned and operated for 36 years, broadcasting an adult album alternative format — a broad mix of indie, alternative, singer-songwriter and local music — and its programming includes the weekly concert broadcast “Nashville Sunday Night,” the industry-focused “Music Business Radio” and “The 615,” which spotlights local artists. The station says those programs will continue after the move, and it recently announced plans to bring back its Live on the Green music festival in 2027. It remains unclear whether Lightning 100 will keep its longtime name once it leaves its current frequency.

The deal still needs the FCC's sign-off, and until that approval arrives, Lightning 100 stays where it is. For now, the focus is on serving Music City — and on an approval that will decide when the new station can finally take its place on the dial.

Why it matters: This deal matters because it is a rare radio story in which nobody obviously loses: a country superstar gets a flagship frequency in Music City, and a beloved independent station gets a financial lifeline and a new home instead of going silent. The test will be whether FM-100 can be what Brooks promises — a service station that promotes rather than competes with its neighbors, gives female artists the airplay country radio has long denied them, and keeps live local voices on the air during emergencies. If it works, Nashville gets a country station that behaves like a public service; if the FCC approval drags or the format drifts toward just another playlist, the goodwill behind the announcement will fade fast. Watch the FCC filing and the launch date for the next step.
