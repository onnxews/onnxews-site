Blanche Walks It Back: No Criminal Probes of Democrats Over Oversight Threats

The attorney general said Thursday the Justice Department has no active criminal investigations into lawmakers' words, days after suggesting oversight threats 'could be' a crime.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 14, 2026 · 4 min readFacebookX

Attorney General Todd Blanche walked back the Justice Department's threats against Democratic lawmakers on Thursday, telling reporters the department has no active criminal investigations into what incoming members of Congress have said about investigating the Trump administration. 'There's no ongoing investigations, criminal investigations into words that that incoming senators or congressmen have said,' Blanche said.

Asked what a Democrat had said that crossed a criminal line, Blanche replied, 'I'd never said that that point has been crossed.' Pressed further, he was blunt: 'That is not a crime to say that. Nobody's saying that's a crime.'

The remarks marked a sharp retreat from Blanche's own position days earlier. In a CNN interview that aired on Sunday, Blanche said 'there could be' a crime to investigate if Democrats' rhetoric turned threatening. His Thursday comments narrowed the question: the words lawmakers have used so far do not, on their own, amount to a crime.

The walk-back came after a week of escalating warnings from the administration. White House counsel Will Scharf wrote this week to Rep. Robert Garcia, the California Democrat in line to chair the House Oversight Committee in January, warning that threats aimed at deterring officials from public service 'may cross the line into blatant criminality.' Garcia has warned Trump officials to 'lawyer up.' Scharf cited a 1957 Supreme Court decision, Watkins v. United States, and wrote that congressional investigations 'must further a legitimate legislative task.'

A day before Blanche spoke, Aaron Reitz, the U.S. attorney for the Southern District of Texas, directed Sen. Richard Blumenthal, a Connecticut Democrat, to preserve records from his inquiry into private donors to the White House ballroom. Reitz accused Blumenthal of threatening 'citizens for associating themselves with the President and other public officials' and warned of 'significant legal consequences' if the senator failed to comply. Blumenthal said Wednesday he 'will not be intimidated' and sent Reitz a preservation demand of his own.

Blanche also addressed the matter in a separate radio interview on Thursday, telling SiriusXM host Dan Abrams, 'I have not threatened any member of Congress with a criminal investigation.' He said his comments were meant to defend administration officials against intimidation, not to discourage legitimate congressional oversight, and he pledged to cooperate with the incoming Democratic-controlled Congress. 'I have every intention of working with the incoming Congress,' he said.

He was equally direct about Sen. Elissa Slotkin, a Michigan Democrat who had suggested young Americans think twice before taking jobs in the administration. 'What the senator said in and of itself is absolutely not a crime,' Blanche said, adding that no criminal investigation of Slotkin was underway. He also confirmed that a grand jury declined to indict Slotkin and other Democratic lawmakers over a video addressing military orders, while noting that 99 percent of federal cases presented to grand juries result in indictments.

Blanche was speaking after a Justice Department press conference announcing Operation TorNado, an international crackdown on dark web drug trafficking that produced 354 arrests worldwide, including 132 in the United States, 59 in the United Kingdom, 45 in Hungary and 41 in Germany. Authorities also seized five metric tons of drugs and more than 210 million euros in criminal assets.

The episode leaves the department in an awkward spot: it spent the week warning Democrats about criminal exposure for their oversight plans, then spent Thursday insisting no such investigations exist — all weeks before Democrats take control of both chambers and Garcia takes the Oversight gavel. The next test is whether the department's actions match its attorney general's words.

Why it matters: The walk-back matters because it draws a line the administration had spent a week blurring: promising to investigate the other party is not itself a crime, and the attorney general has now said so on the record twice in one day. That does not end the fight — the Reitz letter to Blumenthal is still live, Scharf's letter still stands, and Democrats take both chambers in January with oversight plans already announced. But it does mean the Justice Department has publicly ruled out the most extreme version of the threat: criminal cases built on lawmakers' words alone. The next test is whether the department's actions match its attorney general's words.
