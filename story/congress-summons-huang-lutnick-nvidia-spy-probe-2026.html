Congress Summons Huang and Lutnick as Nvidia Spy Scandal Reaches Capitol Hill

Senate Banking Chairman Tim Scott wants the former Nvidia chief on December 3 and the commerce secretary on December 10, with subpoenas threatened if either refuses.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 14, 2026 · 5 min readFacebookX

Congress is hauling the two men at the center of the Nvidia spy scandal to Capitol Hill. Senate Banking Committee Chairman Tim Scott, a South Carolina Republican, has asked former Nvidia chief executive Jensen Huang to testify at a public hearing on December 3 and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick to appear on December 10, as lawmakers demand answers about a suspected Chinese spy, diverted AI chips and a stalled federal investigation.

Lutnick has already agreed to come voluntarily. 'Secretary Lutnick has proactively agreed to appear voluntarily before the Banking Committee to address their questions,' a spokesperson for Lutnick told CNN. Arrangements for his testimony were still being worked out as of Friday.

Scott left little doubt about what happens if Huang declines. In his letter to the former Nvidia chief, the senator warned that the committee was prepared to use all tools at its disposal, including subpoenas, to conduct what he called 'legitimate and necessary oversight.' Scott wrote that 'recent reports raise serious concerns about our national security and the protection of sensitive U.S. technology.'

The summons came days after Huang stepped down as Nvidia's chief executive on November 12, after reports that he had an extramarital affair with Alice Huang, an executive at Megaspeed, a Singapore-based Nvidia customer. U.S. officials believe Alice Huang, who shares the former chief executive's surname but is of no relation, may be a Chinese intelligence agent, and she is suspected of smuggling Nvidia's advanced semiconductors into China.

Attorney General Todd Blanche said at a November 12 press conference that the Justice Department is currently investigating whether Alice Huang is a spy and has not reached a conclusion 'around who she is or is not.'

Senate Democratic Leader Chuck Schumer is demanding a 'full accounting' of what President Donald Trump knew about the alleged spying and how Huang influenced the administration's national security decisions, including its decision to allow Nvidia to sell advanced computer chips to China last year for a cut of the profits. On November 12, Schumer posted on social media that 'Congress must demand answers' from Huang, Nvidia and the Trump administration, despite the chief executive's resignation.

House Democratic leader Hakeem Jeffries said at a November 12 press conference that 'there are certainly more questions than answers.' Sen. Josh Hawley, a Missouri Republican, said he was glad Huang resigned and that a congressional investigation would be appropriate. 'I'm opposed to exporting these chips,' Hawley said.

The House is moving on a parallel track. On November 12, the Republican leaders of the House Foreign Affairs and Intelligence committees and the Select Committee on China sent a second request for Huang to appear before Congress, and also sent letters to Nvidia general counsel Tim Teter and Undersecretary of Commerce Jeffrey Kessler. 'We need to know the full extent to which our national security has been put at risk,' wrote Reps. Brian Mast, Rick Crawford and John Moolenaar.

At the heart of the inquiry is the Commerce Department's handling of Megaspeed. The department's Bureau of Industry and Security launched an investigation in 2024 into whether Nvidia chips supplied to Megaspeed were being diverted to China in violation of U.S. export restrictions, and the investigation was subsequently transferred to the Justice Department. The department has not placed Megaspeed on its Entity List, which restricts access to American technology, and according to CBS News it has not added any company to the list since October 2025.

The scrutiny has also exposed tensions inside the administration. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent confronted Lutnick last week over the Commerce Department's failure to include Megaspeed on the Entity List, and the New York Times reported that Trump was briefed roughly a year ago on intelligence about the alleged spying and did not act on it. Trump has said he does not regret sitting on the information, calling Huang an 'honorable guy' who did an 'incredible job' leading Nvidia, and said on November 10 that Huang told him he had no idea the woman was an accused spy.

The Banking Committee oversees export controls in Congress, and its hearings are expected to examine how those controls were enforced, how the Megaspeed investigation was handled and what sensitive American technology may have moved overseas. With Huang due December 3 and Lutnick December 10, the scandal that cost Nvidia its chief executive is now a formal congressional investigation with dates on the calendar.

Why it matters: This is no longer a corporate scandal — it is now a scheduled congressional inquiry into how American export controls work, or fail to work, at the most sensitive edge of the AI race. The dates matter: December 3 and 10 put both men before the committee in public, weeks before a new Congress takes over, and Lutnick's quick agreement to appear voluntarily suggests the Commerce Department would rather testify than be subpoenaed. The testimony will also test Trump's account of what he knew and when, since the Times has reported he was briefed about a year ago. What the hearings establish about diverted chips will shape the push for tighter export controls that both parties in Congress are now demanding.
