Crypto's CLARITY Act Faces a January 3 Deadline After the Democratic Sweep

The crypto industry's market-structure bill, which failed a Senate procedural vote 49-50 in September, has until January 3 to become law before it dies with the current Congress and must start over under Democratic committee chairs.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 12, 2026 · 5 min readFacebookX

The crypto industry's long campaign for a federal market-structure law is running out of time. The CLARITY Act, the bill that would divide oversight of crypto markets between the Securities and Exchange Commission and the Commodity Futures Trading Commission, faces a January 3, 2027, deadline after Democrats won control of both chambers of Congress in the November 3 midterm elections. Any bill that is not law by the time the current Congress ends dies and must be reintroduced from scratch in the new Congress.

The bill's path through the Senate has been stalled since September 15, when senators rejected cloture on the motion to proceed by a vote of 49 to 50, eleven short of the 60 votes required. The House had passed the legislation 294 to 134 on July 17, 2025, with 78 Democrats voting in favor, but the Senate never got to debate it. Every Democrat who voted opposed advancing the bill, and so did four Republicans: Susan Collins, Josh Hawley, Jerry Moran, and Thom Tillis.

One procedural opening remains. Tillis voted no specifically so he could file a motion to reconsider, a Senate procedure that lets leaders bring the bill back without starting over, and that motion is still pending. Senate leaders could try again in the lame-duck session, the weeks between the election and the new Congress. But the math has not changed: the bill still needs 60 votes, and the Democrats who blocked it in September now have little reason to compromise before they take control in January.

The disagreements that sank the bill have not been resolved. Senate Republicans released revised legislation on September 14, and Senator Cynthia Lummis said the draft incorporated 126 changes requested by Democrats, addressing ethics enforcement, stablecoin-related deposit risks, and protections for developers. But Democratic Senator Mark Warner opposed the text over conflicts involving senior officials' crypto interests, saying the proposal lacked meaningful safeguards against presidential profiteering. In July, seven Senate Democrats who had been negotiating the bill, led by Warner, said the Republican draft "falls short," arguing that provisions "addressing ethics for elected officials, consumer protection, illicit finance, conflicts of interest and market integrity must be strengthened." No deal was reached before the September vote.

Banks also fought the bill. Senator Collins warned it could cause "community banks and credit unions to lose deposits," because banks do not want crypto platforms paying customers rewards on stablecoins, which could pull money out of savings accounts. The fight over stablecoin rewards became one of the issues that stalled the bill, alongside the ethics dispute over the president's family's crypto business.

The bill tries to settle the question the crypto industry has faced since Bitcoin launched: who regulates it. Tokens that behave like investments in a company would fall under the SEC, while tokens that behave like commodities, closer to gold or oil than to stock, would go to the CFTC. Exchanges, brokers, and custodians would get clear paths to register. The House version moved through committee with bipartisan support, and the Senate Agriculture Committee advanced its version on January 29, 2026, without Democratic support, followed by the Banking Committee on May 14, voting 15 to 9. Chairman Tim Scott said the vote showed "Washington can still work together." That spirit did not reach the floor.

If the bill dies on January 3, the next version will be written under Democratic chairs. Senator Elizabeth Warren, the Banking Committee's top Democrat and one of crypto's sharpest critics, would be in line to chair it, and Representative Maxine Waters would be in line to lead the House Financial Services Committee. A reintroduced bill would have to go through committee hearings and floor votes in both chambers again, and a Democratic Senate would also vote on future nominees to the SEC and CFTC.

Without a law, crypto rules will keep coming from regulators. The GENIUS Act, the stablecoin law President Trump signed the day after the House passed the CLARITY Act, stays in force, and the SEC and CFTC can keep writing guidance and rules through a joint effort called Project Crypto. That matters for companies such as Coinbase, Robinhood, and Circle, whose businesses depend on how exchanges and stablecoins are treated. But agency rules are easier to reverse than laws, which is why the industry pushed so hard for Congress to act, and why the January 3 deadline now looms over the whole effort.

The industry spent heavily to shape the Congress that will now decide the bill's fate, pouring money into campaigns and winning its first federal law in the GENIUS Act, but the midterm results cut against it. The current Senate can try again in the lame-duck session, but to pass there the bill needs 11 more yes votes than it got in September, from a mix of the Democrats who voted as a bloc against it and the four Republicans who joined them, and no deal has been made public since the vote. After January 3, that math no longer applies, and crypto starts over with a Congress run by the party that blocked the bill.

Why it matters: The CLARITY Act's collapse is a case study in how a midterm wave can kill a bill that was already wounded. The legislation had everything the crypto industry wanted except the votes: it passed the House with 78 Democrats, it had a motion to reconsider keeping it alive in the Senate, and it had a lame-duck window to try again. What it did not have was a deal on the two issues that sank it — ethics rules for officials with crypto interests and protections for bank deposits — and the election removed any incentive for Democrats to compromise. If the bill dies on January 3, the industry's next market-structure law will be written by Elizabeth Warren's Banking Committee, which is about as far from the industry's preferred authors as Washington gets.
