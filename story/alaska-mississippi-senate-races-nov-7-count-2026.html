Two Senate Races Still Uncalled as Democrats Lead in Alaska and Mississippi

Mary Peltola sues Alaska over absentee ballots while Scott Colom's lead grows in Mississippi, with the size of the Democratic majority still undecided.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 7, 2026 · 4 min readFacebookX

Four days after the midterm elections, two Senate races remain uncalled, and Democrats lead in both. In Mississippi, Democrat Scott Colom leads Republican Senator Cindy Hyde-Smith by roughly 1,800 votes in the Associated Press count as of early Friday. In Alaska, Democrat Mary Peltola leads Republican Senator Dan Sullivan by just 637 votes, with about three-quarters of the ballots counted.

The Associated Press has called 33 of the 35 Senate races on the ballot. Democrats have already secured at least 54 seats to the Republicans' 44, enough for control of the chamber. The two outstanding races will decide only how large the majority is: 54, 55 or 56 seats, depending on how Alaska and Mississippi finish.

The Alaska race took a legal turn on Friday when Peltola's campaign filed a lawsuit seeking information on absentee ballots in the narrow contest. Alaska's Department of Law responded that it could not and would not turn over the information the campaign requested, setting up a court fight over ballot records in a race separated by a fraction of a percentage point.

Alaska's count is complicated by the state's ranked-choice system. Peltola leads with 48.2 percent of the vote to Sullivan's 47.9 percent, but if no candidate passes 50 percent, a ranked-choice tabulation is scheduled for November 18. About 10,000 ballots still need to be hand-counted, and the state must finish counting by November 18 and certify within seven days, by November 25.

A third candidate adds another wrinkle to the Alaska race. A retired teacher also named Dan Sullivan received about 3,583 votes, and the similarity of his name to the incumbent senator's has become the center of a legal and political fight. The Justice Department opened an investigation in July after reports that the namesake candidate had met with Democratic operatives and members of Peltola's campaign staff, though Peltola has denied any involvement.

In Mississippi, Colom leads Hyde-Smith 49.0 percent to 48.9 percent, with about 97 percent of ballots counted and roughly 20,000 ballots still uncounted. His lead has grown as late ballots have been counted, and Hyde-Smith has vowed to keep fighting. We are not backing down, she posted on X on Friday. This election is not over.

Mississippi's timeline stretches into December. Late mail ballots can still arrive until November 10 if postmarked by Election Day, and affidavit voters have until then to cure their ballots with identification. Counties must certify their results by November 13, and the Secretary of State declares the final results on December 3, unless court challenges delay it.

Mississippi has no formal recount law, which could matter if the final margin narrows to a few hundred votes. Candidates cannot simply ask for a recount, so any challenge would have to go through the courts or the state's contest procedures. That absence of a recount mechanism makes the counting of every remaining affidavit and mail ballot especially consequential.

The House picture is also still filling in. The Associated Press had called 254 races for Democrats and 162 for Republicans as of Friday afternoon, with 19 House seats still up in the air. Friday's calls included Republican wins for Representative Jeff Hurd in Colorado, Mark Lamb in Arizona and Anthony Constantino in New York's 21st District, but the Democratic majority is far beyond doubt.

Neither Senate race is expected to change control of the chamber, but both matter for the next two years. A 56-seat majority would give Democrats more room to confirm nominees and pass legislation than a 54-seat one, and the two contests have become the last unresolved business of an election that has already redrawn the balance of power in Washington. The counts continue into next week, and both sides are watching every batch.

Correction (Nov. 11, 2026): An earlier version of this article said Mary Peltola led Dan Sullivan by 626 votes in Alaska. The count at the time was 102,962 to 102,325 — a lead of 637 votes.
