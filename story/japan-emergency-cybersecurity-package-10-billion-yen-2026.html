Japan Approves a 10 Billion Yen Emergency Cyber Package After a Wave of Data Breaches

Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi's government will spend about $63 million from reserves on AI-assisted vulnerability checks, police cyber teams and a public-private rescue unit.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 8, 2026 · 4 min readFacebookX

Japan has approved 10 billion yen in emergency cybersecurity funding after a wave of data breaches hit major companies and government agencies. Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi announced the package at a ministerial meeting on Friday, November 6, and the Cabinet authorized the reserve spending the same day, according to the Associated Press.

The package is designed to build a resilient society in which the public and private sectors share information, provide swift assistance to those affected and recover from damage, the government said. Takaichi told ministers that a rapid response to potential attacks is essential in the era of AI-generated attacks, and she directed them to secure further resources beyond the initial allocation.

The initial funding, worth roughly $63 million, is intended to start urgent work rather than cover the entire program. About 2 billion yen will fund AI-assisted checks for vulnerabilities in important government systems. Another 4.1 billion yen is allocated for equipment and training to support police investigations into cyberattacks, while 3.8 billion yen will strengthen the Digital Agency's system security, according to Jiji Press reporting.

The package rests on four pillars set out by the Prime Minister's Office: improving basic security using AI, consolidating intelligence and investigative capabilities, responding strategically to attacks, and strengthening assistance for victims. Officials are also discussing faster reporting rules for critical infrastructure, with the aim of reports within several hours of an incident.

One of the most concrete measures is a specialist team to be established by the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry by the end of November. The team, described as a top gun unit of cybersecurity experts, will use AI to simulate attacks, find weaknesses in network systems and counter hackers. A separate public-private cyber rescue team will act as a first responder during attacks, helping organizations with their initial response.

For attacks that threaten national security, the Ministry of Defence, the National Police Agency and the National Cybersecurity Office will deploy joint teams. The government will also enhance its capability to access servers used by hackers as a preemptive measure, and it plans faster discussions on blocking access to websites that display stolen personal information.

The emergency package follows months in which dozens of companies and public bodies suffered unauthorized access and leaks of customer data, prompting unease across Japan. At least 81 incidents have been reported in Japan since July, about a third of all attacks in the past three years, according to Insurance Journal reporting cited in coverage of the announcement.

Japan is far from alone in facing this pressure. Governments around the world are racing to defend critical systems against AI-assisted attacks, and the use of artificial intelligence on both sides of the contest is now the norm. Attackers use AI to find weaknesses faster and to craft more convincing phishing and impersonation attempts, while defenders are turning to the same technology to scan systems and spot intrusions.

The funding decision also reflects a shift in how Japan thinks about cyber defense. Rather than treating each breach as an isolated incident for the affected company to handle, the package puts the state in the role of coordinator, with shared intelligence, joint response teams and direct help for victims. Whether that model works will depend on how quickly the new teams are staffed and how freely companies share information about attacks.

The money has been approved, but several parts of the program remain at the planning or implementation stage. The specialist team is not due until the end of November, legal changes are still under discussion, and no measured reductions in breaches, response times or recovery costs have been presented yet. The package is a down payment on a larger effort, and its success will be judged by whether the next wave of attacks is caught sooner and contained faster.
