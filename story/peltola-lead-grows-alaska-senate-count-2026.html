Peltola's Lead Grows to 5,800 Votes in Alaska's Uncalled Senate Race as Judge Rejects Ballot-List Request

Democrat Mary Peltola extended her lead over Republican Sen. Dan Sullivan in the latest count, while a state judge ruled her campaign cannot get a list of rejected absentee voters mid-count.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 13, 2026 · 5 min readFacebookX

Democrat Mary Peltola's lead over Republican Sen. Dan Sullivan in Alaska's uncalled U.S. Senate race grew to 5,800 votes in the latest count, as the state works through its final batches of ballots ahead of a ranked-choice tabulation next week. The Associated Press, which has not called the race, showed Peltola with 125,157 first-choice votes to Sullivan's 119,357 as of Friday morning, with about 88 percent of the expected vote counted.

The margin widened from 3,975 votes after Wednesday night's batch of results, when the Alaska Division of Elections added 5,618 ballots to the count. After that batch Peltola stood at 48.73 percent to Sullivan's 47.13 percent, according to the Alaska Beacon. The latest update pushed her to about 49 percent to Sullivan's roughly 47 percent, with the Friday deadline for most mailed ballots to arrive now passing.

The race is the last uncalled Senate contest in the country, and it will decide the size of the Democratic majority rather than which party controls the chamber. Democrats have already secured the Senate, and Mississippi's race was called for Democrat Scott Colom earlier this week. Alaska's outcome matters for how large that majority is — and for whether Sullivan, a two-term incumbent, survives the closest race of his career.

Neither candidate has crossed 50 percent of first-choice votes, which means the race will almost certainly be decided by Alaska's ranked-choice system. Under that system, the lowest-ranked candidates are eliminated and their ballots transfer to voters' next choices until someone has a majority. The Division of Elections has targeted November 18 for the ranked-choice tabulation, the same day the last overseas ballots are due, and certification is targeted for November 25.

The count has also produced a court fight. Anchorage Superior Court Judge Thomas Matthews ruled Wednesday that the Division of Elections does not have to produce a list of voters whose absentee ballots were rejected, rejecting a lawsuit filed last week by Peltola's campaign group, Alaskans for Mary. The campaign wanted the names and the reasons for rejection before certification, saying it sought the list "to safeguard an accurate ballot count and ensure that Peltola's supporters are not unlawfully disenfranchised."

"Given that the DOE is currently busy processing ballots from the 2026 general election, it is understandable that the division simply lacks the capacity to generate the report," Matthews wrote in the ruling. "Even if the request were otherwise considered reasonable outside the peak election time of year, it is simply not reasonable or required now," he added. "The timing of the Campaign's request could simply not be worse." The judge also found that the observer reports the campaign wanted are preliminary working documents, not public records, and that the division is not required to build a new report from its voter database in the middle of the count.

The ruling came as the count itself kept moving. Most mailed ballots had to arrive by Friday, November 13, while ballots mailed from overseas can arrive through November 18. The state has said a voter's rejected-ballot determination can change until certification on November 25, after which the division must notify voters whose ballots were rejected. A separate list of how many absentee ballots were rejected and why is typically published weeks after certification. Peltola's campaign did not immediately say whether it planned to appeal the ruling.

One subplot of the race has faded as Peltola's lead has grown. A second candidate on the ballot, Daniel J. Sullivan Jr., a retired schoolteacher from Petersburg who shares the incumbent's name, drew about 4,200 votes. Earlier in the count, Peltola's lead was smaller than that total, which fed a legal challenge by the Trump administration arguing that voter confusion over the similar names could have affected the result. With her lead now at 5,800 votes, it is larger than the namesake candidate's entire vote total.

The governor's race is moving in the same direction for Democrats. In the latest results, Democrat Jonathan Kreiss-Tomkins narrowly crossed 50 percent, though the Associated Press has not declared a winner in that race either. A ballot measure to repeal Alaska's ranked-choice voting system was also still too close to call in the latest results.

What happens next is largely set by the calendar. The Division of Elections will keep processing ballots through the November 18 deadline for overseas arrivals, then run the ranked-choice tabulation if, as expected, no candidate has a first-choice majority. Until that tabulation, Peltola is the leader whose campaign is claiming victory, not the confirmed winner — and Sullivan's campaign has not conceded.

Why it matters: Alaska is the last loose end of the midterms, and it now points the same way as the rest of the map. Peltola's lead has grown with every batch of ballots, and it has now passed the one number that mattered most to the legal challenges around the race — the vote total of the namesake candidate who shared Sullivan's name. The court ruling also closes off the campaign's attempt to audit rejected ballots mid-count, so the ranked-choice tabulation on November 18 is now the decisive moment, and it will settle the size of the Democratic Senate majority.
