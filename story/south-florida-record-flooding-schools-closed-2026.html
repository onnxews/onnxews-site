Record Rain Floods South Florida, Closes Schools and Collapses a Synagogue Roof

A slow-moving storm dumped 15.3 inches of rain on parts of Broward County in a single day, flooding roads and homes, collapsing the roof of a Hollywood synagogue and closing public schools across South Florida on Thursday.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 19, 2026 · 4 min readFacebookX

A slow-moving storm system dumped record rainfall across Broward County on Wednesday, flooding roads and homes, damaging a hospital and causing the roof of a Hollywood synagogue to collapse. Preliminary National Weather Service figures showed that parts of Broward County received 15.3 inches of rain in the 24 hours ending Wednesday, according to Associated Press reporting carried by The Library Estate.

The amount was the highest recorded in Plantation, the Weather Service said. Fort Lauderdale recorded 5.72 inches in a downpour that ended early Wednesday, breaking the city's daily rainfall record of 1.8 inches, set in 1959, according to National Weather Service meteorologist Alejandra Garcia.

The heavy rain caused the roof of Temple Sinai, a synagogue in Hollywood, to collapse. The roof of a local hospital was also damaged, and its hallways flooded. No deaths or injuries had been reported in the flooding or the two building incidents.

Hollywood declared a state of emergency on Wednesday as floodwater affected streets and buildings. Mayor Josh Levy urged motorists to stay off the roads and described the rainfall as “a historically significant rainfall event in the city” Levy said the city received more than 300 calls for service overnight, and flooding forced the closure of portions of the Florida Turnpike and Interstate 95 in Hollywood because of high water and stalled vehicles.

Schools in both Broward and Miami-Dade counties will be closed on Thursday. Miami-Dade County Public Schools will be closed Thursday, including the public schools in Miami Beach and the barrier-island towns, after the School Board voted Wednesday afternoon to close all schools and district operations as a precaution. Broward schools will be closed for a second day.

The Flood Watch runs through 7 p.m. Thursday, and forecasters expect 1 to 2 more inches of rain before it ends, with isolated totals of 3 to 5 inches. The Weather Service says several days of rain have left 3 to 5 inches across much of South Florida's east coast, with pockets of 8 to 15 inches, and that storms are likely Thursday, with a high near 81 and east winds of 12 to 16 mph.

A Wind Advisory for coastal and metro Miami-Dade, Broward and Palm Beach called for east winds of 25 to 30 mph with frequent gusts up to 45 mph, enough to blow loose objects around, bring down tree limbs and cause a few power outages. A Rip Current Statement for Miami-Dade's beaches runs through 7 p.m. Friday.

Garcia attributed the heavy precipitation in part to the El Niño warming cycle, which she said had disrupted Florida's typical dry season, running from October through May. The immediate threat is expected to continue along Florida's Atlantic coast through Friday, and forecasters said up to another 12 inches of rain could fall. The National Hurricane Center is also monitoring a low-pressure system expected to form near the northern Bahamas, which could develop into a subtropical or tropical storm.

In Miami Beach, the city moved to protect residents and vehicles, opening 10 city garages free for residents until 8 a.m. Friday, and the Bass museum, its Creativity Center and the Rotunda will be closed Thursday because of the weather. Miami-Dade schools have not decided about Friday: Superintendent Rafael Villalobos said the district is taking it “on a day-to-day basis”

Why it matters: The flooding matters because it struck in Florida's dry season, when November usually brings cooler, drier air, and because the ground was already saturated when the heaviest rain arrived. El Niño has scrambled the usual pattern, and with more rain forecast through Friday, even an inch or two more could flood streets that held up until now. For South Florida, the week is a reminder that record rain no longer needs a hurricane to shut down schools, roads and hospitals.
