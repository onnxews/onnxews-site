Congress Returns to a Lame-Duck Sprint: Funding Deadline, Hegseth Fight and Epstein Files on the Agenda

Lawmakers are back in Washington on Monday for the final weeks of the 119th Congress, with a December 11 funding deadline looming and Democrats set to take over in January.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 9, 2026 · 3 min readFacebookX

Congress returns to Washington on Monday after weeks away, and the lame-duck session that begins now will be one of the busiest in years. Lawmakers are back after a seven-week recess in the House and a break of more than a month in the Senate, with a short list of must-pass bills and a long list of political fights to squeeze into the final weeks of the year.

The stakes are high because the balance of power is about to change. Democrats swept to comfortable new majorities in the House and Senate in last week's midterm elections, but they will not take control until the 120th Congress is sworn in on January 3, 2027. Until then, Republicans still hold both chambers, and they have a pile of unfinished business to wrap up before the handover.

The most urgent item is keeping the government open. In September, Congress sent President Donald Trump a bill funding the government at current levels until December 11. That deadline is now barely a month away, and lawmakers will have to act again to avoid a shutdown. The likeliest outcome, experts say, is another short-term stopgap measure that carries funding through January or March of next year, pushing the hardest decisions into the new Congress.

Democrats may try to use their new political momentum to extract concessions from Republicans in the funding fight. Representative Rosa DeLauro of Connecticut, the top Democrat on the House Appropriations Committee, has signaled that her party will press its priorities as the deadline approaches. With Republicans holding only narrow margins, even a small group of defectors can stall a bill.

House Speaker Mike Johnson of Louisiana has been operating on razor-thin margins that have allowed Republican rebels to regularly grind legislating to a halt. That dynamic has not changed, and the final weeks of the session are expected to test his ability to move bills through a divided and restless conference.

The most politically fraught fight of the session is likely to be over Pentagon chief Pete Hegseth. In mid-September, Representative Thomas Massie, a consistent critic of President Trump and the Iran war, moved to force a vote on articles of impeachment against Hegseth. Massie is exiting Congress at the end of the year after losing his primary to a Trump-backed challenger, but he has promised to continue his push when Congress returns.

Another item on the agenda is the Epstein Files Transparency Act II, a bipartisan bill that would close loopholes in the first version of the legislation, which Congress passed almost unanimously in 2025. The measure has drawn support from both parties, and its backers want it finished before the session ends.

Lawmakers also need to renew critical defense and farm programs that are due to expire. Those bills are typically routine, but in a session this charged, even routine renewals can become bargaining chips in larger fights over spending and policy.

The calendar is tight. Lawmakers will work through the coming weeks with a short break for Thanksgiving, continuing until just before the holidays in late December. The House returns at noon on Monday and the Senate at 3 p.m., and the work begins immediately.

When the new Congress is sworn in on January 3, incoming committee chairs will gain subpoena power, and the balance of power in Washington will shift decisively. The lame-duck session is the last chance for the outgoing Republican majority to set terms before that handover, and both parties know it.
