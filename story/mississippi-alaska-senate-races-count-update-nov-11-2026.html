Mississippi and Alaska Senate Races Still Uncalled as Final Counts Drag On

Democratic challengers hold razor-thin leads in both states, with certification and ranked-choice deadlines still ahead.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 11, 2026 · 4 min readFacebookX

Two United States Senate races remain uncalled more than a week after Election Day, with Democratic challengers holding razor-thin leads over Republican incumbents in Mississippi and Alaska. The outcomes will decide just how wide the Democrats' new Senate majority will be, though not who holds it: Democrats have already secured 54 seats and will take control of the chamber in January.

In Mississippi, Democrat Scott Colom leads incumbent Republican Senator Cindy Hyde-Smith by 1,138 votes as of Wednesday night, according to the Associated Press, with both candidates holding about 49 percent of the vote and 96 percent of ballots counted. The contest proved far tighter than expected in a state where Hyde-Smith, a staunch ally of President Donald Trump, had been favored to win comfortably.

The count in Mississippi is not finished. Secretary of State Michael Watson said in a statement on Wednesday that roughly 3,600 mail-in ballots were still to be tallied, along with an unknown number of affidavit ballots, and election officials had processed more than 18,000 affidavit ballots by Tuesday evening. Mississippi allows 10 days for such votes to be processed, and mail-in ballots have a window of five business days to be received after the election.

Counties have until Friday, November 13, to submit their certified results to the secretary of state's office, and 76 of the state's 82 counties had submitted uncertified results by November 10, with four having certified. Final results depend in part on whether a candidate tries to change the outcome by challenging certain ballots in court, and election-related lawsuits, if any, will likely be filed after full results are announced on or around Friday.

Mississippi law does not allow for runoffs in federal general elections, no matter how close the final count is, and the state has no automatic recount provision. A judge would have to order a new count based on findings of fraud or irregularities, so the certified count on Friday is likely to stand unless a court intervenes.

In Alaska, Democrat Mary Peltola leads incumbent Republican Senator Dan Sullivan by 637 votes as of Wednesday night, according to the Associated Press, with about 87 percent of ballots counted. More than 245,000 ballots have been counted, with 401 of the state's 403 precincts reporting, but nearly a quarter of the votes remained uncounted earlier in the week, and the race has become one of the most closely watched in the country.

Alaska's outcome will take longer still because of the state's ranked-choice voting system, under which voters number candidates in order of preference and a candidate must win more than 50 percent of the vote. Ranked-choice results will not be published until 15 days after the election, on November 18, after all eligible ballots, including late-arriving absentee ballots, are reviewed and counted.

Legal fights are already under way in Alaska. The Justice Department sued the state's Division of Elections last month, and the Peltola campaign has filed a public records lawsuit, with a hearing expected this week. The disputes add another layer of uncertainty to a count that was already going to stretch into late November.

The stakes are real even though control of the Senate is settled. If Democrats win both races, they would hold 56 seats, and if they win one, 55. That margin matters for the hardest votes ahead: removing a cabinet secretary requires a two-thirds vote in the Senate, and with 56 seats Democrats would need as few as 11 Republican defections, a threshold that shapes the coming fight over Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth.

President Trump has already declared the Alaska race over, though no major decision desk has called either contest. For now, the country waits on two counts moving at the speed of paper ballots: Mississippi's certification deadline on Friday, and Alaska's ranked-choice tabulation on November 18. Until then, the final shape of the new Senate majority remains an open question.
