Young Child Dies in Virginia's First Measles Death of 2026

The Virginia Department of Health said a child aged 0 to 4 who tested positive for measles has died, as the state reports 220 cases this year.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 21, 2026 · 4 min readFacebookX

Virginia has recorded its first measles-associated death of 2026, a young child who tested positive for measles, the state health department announced on Friday. The Virginia Department of Health said the child was between 0 and 4 years old, and it will not provide any further information about the death, including where the child lived or whether the child was vaccinated, to protect the family's privacy.

The death comes as Virginia's measles count keeps climbing. The state has reported 220 measles cases in 2026, including 40 among children aged 0 to 4 and 75 among those aged 5 to 12, according to state health department data as of November 19. Of those cases, 179 were in unvaccinated people, 39 were in people whose vaccination status was unknown, and two were in vaccinated people. The three groups add up to exactly the 220 total cases.

The outbreak is concentrated in central Virginia. Buckingham County has reported 127 cases, the highest in the state, while neighboring Charlotte County has recorded 34 cases. State health officials said there has been a recent increase in measles activity in some areas of Virginia, and they urged residents to be proactive in protecting themselves and their communities from measles and avoiding future tragedies such as this one.

The announcement followed another worrying case just days earlier. Three days before the death was reported, a young child in the same age range tested positive for measles at UVA Health University Medical Center in Charlottesville, but the health department would not say whether the two cases were linked. On the same day that child was reported sick in Charlottesville, the United States lost its measles-free status with the World Health Organization.

Virginia is not alone. Pennsylvania, the hardest-hit state, has reported an eighth measles-related death and confirmed 1,631 measles cases as of November 20. Wisconsin has reported two deaths, and New York state has recorded its first measles-associated death, involving a child. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention has confirmed six measles-associated deaths so far in 2026, though it does not specify the states where the deaths were recorded, and a discrepancy between state and federal tallies arose earlier this year after the CDC began using different mortality data.

Measles is far more dangerous than its reputation as a childhood rash suggests. The virus can cause serious illness, including pneumonia, brain inflammation, complications during pregnancy and death, and it can also damage immune cells that help the body protect against viruses and bacteria it has encountered before, leaving patients vulnerable to other infections. Although anyone can become seriously ill, children younger than five, pregnant people and people with weakened immune systems are at increased risk of complications.

The health department's central message is vaccination. The measles, mumps and rubella vaccine is the best way to prevent and stop the spread of measles, and two doses provide lifetime protection, offering approximately 97 percent effectiveness at preventing the disease. Virginia residents can check their vaccination records through the health department's record request portal or by calling their health care provider.

Anyone who develops fever and rash, especially after exposure to measles or if they are not vaccinated, should contact their health care provider, and should call ahead before visiting a clinic or hospital so steps can be taken to prevent exposing others. The health department's measles dashboard will next be updated on Tuesday, November 24, to reflect the death and the latest case counts.

The death is a grim marker in a year when measles has returned across the country. Virginia's 220 cases in 2026, with more than half of them in children 12 and younger, show how quickly the virus spreads once it finds unvaccinated pockets, and the concentration of cases in Buckingham and Charlotte counties shows how local an outbreak can be even as it becomes a statewide problem.

Why it matters: This death matters because it is the human cost behind the case counts: a child under five, in the age group most at risk, in a state that has now logged 220 measles cases this year, most of them in unvaccinated people. It comes days after the United States lost its measles-free status, a standing it held for a quarter century, and it lands alongside deaths in Pennsylvania, Wisconsin and New York. The pattern is the same in every state — the virus finds the unvaccinated — and the health department's answer is unchanged: two doses of the MMR vaccine, about 97 percent effective, remain the protection that works.
