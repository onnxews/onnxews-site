Fed Officials Open Door to Half-Point December Hike After Hot Inflation Report

Core prices rose 0.3% for a third straight month, hitting the threshold Governor Waller set for a bigger move.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 11, 2026 · 4 min readFacebookX

The odds that the Federal Reserve will raise interest rates by half a percentage point at its December meeting jumped on Tuesday after new data showed prices rising faster than expected and policymakers opened the door to a quicker pace of rate hikes. A quarter-point increase next month is now seen as all but certain, and the open question has shifted to how big the move will be.

The consumer price index rose 0.4 percent in October from September, the Bureau of Labor Statistics said, down from a 0.6 percent gain in September but above the 0.3 percent economists had expected. Core prices, which exclude volatile food and energy costs, rose 0.3 percent for the third month in a row, also above the 0.2 percent consensus. The bureau did not publish most 12-month changes because it collected no data for October 2025 during the government shutdown, though the New York Times estimated the year-over-year increase at about 4 percent, up from 3.7 percent in September.

The core reading landed exactly on a threshold set hours earlier by Federal Reserve Governor Christopher Waller. Speaking at an event in London on Tuesday morning, before the data was released, Waller said he was leaning toward supporting a 25-basis-point increase at the December meeting even if October inflation improved, but that if core consumer prices rose 0.3 percent or more, a stronger policy move may be needed and a 50-basis-point hike should be considered. The report met his condition precisely.

Traders reacted immediately. Futures put the chance of a quarter-point hike in December at about 91 percent and a half-point move at roughly 9 percent on Tuesday, according to the CME FedWatch tool cited by CNN, and other readings put the half-point odds as high as 13.6 percent, up from around zero before the report. By Wednesday in Asia, futures fully priced in three quarter-point increases by the middle of next year, according to Reuters, and Goldman Sachs began forecasting another quarter-point hike in March after previously expecting the hiking cycle to end in December.

Waller was not the only official signaling a tougher stance. Dallas Fed President Lorie Logan said it remains clear that the Federal Open Market Committee needs to implement at least two more 25-basis-point rate increases, and that she feels the Fed needs to raise rates at least half a percentage point before pausing to take stock. Vice Chair for Supervision Michelle Bowman said the benefit of further hikes has to be weighed against the costs for housing and other rate-sensitive sectors.

The bond market sold off sharply on the news. The yield on the benchmark 10-year Treasury touched 5.03 percent on Tuesday as investors contemplated the prospect of a 50-basis-point hike, and the 2-year yield jumped 17 basis points, its biggest one-day move since April 2025. Higher yields feed directly into borrowing costs across the economy, from mortgages to corporate loans, and they were the main reason stocks fell on Tuesday.

The December 8 to 9 meeting will be the last of the year for the committee led by Chairman Kevin Warsh, and it comes with a new Summary of Economic Projections. The Fed raised its target range on September 16, its first increase in three years and two months, since July 2023, and held rates steady at its October 27 to 28 meeting on a 10 to 2 vote, with two members wanting another hike. The current target range is 3.75 to 4 percent.

The details of the inflation report show the pressure spreading. Food prices rose 0.5 percent over the month, with food at home up 0.4 percent and food away from home up 0.5 percent, while energy prices rose 0.7 percent, a sharp deceleration from September's 5.2 percent. The core personal consumption expenditures price index, the inflation gauge the Fed weighs most heavily, was estimated to have risen 3.3 percent in October, and the official October reading is due on November 25.

For households, the report prolongs a more than five-year stretch of inflation running above the Fed's 2 percent target, and it complicates the outlook for anyone waiting for borrowing costs to fall. The Fed's own projections in September put the median year-end rate at 4.125 percent, and if Waller's bigger-step comments materialize, total tightening this year would exceed that forecast. The decision lands on December 9, and the next six weeks of data will decide whether the hike is a quarter point or a half.
