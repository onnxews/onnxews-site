Car Bomb in Moscow Wounds Ex-Aide to Ukraine's Former Pro-Russian Premier

Sergei Zavorotniy, a former spokesman for exiled Ukrainian prime minister Mykola Azarov, was hospitalized after the car he was driving exploded in Moscow's Chertanovo district, a day after a car bomb killed a serviceman in St. Petersburg.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 13, 2026 · 4 min readFacebookX

A car bomb wounded a former aide to Ukraine's ousted pro-Russian prime minister in Moscow on Friday, the second car bombing in Russia in two days, in the latest attack on a figure linked to the political order that was overthrown in Kyiv in 2014. The target survived, but the attack underscored how the war in Ukraine continues to reach into the Russian capital.

The explosion took place on Friday in the Chertanovo district of Moscow, where a car driven by Sergei Zavorotniy blew up, according to Reuters. Zavorotniy was hospitalized. Russian media reported that he lost his left arm and was being treated for a concussion, rib fractures and other injuries. Russia's Investigative Committee opened an investigation into the bombing.

Zavorotniy was a onetime spokesman and top aide to Mykola Azarov, who served as Ukraine's prime minister from 2010 to 2014 and has been living in Russia since fleeing the country in 2014. Azarov told the state news agency TASS that he had no information about the bombing except that Zavorotniy had survived. The attack was reported by Ren TV and the 112 Telegram channel, which said the car he was driving exploded.

The bombing came a day after a Russian serviceman was killed by a car bomb in St. Petersburg, in the city's Pushkinsky District, on Thursday. Moscow has blamed Ukraine for previous assassinations of officials and military figures inside Russia, and there was no immediate comment from Kyiv on Friday's bombing. Ukraine has claimed responsibility for some past attacks and denied involvement in others.

Azarov and the president he served, Viktor Yanukovych, fled Ukraine after the Maidan protests of 2014, in which more than 100 demonstrators were killed. Yanukovych was later sentenced in absentia to 13 years in prison for treason in 2019, and Kyiv has sanctioned both men. Zavorotniy, who worked as a Soviet-era foreign correspondent in Latin America for the newspaper Komsomolskaya Pravda, wrote a doctoral thesis on Cuba and met the novelist Gabriel Garcia Marquez before later working as a political commentator who criticized the Ukrainian security establishment.

The attack fits a pattern that has been building since Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022. A series of assassinations and bombings inside Russia has targeted military officers, officials and public figures associated with the war, and Moscow has repeatedly blamed Ukrainian intelligence. Kyiv has at times claimed responsibility for past attacks and at other times has said nothing.

For now, the investigation is in its early stages, and the motive has not been established. But the timing, a day after the St. Petersburg bombing, and the target, a figure from the old pro-Russian government in Kyiv, suggest that the campaign of attacks inside Russia is continuing.

Whether Friday's bombing was the work of Ukrainian intelligence, of Russian domestic opponents of the war, or of someone else entirely, it is a reminder that the war's front line is not only in eastern Ukraine but also on the streets of Moscow and St. Petersburg. Neither bombing has been publicly claimed, and both remain under investigation.

Why it matters: The bombing matters because it shows that the war in Ukraine is being fought inside Russia as well as on the battlefield, and because its targets are increasingly political rather than purely military. Zavorotniy was not a general or a weapons designer but a former spokesman for a government that fled Kyiv twelve years ago — a symbol of the pro-Russian order that the Maidan protests overthrew. Hitting him, a day after a serviceman was killed in St. Petersburg, sends a message that no one associated with that order, or with the war, is safe in Moscow. For Russia, the attacks are a security failure that undermines the claim that the war is contained; for Ukraine, they are a deniable pressure campaign whose authorship is deliberately left unclear.
