COP31 Enters Final Week With Fossil Fuel Divide Unresolved, Climate Chief Warns

UN climate chief Simon Stiell urged negotiators in Antalya to move swiftly to the hardest issues and avoid tactical delays, as big fossil fuel producers resist any deal with firm new targets.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 15, 2026 · 5 min readFacebookX

The UN's top climate official urged negotiators at the COP31 conference in Turkey on Saturday to move swiftly towards a deal and "avoid tactical delays" as the summit heads into its second and final week. Simon Stiell, executive secretary of UN Climate Change, told delegates in Antalya that agreement on every word remained tough but vital work, and that no country could succeed alone.

"Each country has a view. Every country has a voice. Getting agreement on every word, every sentence is tough but vital work," Stiell said. "There is no collective success without compromise from every party." He added: "As we head into the second week, I urge parties to move swiftly to the hardest issues and to avoid tactical delays." The summit, which opened on November 9, is due to close on Friday, November 20.

The central divide is over fossil fuels. Big fossil fuel producers and consumers at the talks are resisting any deal that would specify firm new targets or a shift away from oil, gas and coal, observers said, posing a challenge for summit host Turkey and lead negotiator Australia to land the kind of ambitious agreement climate advocates say is needed to address global warming.

A UN report last month found the world is already on course to exceed the 1.5 degrees Celsius warming threshold, the limit scientists say should prevent the worst effects of climate change. The finding has framed the talks in Antalya, where negotiators are wrestling with how to respond to the prospect of temperatures overshooting 1.5 degrees, and how to bring them back down.

On Friday, the COP31 Presidency published a "summary of reflections" on a proposed cover decision, the broad political agreement that would wrap up the summit. The document gathers the issues countries have said they want included, from advancing the transition away from fossil fuels to recognising the role of forests and oceans and responding to the prospect of temperatures overshooting 1.5 degrees. None of it is agreed yet. Countries are expected to submit text proposals over the weekend, with a proper draft emerging early next week, according to Australia's COP31 President of Negotiations, Chris Bowen.

The list also covers methane, electrification, adaptation finance, loss and damage, the Brazil-led roadmaps on the fossil-fuel transition and deforestation, and even a reference to a third commitment period of the Kyoto Protocol. Climate analysts described it as a catalogue of every country's wishes rather than a negotiated text. "There's all kinds of signals being sent here about what could be in the cover decision," Alden Meyer, an expert at climate think tank E3G, told AFP. "The big question is what can survive that test of opposition by different groups of countries."

Host Turkey is pushing a global electrification plan to the centre of any eventual deal, arguing that faster electrification will cut emissions sooner by replacing fossil-fuel cars, boilers, furnaces and other equipment with electric alternatives. The proposal aims to raise electricity's share of global energy use to 35 percent by 2035, up from about 23 percent today. But the plan does not require the extra electricity to come from renewable sources, a gap that has disappointed delegations and observers who want countries to map out their move away from fossil fuels more clearly.

The talks are also short on money. Climate finance pledges from developed countries fell sharply in the first week, running about 70 percent below last year's level, according to an analysis published as the opening week ended. One proposal in the reflections text would triple adaptation finance, which helps developing countries strengthen infrastructure and prepare for climate impacts, but the European Union has warned there is little political appetite for fresh funding commitments at this summit.

The week's earlier announcements show what the summit can still deliver when it moves. Fourteen countries signed the Antalya Statement to grow carbon markets, and more than $480 million was pledged to cut methane emissions. The United States, the world's biggest oil consumer, stayed away for a second straight year under President Donald Trump, who has dismissed global warming as a hoax and backed continued fossil-fuel use.

The fossil-fuel question has split climate summits before. A commitment to move away from fossil fuels across energy systems was agreed at COP28 in Dubai in 2023, but explicit references to that transition were absent from the final decisions at COP29 in Azerbaijan and COP30 in Brazil, with oil-producing countries accused of resisting the language. Major oil producers such as Saudi Arabia and Russia have traditionally resisted language that constrains fossil fuels, while large developing economies such as India have opposed measures they say could limit their development and energy choices. Stiell described the engagement so far as constructive, but added: "We know where those natural tensions lie."

Why it matters: The second week of COP31 matters because it is when the summit either produces a deal or dissolves into another list of unkept wishes. The fossil-fuel divide has blocked every recent summit from restating the transition away from oil, gas and coal, and the draft cover decision now on the table is a catalogue of what countries want rather than what they have agreed. With the United States absent and climate finance falling, the presidency's test is whether it can turn that wish list into a text with real timelines before the summit closes on Friday.
