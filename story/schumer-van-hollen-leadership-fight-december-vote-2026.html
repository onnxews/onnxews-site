Schumer Snaps at Reporters as Van Hollen Challenge Roils Senate Democrats

With the leadership vote set for December 1, the majority leader insists he has the votes while his challenger claims growing support in a secret-ballot contest.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 18, 2026 · 5 min readFacebookX

Senate Democrats will pick their leader in a secret ballot on December 1, and the contest between Majority Leader Chuck Schumer and Sen. Chris Van Hollen is turning into the first real test of the party's new majority. With Democrats now holding 56 seats after Mary Peltola's win in Alaska, a candidate needs 29 votes to win the leadership election, one more than the 28 that would have been required before Peltola's win.

Schumer, who has led Senate Democrats since 2017, snapped at reporters at his weekly press conference on Tuesday. "Look, I'm confident I'll be the next leader, I have the votes, and we're all going to roll up our sleeves, and we are going to focus on lowering costs, ending corruption," Schumer said. Asked by Fox News correspondent Chad Pergram whether Van Hollen's bid reflects on his leadership, Schumer repeated himself: "We are all united together. I am confident I have the votes, and we're united together,"

Van Hollen, 67, announced his challenge on Thursday, November 12, arguing that the party needs new leadership after the midterm wave. He thinks he has more support from his colleagues than is publicly known and attributes their silence to a fear of angering Schumer. "What you've got obviously is somebody who has the power of incumbency and so there is an element of concern about political retaliation," Van Hollen told The Washington Sun. "So it's hard to ask people to be public when they're worried that it will lead to bad consequences." He also disputes Schumer's whip list: "There was some article that came out that listed a bunch of people as having committed to Schumer, where I know for a fact some have said they're voting for me, and others I know are undecided on that list," Van Hollen said Monday.

The public count tells a different story than either camp's private claims. Only four senators have publicly endorsed Van Hollen: Sen. Bernie Sanders of Vermont, Sen. Jeff Merkley of Oregon, and Senators-elect Abdul El-Sayed of Michigan and Peggy Flanagan of Minnesota. Around 15 senators have publicly backed Schumer, far short of the roughly 30 votes he needs.

Senators did not discuss the race at their closed-door caucus lunch on Tuesday, according to Punchbowl News' Laura Weiss, who cited multiple Senate Democrats. That silence is itself a signal: in a secret ballot, senators can avoid taking a public position while the two camps count votes behind closed doors. "We just had the single greatest results in a midterm election in a generation, and Leader Schumer was central to a lot of the recruitment and fundraising," Sen. Chris Coons of Delaware told The Washington Sun, describing Van Hollen's chances of becoming leader as "remote."

Sen. Mazie Hirono was the only Democrat to newly announce support for Schumer as senators returned to Washington on Monday. Sen. Tim Kaine offered a mixed verdict: "No one who was not on the ballot worked harder for this victory than Chuck Schumer. Chuck Schumer also happens to know Donald Trump better than anybody else in Congress," Kaine said, though he added that "the voters are also overwhelmingly demanding change." Sen. Cory Booker declined to say how he will vote, telling reporters, "It's a secret ballot."

Schumer's allies have discussed moving the vote earlier, before the Thanksgiving recess, a step that would require waiving caucus rules in a vote that would itself be held by secret ballot. "I haven't heard that seriously proposed," Sen. Jon Ossoff said. "But I would oppose expediting before we've had a full process." Roll Call reported on Tuesday that Democrats rejected efforts to bring the election forward.

Van Hollen is running as the change candidate, backing more aggressive tactics to end the Iran war, cutting off U.S. aid to Israel, ending the filibuster, and Medicare for All. He has also criticised Schumer for intervening in contested primaries and for opposing former President Barack Obama's Iran nuclear deal. It is the first contested Senate Democratic leadership election since 1994, and the winner will lead a 56-seat majority into a Congress where the party's ambitions, and its divisions, will be on full display.

Why it matters: The leadership fight is the first test of what the midterm wave actually means for the Democratic Party. Schumer is the architect of the 56-seat majority, and his allies argue the results validate his leadership, but the fact that a challenge is happening at all, and that the vote is secret, means the public whip counts are unreliable and the outcome is genuinely uncertain. The secret ballot is the key detail: senators who will not criticise Schumer in public can vote against him in private, which is exactly what Van Hollen is counting on. Whoever wins on December 1 will set the party's course on the filibuster, the Iran war, and the 2028 agenda, so this is a contest about the party's direction, not just its leader.
