Spain's Right Holds Lead in Final Polls Before November 29 Vote, but Needs Vox for a Majority

Final polls before Spain's November 29 snap election put the conservative PP ahead of the Socialists, but short of a majority — leaving the far-right Vox as the likely kingmaker.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 21, 2026 · 6 min readFacebookX

Spaniards head to the polls on Sunday, November 29, to vote in the country’s sixth general election in 11 years, and the final polls before a pre-election blackout point to the same outcome they have pointed to all campaign: the conservative People’s Party finishing first, but needing the far-right Vox party to govern. The snap election could end eight years of socialist-led rule and bring a far-right party into national government for the first time since Spain’s return to democracy after the death of General Franco.

According to an average of 16 polls compiled by the Spanish news site Demócrata as of November 16, the PP leads on 31.4 percent, followed by Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez’s Socialists (PSOE) on 27.0 percent, Vox on 19.0 percent and the left-wing Frente Amplio alliance on 6.2 percent. The exception is the state-run polling institute, the CIS, which puts the Socialists ahead. Four polls published in the same week tell a consistent story with different margins: a SocioMétrica poll for El Español projected around 200 seats in the 350-seat lower house for the PP and Vox combined, while a 40dB poll for El País showed the gap between the PP and the Socialists narrowing to 2 percentage points.

Spain’s Congress has 350 seats, meaning any party or coalition needs at least 176 seats to secure an outright majority. According to the polls, the PP could win about 130 seats, the PSOE about 110 and Vox about 63. A Sigma Dos poll for El Mundo pointed to the Socialists recovering some ground and winning 105 seats, but still far behind the 199 seats projected for the PP and Vox together. An IMOP poll for El Confidencial focused on vote transfers rather than seat projections, pointing to a drop in voter loyalty for the Socialists and a rise for the PP, largely at the expense of Vox supporters. Between them, the Broad Front and Podemos are forecast to win about 13 seats, leaving them and the PSOE far short of a majority.

Sánchez, Spain’s socialist prime minister, called the snap election on October 5 after the conservative People’s Party, the far-right Vox party and the right-wing, pro-independence Catalan party Junts voted down emergency measures the government had put together to tackle a housing crisis. The election was called after parliament rejected the minority government’s two emergency housing decrees on October 2, bringing forward a vote that had originally been scheduled for 2027. The official campaign began on Friday, November 13, and ends on November 27, with a ban on publishing opinion polls from November 24 to 28.

The campaign has been dominated by housing, immigration and corruption, and by the aftermath of the deadly train crash near Urda on October 28, which killed 69 people and led to the resignation of Transport Minister Óscar Puente. Most parties except Vox temporarily suspended their campaigns after the disaster. PP leader Alberto Núñez Feijóo has promised “a return to decency as a basic principle” if his party wins on November 29, while Sánchez has projected a message of hope and joy, counting on mobilising left-wing voters and on his alliances with regional parties. Vox leader Santiago Abascal has said his party stands for “putting nationals first, deregulation, tax relief, and the return of those who should not be in” Spain.

Vox’s rise is sharpest among the young. Among 18- to 24-year-olds, Vox is polling at around 26 percent — level with the PP and ahead of the PSOE on 22 percent. Among 25- to 34-year-olds, Vox is close to 30 percent and leads the field. That strength among younger voters is one of the most striking features of the campaign, and it helps explain why the combined right is projected to win around 195 seats in the 350-member Congress, comfortably above the 176 required for an absolute majority.

A coalition deal between the PP and Vox would signal the return of the far right to government in Spain for the first time in the country’s post-dictatorship democratic history. It would also mark a significant departure for a country where the radical right has remained outside national executive power since the end of Franco’s dictatorship. The prospect has given Sánchez a chance to rally the left, warning voters against a Vox role in government, but the polls suggest that even his ability to turn weaknesses into strengths may not be enough to prevent a shift to the right. Barring a far better than expected performance from the PSOE, the PP is set to win, ushering in the prospect of a deal with Vox.

The election will also decide the shape of Spain’s upper chamber, with 209 of 267 Senate seats up for election alongside all 350 Congress seats. A total of 38,728,356 voters are registered. For the first time since 1977, a major left-wing alliance including the PSOE — the Senate of Progress — was formed for the upper chamber, prompting the PP and Vox to assess potential forms of electoral cooperation. Together with Vox, Feijóo has pledged to carry out sweeping right-wing reforms, including on social issues and constitutional reform. Whatever the result on November 29, the vote will redraw Spain’s political map — and, if the polls are right, end the Sánchez era.

Why it matters: Spain has had six general elections in 11 years, but this one is different: it is the first in which the far right is not a protest vote but a likely governing partner. The arithmetic is what matters — the PP cannot reach 176 seats alone in any poll, so a Vox deal is not a choice but a necessity for a right-wing government, and Vox knows it. That leverage is why Abascal’s demands on immigration, deregulation and tax will shape the next government even if the PP wins the most votes. For Sánchez, who has survived eight years by assembling improbable coalitions, the question is whether the left can still be mobilised in eight days — or whether Spain joins the growing list of European countries where the nationalist right has moved from the margins into power.
