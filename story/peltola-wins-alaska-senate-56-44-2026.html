Peltola Wins Alaska Senate Race, Giving Democrats a 56-44 Majority

The Associated Press called the race Tuesday night, ending a two-week wait and capping a nine-seat Democratic flip in the Senate.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 18, 2026 · 4 min readFacebookX

Democrat Mary Peltola defeated Republican Sen. Dan Sullivan in Alaska's U.S. Senate race, The Associated Press called on Tuesday evening, November 17. Peltola won 144,302 votes, or 50.12%, to Sullivan's 131,844 votes, or 45.80%, a margin of 12,458 votes with all 401 of the state's 401 precincts reporting. The AP estimated 99% of the vote had been counted as of Tuesday night, and 290,003 ballots were cast out of 605,433 registered voters, a turnout of 47.90%.

The rest of the field finished well behind. Republican Gerald L. Heikes took 6,105 votes, or 2.12%, while Daniel J. Sullivan Jr., an undeclared candidate, took 4,760 votes, or 1.65%. Write-in candidates combined for 885 votes, or 0.31%. Total votes cast in the race were 287,896, and the figures add up: 144,302 plus 131,844 plus 6,105 plus 4,760 plus 885 equals 287,896, and Peltola's margin over Sullivan is exactly 12,458 votes.

Clearing 50% of first-choice votes matters because of Alaska's ranked-choice voting system. Under the state's rules, a candidate who wins more than half of first-choice votes wins outright, with no elimination or tabulation rounds needed. The ranked-choice tabulation that had been scheduled for Wednesday, November 18, is no longer necessary.

Peltola's victory is historic on several fronts. She is the first Alaska Native elected to the U.S. Senate, and the first Democrat to win an Alaska Senate race since 2008. She will be just the fifth Democrat since statehood to represent Alaska in the Senate. The most recent was Mark Begich, whom Sullivan defeated in 2014. Peltola previously held Alaska's lone House seat, making her the first Alaska Native to serve in Congress, before losing her 2024 reelection bid to Rep. Nick Begich.

The result completes a sweeping Democratic night in the Senate. Democrats flipped nine Senate seats in the midterms, the most since 1958, according to the AP. Republicans entered the midterms with a 53-47 majority, and Democrats now emerge with a 56-44 margin, a result that reshapes the balance of power in Washington for the next two years.

The final stretch of the race was shadowed by controversy. Less than a week before the November 3 election, The New York Times first reported allegations that Peltola's campaign had helped recruit retired teacher Dan J. Sullivan to run in order to siphon votes from the incumbent. Peltola denied any involvement, saying, "I knew nothing about any other candidates on the ballot except for me and the incumbent," Dan J. Sullivan also denied any contact with Peltola's campaign or running at Democrats' behest.

The Justice Department filed a lawsuit that Trump said he ordered, seeking to block certification of the results if the incumbent lost, pending an investigation into possible voter confusion. A federal judge dismissed the suit, and the Justice Department appealed. The department also confirmed that a criminal investigation exists, though Alaska's U.S. attorney would not say what potential crimes were being looked into.

Peltola declared victory on Saturday, November 14, but Sullivan had not conceded as of Monday, November 16. The AP reported that messages seeking comment were left with both campaigns on Tuesday night. State certification of the results is targeted for November 25, and the new Senate will be seated on January 3, 2027.

Alaska also elected a Democrat as governor for the first time since 1998. Former state lawmaker Jonathan Kreiss-Tomkins defeated three Republicans, including one endorsed by Trump, winning 145,268 votes, or 51.07%, according to the Alaska Division of Elections. Republican Wilson/Shower finished second with 79,269 votes, or 27.87%. In the Senate primary, Peltola had led with 49.5% to the incumbent's 41.4%, with Dan J. Sullivan in third at about 2.5%. Campaign spending in the Senate race topped $140 million overall, according to the Wall Street Journal.

Why it matters: A 56-44 majority is bigger than either party expected when the midterms began, and it changes the arithmetic of the next Congress in concrete ways: the new threshold for winning the Democratic leadership election is 29 votes, not 28, and the party has a cushion it can spend on the filibuster debate and on confirmations. Peltola's win also matters beyond the numbers. The first Alaska Native in the Senate, elected on a platform built around cost of living and affordability, is the clearest symbol of a midterm wave that reached even the reddest corners of the map. The open question is the Justice Department's criminal investigation, which could hang over her first months in office even though a federal judge has already thrown out the related lawsuit.
