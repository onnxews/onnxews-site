Much Ado About Nothing Opens on Broadway as a Confetti-Soaked Dance Party

Jamie Lloyd's production starring Tom Hiddleston and Hayley Atwell opened at the Winter Garden Theatre on November 19, turning Shakespeare's comedy into a pink, pop-fueled rave that mostly won over the critics.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 20, 2026 · 5 min readFacebookX

Jamie Lloyd's production of William Shakespeare's Much Ado About Nothing officially opened on Broadway on Thursday, November 19, at the Winter Garden Theatre, starring Tom Hiddleston as Benedick and Hayley Atwell as Beatrice. The production, which arrives on Broadway following its run in London's West End, turns the 16th-century comedy into a deafening dance club, and the critics have mostly been swept along.

The show is playing a strictly limited ten-week engagement through Sunday, January 10, 2027, produced by The Jamie Lloyd Company. It marks Atwell's Broadway debut, while Hiddleston, a Tony Award nominee and Olivier Award winner, returns to the New York stage as the bickering Benedick opposite Atwell's Beatrice, in Shakespeare's romantic comedy about love, deception and mistaken identities.

Lloyd's staging reimagines Messina as a nightclub. The set by Soutra Gilmour consists of almost nothing else but a table, a set of simple molded-plastic chairs and a massive quantity of pink tissue confetti, which coats the floor and often rains from the ceiling. A giant red Valentine balloon hovers toward the back of the playing space, descending on occasion to nearly swallow someone whole. The soundtrack is 1980s and 1990s pop, much of it sung by Mason Alexander Park as Margaret, and the evening ends with the cast belting out a euphoric When Love Takes Over by David Guetta and Kelly Rowland.

The party starts before the play does. Theatergoers are greeted by ushers who dance them to their seats, jumping in place to a disco-heavy playlist before leading the crowd in the wave. Dance hits regularly blare before the show and between scenes, with needle drops ranging from the Beastie Boys and C and C Music Factory to Taylor Dayne, Missy Elliott and the Backstreet Boys. The production's creative team includes lighting designer Jon Clark, sound designers and composers Ben and Max Ringham, and choreographer Fabian Aloise.

The cast also features Mara Huf as Hero, James Phoon as Claudio, Gerald Kyd as Don Pedro, Forbes Masson as Leonato, Phillip Olagoke as Friar Francis, Mika Onyx Johnson as Borachio and Tim Steed as Don John. The story follows two sets of lovers: Claudio and Hero, whose race to the altar is hampered by rumors of infidelity, and Beatrice and Benedick, who sling snark-tipped arrows at each other while cynically insisting they will never fall prey to romance.

The reviews have been warm, with the praise for the two leads coming close to unanimous. The New York Times' Helen Shaw wrote that the confetti is, metaphorically, love, showering quietly down like grace, and that when you get home afterward, you will pluck a piece of it out of your collar. Time Out New York gave the production four out of five stars, the New York Post gave it four out of four, and Entertainment Weekly graded it B plus. BroadwayWorld's review roundup put the average rating at 81.8 percent across the critics who weighed in.

Not every critic was won over. Vulture's Sara Holdren was the sharpest dissenter, arguing that Lloyd strips the play of its grounding so the characters become little more than outfits, and that the Claudio and Hero plot is left impossible to play. TheWrap enjoyed the first act far more than the second, saying Lloyd runs out of ideas to keep audiences diverted after intermission. USA TODAY called the show equal parts exhausting and exhilarating, and said that somewhere between the DJ airhorns and phallic ballcaps, Shakespeare's scintillating prose and artful wordplay get muddled, even as Hiddleston and Atwell fare spectacularly.

Much of the production's appeal rests on the chemistry between its stars, who are best known as Loki and Peggy Carter in the long-running Marvel franchise. USA TODAY's Patrick Ryan wrote that Hiddleston is a supernova talent gleaming with infinite charm and sex appeal, gamely breakdancing and flashing his abs while crooning the Backstreet Boys, and that Atwell is a fiery sparring partner as Beatrice, dryly swatting back witticisms just as splendidly as she drops into the character's softer, more vulnerable moments. Their longtime friendship shows in the rapid-fire banter that makes Much Ado soar.

Lloyd is no stranger to polarizing Broadway, having beguiled and maddened audiences with stripped-back, high-concept revivals of Sunset Boulevard, A Doll's House and Waiting for Godot. This production is his first go at pure joy, and it is the opening show of his season-long Broadway residency at the Winter Garden Theatre. Whether the confetti settles into a hit or a curiosity, the opening night made one thing clear: this Much Ado is the most fun you will have on Broadway this season, according to TheaterMania's review quoted in BroadwayWorld's roundup, and the party is only just getting started.

Why it matters: This opening matters because it tests how far a director can push Shakespeare before the play disappears under the concept. Lloyd has built his Broadway reputation on stripping classics down to a single bold idea, and here the idea is pure celebration: confetti, pop hits and two movie stars having the time of their lives. The critics who loved it loved the joy; the ones who resisted it missed the darker edges Lloyd cut away. That split is the story of Lloyd's career in miniature. For Broadway, the commercial bet is clear: Hiddleston and Atwell are the draw, and a ten-week limited run through January 10 is short enough to keep demand high. If the word of mouth holds, this could be the season's hottest ticket; if the second act sags for audiences the way it did for some critics, the confetti may settle faster than expected.
