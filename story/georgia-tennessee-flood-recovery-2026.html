Georgia-Tennessee Border Towns Face Long Recovery After Record Flood

McCaysville, Georgia, and Copperhill, Tennessee, are cleaning up after a once-in-a-millennium flood that set a river record, knocked out a town's water system and forced more than 140 rescues.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 8, 2026 · 4 min readFacebookX

Towns along the Georgia-Tennessee border are in recovery mode after record flooding devastated the area on Thursday, with the Toccoa River surging to a crest never seen before and the twin communities of McCaysville, Georgia, and Copperhill, Tennessee, left to pick up the pieces. Flash flood emergencies were declared across Polk County, Tennessee, and Fannin County, Georgia, early Thursday morning as torrential rain overwhelmed rivers and creeks in the mountain watershed.

The scale of the rain was extraordinary. Between five and eight inches fell across much of the region in just 12 hours, with some mountain areas receiving 8 to 11 inches overnight. The Toccoa River crested at a record 18.05 feet, passing the previous record of 17.75 feet set in 1990, before flowing across the state line to become the Ocoee River in Tennessee.

Rescue crews pulled more than 140 people from the floodwaters across the two counties, including at least 100 across Fannin and Gilmer counties in Georgia, along with dozens of pets. Swift-water teams from Cherokee, Union and Murray counties, the Tri-State Mutual Aid Group and the Georgia Department of Natural Resources joined local crews in the rescues. At least one person died: a driver whose vehicle was swept away by floodwaters along a road in Murray County, according to the state emergency management director.

Georgia Governor Brian Kemp declared a state of emergency on Thursday for Fannin, Gilmer, Murray and Union counties, unlocking additional state resources for the affected communities. Gilmer County also declared its own local emergency after flooding washed out roads and culverts and left some residents temporarily cut off. In Tennessee, Polk County declared a state of emergency after at least 68 people were rescued there.

In McCaysville, the most pressing problem is the town's damaged water system. City water department employees are working to restore service so families and businesses can begin cleaning and rebuilding, but officials have not given a timeline for full restoration. While the system remains disrupted, the Georgia Emergency Management and Homeland Security Agency has sent bottled water into Fannin County, and two distribution centers are operating in McCaysville, offering food, clothing and cleaning supplies to anyone affected.

Roads remain a major obstacle. Several streets in McCaysville are still closed to through traffic, including Toccoa Avenue and parts of Blue Ridge Drive, and Highway 68 through Copperhill is shut. Tennessee State Route 68 reopened on Friday morning before closing again, complicating access for recovery crews. Copperhill is dealing with a separate water-main break and a boil-water advisory, and McCaysville has a curfew in effect from 6 p.m. to 6 a.m. in its downtown area.

The American Red Cross has opened a shelter at the Fannin County Recreation Center in Blue Ridge for people forced from their homes. Georgia emergency management officials said they have fulfilled 18 resource requests connected to the disaster, including helicopters, boats, drones, debris-clearing personnel and drinking water. State damage assessment teams are still working through the affected communities, so the full scope of the destruction is not yet known.

For McCaysville and Copperhill, the blow is especially cruel because it is the second disastrous flood in three months. The town flooded badly in early August as well, and McCaysville had only just reopened its renovated police building last week. Police Chief Michael Otis Earley said his department lost virtually everything in the latest flood and that recovering again will not be easy.

Downstream, the Tennessee Valley Authority continued inspecting dams and managing reservoir operations as high water moved through the system. Most of the floodwater had receded by Friday, state emergency management director Josh Lamb said, but the cleanup will take far longer than the water took to rise. City officials in McCaysville said on Saturday that residents cleaning out flooded properties should place damaged belongings and debris along streets or sidewalks for collection.

The flooding is a reminder of how quickly mountain rivers can turn deadly when a slow-moving storm parks over a steep watershed. The rain fell hardest overnight, when many residents were asleep, and the rivers rose so fast that some people were rescued from rooftops and second floors. With the water gone, the towns now face the slower work of restoring water service, reopening roads and rebuilding homes and businesses, much of it in communities that were still recovering from the last flood.
