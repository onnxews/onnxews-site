The Fantasticks Finally Reaches Broadway, 66 Years After It Opened Off-Broadway

The world's longest-running musical opened at the Hayes Theater on Monday with a reimagined romance: boy meets boy.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 17, 2026 · 4 min readFacebookX

The Fantasticks has finally reached Broadway. The world's longest-running musical opened on Monday, November 16, at the Hayes Theater in New York, 66 years after it first opened Off-Broadway — and with a reimagined romance at its heart. The Second Stage production, directed and choreographed by Tony winner Christopher Gattelli, runs for a limited 15-week engagement through February 7, 2027.

The Broadway debut is a piece of theatre history in itself. The Fantasticks premiered Off-Broadway on May 3, 1960, at the Sullivan Street Playhouse, and the original production ran for 42 years and 17,162 performances before closing on January 13, 2002. A revival in 2006 ran until 2017, giving the show a combined 53 years Off-Broadway. It is the longest-running musical in the world, and until Monday it had never played Broadway at all.

The production is a reimagining, not a museum piece. The show's original book writer and lyricist, Tom Jones, who died in 2023 at the age of 95, reworked the story before his death, and the most significant change is at its centre: the boy-meets-girl romance is now boy-meets-boy. Matt, played by Maxim Chlumecky, 20, is a bookish young man; Lewis, played by Nathan Levy, 18, is starry-eyed. Their mothers, Mildred and Bessie Mae, played by Leslie Rodriguez Kritzer and Ruthie Ann Miles, fake a feud to bring the boys together, while Zane Phillips plays El Gallo, the mysterious narrator. Kevin Del Aguila and David Patrick Kelly play the actors, and Ahmad Simmons plays the wall.

The songs are the ones audiences have known for decades: "Try to Remember," "Soon It's Gonna Rain," "They Were You" and "Much More." The show runs two hours and 15 minutes including an intermission, and tickets start at $65. The production is playing the Hayes Theater at 240 W. 44th Street, one of Broadway's smallest houses, which suits a show that was always intimate rather than spectacular.

The reviews have been mixed to warm. USA Today called the production "the most heart-tugging show on Broadway" and "utterly enchanting." The New York Theatre Guide praised the singing but noted that the second act meanders and that "Much More" does not quite fit the reimagined romance. Audiences have been kinder than some critics: the show holds an 82 percent approval rating on Show-Score, and a review roundup put the average critical score at 77.6 percent.

The reimagining raises a question that has followed the show for years: can a musical written in 1960 speak to an audience in 2026? Jones, who wrote the book and lyrics with composer Harvey Schmidt — who died in 2018 at 88 — clearly believed it could, and his decision to change the central romance was a deliberate attempt to make the story feel current rather than preserved. The production's defenders say the change works because the show was always about young love and parental scheming, not about who the lovers are. Its critics say the second act, which was always the show's weaker half, has not been fixed.

For Broadway, the opening is a reminder that the district's history is longer and stranger than its current hits suggest. The Fantasticks ran for four decades a few blocks from the theatres that turned it down, and it outlasted almost every show that did make it to Broadway in that time. Its arrival now, in a small theatre with a young cast and a reimagined love story, is less a coronation than a homecoming.

The run is short — 15 weeks, ending February 7, 2027 — and the show will have to prove itself quickly. But after 66 years, 17,162 performances and a world record, The Fantasticks has already proved the only thing that matters: that a simple story, well told, can outlast everything else on Broadway.

Why it matters: The Fantasticks on Broadway is a story about time — 66 years of it. The show outlasted the theatre district that ignored it, the critics who dismissed it and the composers who wrote it, and it arrives now with a young cast and a changed love story. That is the point: the reason it ran for 17,162 performances is not that it stayed the same, but that it could change. A musical that can turn boy-meets-girl into boy-meets-boy without losing its soul is a musical that understands what it is for. Broadway, which has spent years chasing spectacle, could do with the reminder.
