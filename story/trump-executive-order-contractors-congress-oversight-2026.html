Trump Orders Federal Contractors to Alert White House Before Sharing Information With Congress

Executive Order 14438 tells federal contractors and subcontractors to alert the White House before answering Congress, and to withhold material deemed privileged, weeks before Democrats take control of both chambers.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 21, 2026 · 4 min readFacebookX

President Donald Trump signed an executive order on Friday directing federal contractors to notify the White House of any request from Congress about their work with the executive branch, and to withhold material the administration deems confidential. Executive Order 14438 applies to all federal contractors and their subcontractors, and it orders agencies to inform contractors of the new obligations promptly.

The order sets out three duties. Contractors must notify the White House and the contracting agencies of any pending or future congressional request respecting their communications or work with the executive branch. They must coordinate with the White House and the agencies on appropriate responses, including the application or assertion of any relevant privileges. And they must abide by any instructions from the White House or the contracting agency on withholding communications that the White House or the agency has determined are protected by executive privilege.

The order also reaches down the contracting chain. It states that the requirements apply to subcontractors working for a federal contractor on a federal contract, and that agencies must take steps to require federal contractors to make sure their subcontractors comply. The Office of Management and Budget is directed to develop guidance on how agencies can ensure contractors are complying and on the steps needed to coordinate responses between agencies, contractors and the White House.

Noncompliance carries real consequences. The order says that if a contractor or subcontractor fails to comply, contracting agencies shall consider taking all appropriate actions, consistent with applicable law, to enforce its terms, including the potential termination or cancellation of contracts and the potential debarment or suspension of contractors.

The White House framed the order as a defense of executive privilege. The order says the executive branch must be able to work closely with its employees and contractors on sensitive matters without fear that confidential information will be released to unauthorized third parties, and that executive privilege, which the Supreme Court has recognized as rooted in the separation of powers, protects confidential executive branch information from disclosure. It adds that the executive branch engages in sensitive and vital work with contractors, including on matters of national and homeland security, and that if it cannot be assured those communications will remain confidential, agencies and the White House will be chilled from sharing information, stymieing critical government programs.

The order is the second step in less than a week to channel congressional oversight through the White House. On Monday, November 16, White House Counsel Will Scharf directed all federal agencies and executive departments to get approval from the White House legal and legislative affairs offices before employees agree to testify before congressional committees. Together, the two moves cover both sides of oversight: agency employees who might testify, and private companies that hold the government's contracts and communications.

The timing is tied to the midterm results. Trump's Republican administration is set to face investigations from Democrats, who will take control of both chambers of Congress in January as a result of big wins in this month's midterm election. Incoming Oversight Chair Robert Garcia this week asked Trump family members and top officials to preserve records ahead of planned probes, and Democrats have vowed to investigate the administration, its family business dealings and its relationships with private companies.

The order also asserts a limit on Congress itself. It states that Congress cannot seek information from executive branch departments, agencies, employees or federal contractors without a valid legislative purpose that does not intrude on core executive functions, and cannot obtain privileged information, including information shared between federal employees and federal contractors, before the executive branch has an opportunity to assert relevant privileges. It accuses certain congressional leaders of threatening to intrude on executive prerogatives by seeking confidential information and harassing private companies that contract with the federal government.

The order takes effect immediately, and its first test will come quickly. Agencies must now tell every contractor and subcontractor about the new duties, the budget office must write compliance guidance, and the first congressional request to a contractor under a Democratic Congress will show whether the White House is notified first, and whether any material is withheld.

Why it matters: The order matters because it draws the first battle line of the next Congress before that Congress is even seated. Democrats won the midterms promising investigations, and the White House has now told the private companies that do the government's work to check with it first before handing anything to Congress, on pain of losing their contracts. The fight will turn on a constitutional question the courts have wrestled with for decades — how far executive privilege reaches when the information sits with a contractor rather than an agency — and the answer will shape what the incoming Democratic Congress can actually learn.
