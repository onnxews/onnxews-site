Two Senate Races Still Uncalled as Democrats Hold 54 Seats — and Trump Declares Alaska Over

Scott Colom leads in Mississippi by about 2,500 votes and Mary Peltola leads in Alaska by about 600, but neither race has been called by the Associated Press.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 9, 2026 · 3 min readFacebookX

Nearly a week after Election Day, two U.S. Senate races remain uncalled, and the final size of the Democrats' new majority is still to be decided. Democrats have secured 54 seats to Republicans' 44, according to the Associated Press, with the races in Mississippi and Alaska still to be settled.

In Mississippi, Democratic challenger Scott Colom, a district attorney, leads Republican Senator Cindy Hyde-Smith by 2,574 votes, 49.1 percent to 48.8 percent, with about 97 percent of the vote counted in the AP tally posted on Friday evening. It is the closest Senate race in the country, and one of the most surprising: it has been more than a decade since any Democrat won statewide office in Mississippi.

County election officials are still reviewing absentee ballots and affidavit ballots, Mississippi's term for provisional ballots. Mail-in absentee ballots postmarked by Election Day must reach circuit clerks by Tuesday, November 10, and counties have until Friday, November 13, to submit certified results to the state. Mississippi has no recount process.

Republicans are watching the remaining ballots closely. Hyde-Smith's campaign and the National Republican Senatorial Committee have asked officials in all 82 counties to set aside and preserve affidavit ballots, and the state Republican Party says it has sent more than 50 volunteer lawyers and observers to counting sites. Republicans estimate roughly 20,000 ballots remain outstanding.

If Colom holds on, he would be Mississippi's first Black senator since 1881, according to reports, and the result would rank among the biggest upsets of the midterm cycle. Colom took the lead very late on election night, by just 28 votes, and his margin has grown steadily as more ballots have been counted.

In Alaska, Democrat Mary Peltola leads Republican Senator Dan Sullivan by 637 first-choice votes, 102,962 to 102,325, with about three-quarters of the vote counted. A third candidate, Daniel Sullivan, has about 3,600 votes. Absentee ballots can arrive through November 18, and a ranked-choice tabulation is set for that day if no candidate tops 50 percent.

An analysis by the New York Times of ballots tabulated so far found that Senator Sullivan is unlikely to make significant gains in a second round of counting, because the second-choice votes of the other Sullivan's supporters do not break his way. If that holds, Peltola could cross 50 percent once the ranked-choice tabulation runs.

President Trump posted on Saturday that Senator Sullivan had 'won' the Alaska race, but the Associated Press has not called it, and its tally still shows Peltola ahead. The declaration added to the confusion around a race that will not be resolved for more than a week.

The two open races decide only the final margin, not control: Democrats take over the Senate on January 3 whether the majority is 54, 55 or 56 seats. In the House, the AP tally on Saturday stood at 254 seats called for Democrats and 162 for Republicans, with 19 uncalled.

What happens next is a matter of deadlines. Mississippi's mail-ballot window closes on Tuesday and its counties must certify by Friday. Alaska's ranked-choice tabulation is set for November 18. Senate Republicans, meanwhile, are scheduled to elect their leaders on Tuesday, November 10, as they prepare for life in the minority.

Correction (Nov. 11, 2026): An earlier version of this article said Scott Colom would be Mississippi's first Black senator since 1875. Blanche K. Bruce, the last Black senator from Mississippi before Colom, served from March 4, 1875, to March 3, 1881 — so Colom is the first since 1881.
