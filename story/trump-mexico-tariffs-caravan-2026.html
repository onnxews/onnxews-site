Trump Threatens to Double Tariffs on Mexico Over Migrant Caravan Heading North

The president warned that tariffs on Mexico will be immediately doubled if the country does not stop a caravan of about 1,500 migrants that set out from Tapachula on Tuesday.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 13, 2026 · 4 min readFacebookX

President Donald Trump threatened on Thursday to double tariffs on Mexico if it does not stop a migrant caravan walking north toward the United States, warning in a social media post that the migrants would not be allowed to enter and that there would be no leniency. The threat ties trade policy directly to immigration enforcement, and it comes as the United States and Mexico are in the middle of negotiating a new trade agreement.

Trump's message, posted on Truth Social on Thursday morning, read in full: Here we go again! They will not be accepted! MEXICO MUST STOP THEM, NOW, and if they do not do so, the Tariffs on Mexico will be IMMEDIATELY DOUBLED. There will be no leniency! He linked the caravan's formation to last week's midterm elections, writing that with the perceived loss by the Republicans in the midterms, the invasion of our country is taking place again.

The caravan at the center of the threat is an estimated 1,500 people, mostly from Central America, who set off on Tuesday from Tapachula, a city in southern Mexico near the Guatemalan border, according to Reuters. The group is hoping that the Democrats' victory in the midterm elections could open pathways for them to live and work in the United States. A separate report put the number of people moving north at nearly 2,000, a different count that appears to include more than one group on the road.

Mexican President Claudia Sheinbaum acknowledged on Thursday that a group of people was walking northward toward the United States, but she said no migrant caravan has arrived at the U.S. border since Trump returned to power last year. She said two groups were moving: one in Arriaga, in the southern state of Chiapas, that started with 500 to 600 people and has shrunk to fewer than 100, and another that set out from Honduras.

The tariff threat would fall on an already strained trading relationship. The administration imposed a 10 percent tariff on most Mexican goods in July under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974, so doubling it would mean a 20 percent duty. The United States bought $481.6 billion in Mexican goods through September of this year, more than from any other country, which means a doubling would reach deep into American supply chains for cars, electronics, food and manufactured goods.

Economists who have studied the existing tariffs say the cost would land mostly on Americans. The Congressional Budget Office estimated in February that foreign exporters absorb only 5 percent of tariff costs and that U.S. consumers bear the remaining 95 percent. The Tax Foundation estimated that all tariffs now in place will cost the average American household about $820 this year, with about $130 of that attributable to duties on Canadian and Mexican goods.

The threat also raised a legal question. The Supreme Court ruled in February, in a case known as Learning Resources v. Trump, that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act does not authorize tariffs, leaving it unclear what authority the president would use to double the duties on Mexico. U.S. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer, asked on CNBC's Squawk Box on Thursday whether doubling the tariffs was likely, did not say.

The backdrop is a trade negotiation that is already delicate. The United States and Mexico are negotiating a trade agreement after Trump chose in July not to extend the U.S.-Mexico-Canada trade pact, and the two governments have been working through a second Section 301 investigation into Mexico. The caravan, meanwhile, is still weeks from the border on foot, which leaves time for Mexico to act, for the White House to follow through, or for the threat to become a bargaining chip in the wider negotiation.

Why it matters: The threat matters because it fuses the two issues that have defined Trump's second term — immigration and tariffs — into a single lever, and because the lever cuts both ways. Doubling the duty on Mexican goods would punish Mexico for a caravan that is still weeks from the border, but the bill would be paid mostly by American consumers and the American companies that buy $481.6 billion a year in Mexican goods. Whether the threat is a genuine policy move, a negotiating tactic, or a message to a domestic audience after the midterms, its first test will be what Mexico does with the caravan in the coming days.
