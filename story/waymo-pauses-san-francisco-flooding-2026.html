Waymo Pauses Bay Area Robotaxi Service as Flooded Streets Strand Cars

The driverless taxi service suspended rides across the Bay Area on Wednesday after a powerful storm left several of its cars stuck in flooded intersections.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 19, 2026 · 4 min readFacebookX

Waymo paused its robotaxi service across the Bay Area on Wednesday morning, November 18, after a powerful storm flooded streets across San Francisco and left several of its driverless cars stranded. The company suspended rides at about 11 a.m. and had not resumed service as of 3 p.m. that afternoon.

The app told riders the service was "paused for now due to weather" and would resume "when road conditions improve and the forecast looks clear." The message stayed up through the afternoon as the storm continued to drench the region, and Waymo said it was in touch with local emergency management officials.

The flooding caused real problems for the robotaxis already on the road. Several Waymo cars were seen stalled or blocking intersections in flooded parts of the city, including one at Oak Street and Cole Street near the Panhandle and another at Bayshore Boulevard and Industrial Street. One car stopped in the middle of a flooded crosswalk, and another idled in place while telling its passenger to stay inside.

Waymo said it had been tracking the storm for hours before the pause and had already scaled back its fleet in response. The company has suspended service for weather before, and it says the decision is about safety: when the roads are flooded and the forecast is uncertain, the cars stay off the street rather than risk getting stuck.

This is not the first time flooding has beaten the robotaxis. In May, Waymo recalled nearly 4,000 vehicles and paused service after a robotaxi drove into a flooded road in San Antonio. The company later paused service in Atlanta, and its cars had trouble with flooded roads in Austin, Dallas, Houston, and San Antonio as well. In December 2025, the company paused service in the Bay Area during a power outage.

The pause came a day after a setback on another front. California's Department of Motor Vehicles barred Waymo from operating on state freeways after a series of construction-zone incidents, and Waymo responded by pulling its robotaxis off freeways in all of its markets, not just California. The two events together make this a difficult week for the company, even as it continues to expand into new cities.

The storm itself was a powerful atmospheric river, the kind of system that can dump heavy rain across the Bay Area in a short time. Forecasters were warning that the region could see a record wet winter this year, with an El Nino pattern expected to bring more storms. For a service that depends on cameras and sensors to see the road, heavy rain and standing water are among the hardest conditions to drive through.

Waymo is the largest driverless taxi operator in the United States, and its cars have become a familiar sight in San Francisco, where the company runs one of its biggest fleets. The service has generally been praised for its safety record, but incidents like Wednesday's, where cars end up blocking intersections, are the kind of thing that sticks in the public mind.

Service is expected to resume once the roads are clear and the forecast improves. For now, riders in the Bay Area are back to calling human drivers, and Waymo has another reminder that the hardest part of running a robotaxi service is not the technology in the car, but the weather outside it.

Why it matters: This matters because Waymo is the test case for whether driverless taxis can work as a real service, not just a demonstration. A weather pause is a safety-first decision, and it is the right one, but it also shows the limits of the technology: heavy rain and flooded streets are exactly the conditions where human drivers are most needed, and where the robotaxis are least useful. Coming a day after the freeway ban in California, the pause adds to a week of questions about how quickly Waymo can expand, and whether its cars can handle the worst of a winter that forecasters say could be unusually wet.
