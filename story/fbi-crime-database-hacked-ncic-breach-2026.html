Hackers Breach the FBI's National Crime Database in Second Cyberattack on the Bureau in Months

The FBI is investigating an apparent breach of the National Crime Information Center, the database police across the country rely on for warrants, fugitives and background checks, and it is still unclear what information was compromised.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 22, 2026 · 4 min readFacebookX

Hackers breached the FBI's National Crime Information Center, three sources familiar with the matter told Fox News, marking the second cyberattack against the bureau in months. The FBI is investigating the hack to determine the unauthorized entry point, but for now it is unclear what information was compromised, the sources said.

The intrusion, first reported by CNN on Friday, November 20, targeted one of the most critical information-sharing systems in American law enforcement. The National Crime Information Center, known as NCIC, is used by federal, state and local agencies to share information on warrants, wanted fugitives, missing persons, stolen property and background checks. Two people briefed on the issue told CNN the agency is investigating the breach.

The danger is not only what may have been taken. A former FBI official who led investigations into cybercriminal groups told CNN that the NCIC database is one of the most critical information-sharing systems in law enforcement, and warned that the bigger concern is whether hackers could compromise the accuracy of the information officers rely on. Every day, officers across the country use the system to identify wanted fugitives, check for outstanding warrants and access information that can directly affect officer and public safety.

President Donald Trump addressed the breach on Saturday in a telephone interview with Fox News. "I will say that it's not good. I don't like that," Trump said, adding that he had "just heard" about the hack. He then used the incident to revisit his longstanding grievances with the FBI over its investigation into Russian interference in the 2016 election, suggesting without evidence that the breach could reveal information about what he called the "Russia, Russia, Russia hoax" and voter fraud.

The FBI did not immediately respond to a request for comment. On Thursday, after bureau officials learned of the latest cybersecurity incident, FBI Director Kash Patel appeared on Fox News to tout the bureau's cybersecurity work, discussing an operation aimed at urging companies to update their systems and prevent hackers from getting to their sensitive data.

The breach is the second intrusion involving an FBI system in recent months. In September, a group called ShinyHunters hacked into the FBI's job portal website, targeting sensitive employment records. The group claimed it stole sensitive information belonging to almost every FBI agent, including names, home addresses, Social Security numbers, assignments and, in some cases, the names of family members.

Canadian cybersecurity executive Edward Dubrovsky was arrested on October 8 and faces federal extortion conspiracy charges in connection with the FBI's investigation into ShinyHunters, according to court records. FBI Director Patel announced the arrest of the suspected ShinyHunters co-conspirator on October 9, a day after Dubrovsky was taken into custody.

The bureau has been targeted before. In 2023, hackers breached a computer system the FBI's New York field office used in child sexual exploitation investigations, including a system used to store images related to the Jeffrey Epstein probe, CNN previously reported. The latest incident also comes amid a string of high-profile outages affecting banks, the National Security Agency and the Federal Aviation Administration's air traffic control system, though administration officials have said those outages resulted from routine upgrades, not breaches.

For now, the key questions are unanswered: who got in, how they got in, and whether anything was changed or taken. The FBI has not said whether the intruders are linked to the ShinyHunters group or to any other actor, and no group has publicly claimed responsibility for the NCIC breach as of Saturday. Until the investigation produces answers, police departments across the country will be left wondering whether the database they check dozens of times a day can still be trusted.

Why it matters: A breach of the NCIC database is different from an ordinary corporate hack because the system is the shared memory of American policing: if its records cannot be trusted, every traffic stop and warrant check carries a new risk. That it is the bureau's second breach in months, after the ShinyHunters jobs-portal attack, points to a pattern rather than a one-off lapse, and it lands in a week when the NSA, the FAA and major banks have all suffered outages. The investigation's findings, and whether the FBI discloses them, will matter more than the political argument the breach has already set off.
