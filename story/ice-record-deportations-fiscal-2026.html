ICE Deported a Record 483,200 People in Fiscal 2026 as Arrests Also Hit a High

The Department of Homeland Security says immigration enforcement set agency records on both fronts in the year that ended September 30 — though independent data shows much of the deportation total came from voluntary departures, not formal removals.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 13, 2026 · 5 min readFacebookX

U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement deported 483,200 people in fiscal year 2026, the highest annual total in the agency's history, the Department of Homeland Security announced on Thursday. ICE also recorded more than 491,000 immigration arrests during the year, another record, as the Trump administration's enforcement crackdown reached its fullest scale yet.

The deportation figure, reported by the Department of Homeland Security on November 12, surpassed the previous record of 442,640 deportations in fiscal 2025 by 40,560, an increase of about 9 percent. It was nearly 78 percent higher than the 271,484 deportations recorded in fiscal 2024, the last full fiscal year of the Biden administration, and about 18 percent higher than the 409,849 removals recorded in fiscal 2012, the Obama-era benchmark the department cited.

The arrest total was just as striking. Nearly 475,000 of the more than 491,000 arrests were administrative arrests, meaning arrests for alleged civil immigration violations rather than criminal charges, and that figure alone was the highest in ICE's history. In September, the final month of the fiscal year, ICE carried out more than 48,000 arrests and 44,000 removals. August set the monthly arrest record at 50,925, breaking the previous high of 50,208 set in July, which had itself broken June's mark of 43,900.

The department framed the year as a break with the recent past. Fiscal 2026 deportations were almost the same as the three full fiscal years under President Joe Biden combined, at 486,000, and the arrest total exceeded the fewer than 427,000 arrests recorded across those three years combined. The 483,200 deportations were also more than eight times the roughly 59,000 recorded in fiscal 2021, and arrests were more than six times the roughly 74,000 recorded that year.

But the record total comes with an important qualification about how it was counted. An analysis by Syracuse University's Transactional Records Access Clearinghouse, based on federal case records obtained through a Freedom of Information Act lawsuit, found that formal removals accounted for only 63 percent of reported deportations in July and August 2026, with the remaining 37 percent classified as voluntary departures or voluntary returns, in which migrants leave without a formal removal order. The group's projection for the full fiscal year, 481,008 deportations, landed close to the department's figure, suggesting the headline total is broadly consistent with the underlying records even as the mix of enforcement changed.

The department's own release did not break down how many of the people arrested had criminal histories, and CBS News reported that in July 2026, for the first time during President Donald Trump's second term, more than half of ICE arrests involved immigrants who had neither criminal convictions nor pending criminal charges. At the same time, the department highlighted cases involving people accused of serious crimes, including a Mexican national charged with second-degree murder and aggravated sexual assault in the death of a 2-year-old girl in Virginia, and a Honduran woman convicted of attempting to murder her newborn son in New York.

DHS Secretary Markwayne Mullin praised the figures in a statement on Thursday, saying fiscal 2026 produced the highest arrest and deportation totals in the agency's history and crediting the president's leadership. The department also said that, as of October, nearly 1.2 million people had been deported and more than 1.25 million arrested since Trump returned to office, and that more than 3 million migrants have left the United States in total.

The record year still fell short of the administration's stated goal of one million deportations a year, and it arrived as the politics of enforcement shifted. Trump told CBS's 60 Minutes that deportations of longtime immigrants who worked and paid taxes had been too tough, and exit polls found that 58 percent of voters believed immigration enforcement had gone too far. Even so, the administration has signalled no slowdown: Trump recently named immigration hard-liner Stephen Miller as White House chief of staff, and CBS News reported that ICE officials have been instructed to target about 19,000 arrests a week.

Congress's 2025 tax and spending legislation provided billions of dollars in additional funding for immigration enforcement, which will help sustain the current pace as Democrats prepare to take control of Congress. The coming year will test whether the record-setting machinery built in fiscal 2026 can be maintained, and whether the political backlash registered in the midterms changes how it is used.

Why it matters: The record matters less as a single number than as a measure of how far enforcement has moved in one year: deportations nearly doubled from the last Biden year while the mix shifted toward administrative arrests and voluntary departures. The counting debate, the share of arrestees with no criminal record, and the exit-poll backlash all point to the same tension the administration now faces — a system built to set records, and a public that is increasingly uneasy about how those records are set. What happens next depends on whether the new Democratic Congress can constrain an enforcement apparatus that now has both the funding and the weekly arrest targets to keep going.
