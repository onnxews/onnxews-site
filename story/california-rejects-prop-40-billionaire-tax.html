California Voters Reject Proposition 40, the One-Time Billionaire Tax

The Associated Press called the race on Monday after voters turned down the first modern wealth tax ever put to a statewide ballot, 52.8 percent to 47.2 percent.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 10, 2026 · 4 min readFacebookX

California voters have rejected Proposition 40, a ballot measure that would have imposed a one-time 5 percent tax on the net worth of the state's billionaires, the Associated Press determined on Monday. With about 83 percent of ballots counted, the 'no' side led 52.8 percent to 47.2 percent, a margin the AP judged impossible to overturn. The result settles one of the most expensive and closely watched ballot fights in California history, and it closes the door, at least for now, on the first modern wealth tax ever put directly to voters in the United States.

The measure would have applied to anyone who lived in California on January 1, 2026, and whose net worth topped 1 billion dollars by the end of that year. Roughly 200 people fell into that category, including some of the richest figures in technology and entertainment. The tax was designed by the French-American economists Gabriel Zucman and Emmanuel Saez and placed on the ballot by the healthcare workers' union SEIU-United Healthcare Workers West, which argued the state needed the money to replace federal healthcare funding it expects to lose.

Supporters estimated the levy would raise about 100 billion dollars over five years. Most of the money would have gone to backfill cuts to Medi-Cal, California's Medicaid program, which serves low-income residents. About 10 percent was earmarked for education and food-assistance programs. The union said the state stands to lose roughly that much in federal healthcare support over the coming years, and it framed the tax as a way to protect clinics and hospitals from the shortfall.

The campaign against the measure was one of the most lopsided spending battles in state history. Opponents spent about 378 million dollars, more than ten times the roughly 33.5 million dollars raised by supporters, according to campaign finance tallies. Google co-founder Sergey Brin was the largest single donor against the tax, contributing more than 100 million dollars. The 'no' campaign flooded television, radio and online advertising in the final weeks, arguing the tax would drive wealthy residents and jobs out of the state.

The politics of the measure cut across party lines in unusual ways. The California Democratic Party endorsed the tax, but Governor Gavin Newsom opposed it, as did both the Democratic and Republican candidates for governor and Planned Parenthood Affiliates of California. Newsom and other critics warned that a one-time retroactive tax on wealth would be challenged in court and could push high earners to relocate, taking their income tax payments with them. Supporters countered that the revenue was essential and that the wealthy could afford it.

The companion measure on the same ballot told a different story. Proposition 42, a counter-referendum backed by Brin that would ban retroactive wealth taxes in California, was leading with 51.6 percent of the vote on Monday evening, though the Associated Press had not yet called it. If it passes, it would make it far harder for supporters to bring a similar tax back to voters in the future. Backers of Proposition 40 have said they could try again in 2028, but that path now looks much steeper.

The result was not uniform across the state. San Francisco, home to many of the tech fortunes the measure targeted, backed Proposition 40 by roughly 56 percent to 44 percent, a notable exception to the statewide verdict. Elsewhere, voters in more conservative and suburban counties rejected it by wide margins. The split underscored how the debate divided even the state's most liberal communities, where support for taxing the rich ran up against fears about the economy and the state's business climate.

The final count is still to come, and the California Secretary of State is scheduled to certify the November 3 election on December 11. But the outcome on Proposition 40 is settled. For supporters, the defeat is a setback for a movement that hoped California would launch a global debate on taxing billionaires. For opponents, it is proof that voters will draw a line even in a state that has repeatedly backed higher taxes on the wealthy. The fight over how California pays for healthcare, and who pays for it, is far from over.

The rejection also carries national implications. Wealth taxes have been debated in Washington for years, and economists who designed the California measure hoped a victory would show the idea could win at the ballot box. Instead, the campaign became a case study in how much money opponents are willing to spend to stop one. Whether the idea returns in 2028, as backers have suggested, will depend on whether they can find a formula that survives both the courts and another campaign of that scale.
