Houthi Missile Damages Riyadh's PIF Tower in Second Strike on Financial District

A suspected Houthi missile struck the PIF Tower in Riyadh early Thursday, blowing out windows across seven to eight floors of the sovereign wealth fund's headquarters in the second attack on the financial district in under two weeks.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 19, 2026 · 4 min readFacebookX

Riyadh's tallest skyscraper, which houses the headquarters of Saudi Arabia's sovereign wealth fund, was struck on Thursday by a suspected Houthi missile in the latest escalation of the Iran-backed Yemeni group's war with the kingdom. The strike blew out windows across seven to eight floors of PIF Tower, bending parts of its metal facade, according to TBS News.

It was the second time in less than two weeks that a Houthi attack had damaged a landmark in Riyadh's financial district, with state oil giant Aramco's building hit in an 8 November attack. Entrances to the King Abdullah Financial District were closed and people were being evacuated, while ambulances and fire trucks were at the scene. There were no immediate reports of casualties.

Three sources familiar with the matter said the building had been struck by an incoming Houthi missile. The Saudi-led coalition fighting the Houthis said it had “dealt with a missile threat” launched by the group towards Riyadh, adding that its initial assessment showed debris had fallen and material damage had been reported. The Public Investment Fund acknowledged receiving a Reuters request for comment. The Houthis issued no immediate statement on the strike.

Residents of the Saudi capital were awoken by a loud boom in the early hours of Thursday. In Riyadh's financial district, damage was visible to one of the tallest buildings, the Public Investment Fund Tower, said a witness, who spoke on the condition of anonymity because of security concerns.

The Houthis have been targeting Saudi airports and oil infrastructure since Yemen's frozen civil war was reignited in July amid the United States-Iran war. On the ground in Yemen, the group has seized swathes of territory from the Saudi-backed government, forcing it to stage a major retreat at the weekend. The group has seized the country's entire Red Sea coast, including the strategically vital Bab el-Mandeb Strait at its mouth, and surrounded the city of Taiz in the highlands overlooking the government's temporary capital, Aden.

The conflict has dealt a major blow to Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman's plans to develop and diversify the economy of Saudi Arabia, the world's biggest oil exporter and a United States ally. The prince met United States Central Command chief Admiral Brad Cooper in Riyadh on Wednesday, Cooper's third publicly reported visit to Saudi Arabia since the Yemen conflict resumed.

Reuters reported on Wednesday that Saudi officials now expect the campaign to continue for up to six months, according to two Western officials briefed by the Saudis on the kingdom's plans. A third source familiar with the Saudi plans confirmed the accounts. The sources said the Saudis, who replaced the commander of their coalition fighting in Yemen this week, want to see their allies make gains on the ground before entering any negotiations with the Houthis.

In the wider war, which began in February when the United States and Israel attacked Iran, there has been no sign of a breakthrough in negotiations mediated by Qatar. Meanwhile, hostilities in the Gulf have intensified in recent days, with Iran firing at United States allies Bahrain and Kuwait, while United States airstrikes hit Iran's Qeshm Island in the Strait of Hormuz on Tuesday.

Saudi Arabia first entered the Yemen conflict in 2015 at the head of an Arab coalition seeking to restore the country's government to power after the Houthis seized the capital, Sanaa, and much of the country. The sides fought to a bloody stalemate before reaching a ceasefire in 2022, which lasted until the summer of this year.

Why it matters: The strike matters because it brings the war to the heart of Saudi Arabia's economic showcase: the financial district that Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman built to symbolize the kingdom's future. Hitting the sovereign wealth fund's own tower, twice in less than two weeks, shows the Houthis can reach the targets that matter most to Riyadh, and it comes as Saudi officials privately concede the campaign could run for six more months. With the wider Gulf war still burning, the attack is a reminder that the conflict's front line now runs through the region's financial capitals, not just its battlefields.
