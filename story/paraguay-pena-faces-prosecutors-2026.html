Paraguay's Ex-President Pena Faces Prosecutors After Impeachment and House Arrest

A day after the Senate removed him from office, former President Santiago Pena appeared before prosecutors in Asuncion as the new president, Pedro Alliana, inherits a banking crisis that froze the savings of three million people.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 8, 2026 · 4 min readFacebookX

Paraguay's former President Santiago Pena arrived at the Attorney General's Office on Saturday to appear before prosecutors, a day after he was impeached and placed under house arrest amid a political and financial crisis triggered by the collapse of a digital bank. The appearance marks the first time the ousted president has faced investigators since the Senate removed him from office on Friday.

Lawmakers voted 44 to 0 on Friday to remove Pena from the presidency for poor performance in his duties, the constitutional ground for impeachment, after a one-day trial. The 45th senator did not vote. Within the hour, Vice President Pedro Alliana, 52, was sworn in as Paraguay's 53rd president before a special joint session of Congress, to complete the term ending in August 2028. Antonio Barrios, until then a Colorado Party senator, was named vice president.

Prosecutors ordered Pena placed under house arrest on suspicion of breach of trust and other crimes, and police arrived at his home in Asuncion at 8:05 p.m. on Friday, closing the street and posting a guard. He was ordered to stay at one of his homes in the capital until he appears before prosecutors, and on Saturday morning he was due to give his first statement to the economic crimes and anti-corruption unit at 11 a.m. local time.

Prosecutors said on Friday that charges against Pena were still being determined but could include influence peddling, breach of trust and using public office for personal gain. The most serious charges carry up to 10 years in prison. Attorney General Emiliano Rolon Fernandez has ordered three criminal investigations covering oversight of the bank, the handling of its failure and the placement of public funds there.

At the center of the crisis is ueno, a digital lender that grew from a niche fintech launched in 2021 into Paraguay's fifth-largest bank by deposits. The bank collapsed after the central bank intervened on November 3, identifying serious solvency problems, and its failure froze the savings of nearly three million Paraguayans, almost half the country's population, and disrupted pension payments. Lawmakers who backed Pena's impeachment accused him of conflicts of interest, manipulating regulatory oversight and participating in an institutional cover-up involving the central bank.

Pena, who was a shareholder in ueno's holding company until March 2025, has denied wrongdoing and said the bank's problems were a regulatory matter. His removal came after he first announced his resignation on Thursday, but lawmakers filed impeachment charges anyway, determined to remove him through the constitutional process rather than accept his departure on his own terms. He is only the second president ousted by impeachment since the end of General Alfredo Stroessner's 35-year dictatorship in 1989.

Alliana now takes over a country in the middle of one of its worst political and economic scandals in years. The longtime ruling Colorado Party politician was sworn in within the hour of Pena's removal, but he too is under investigation in connection with ueno, according to the Associated Press, leaving the new government under a cloud from its first day. His immediate test will be getting depositors their money back and restoring confidence in a banking system shaken by the collapse.

The speed of the crisis has been extraordinary. In the space of a week, the central bank intervened in a failing lender, the president announced his resignation, the Senate removed him anyway, prosecutors placed him under house arrest, and a new president was sworn in. For a country that has worked to consolidate its democracy since the end of dictatorship, the impeachment and criminal investigation of a sitting president is a watershed moment.

What happens next depends on what prosecutors decide after Pena's statement. Formal charges could follow, and the investigations into the bank's oversight and the placement of public funds could reach further into the political establishment. The attorney general's three investigations suggest the inquiry is only beginning.

For the millions of Paraguayans locked out of their accounts, the political drama in Asuncion is secondary to a simpler question: when will they get their money back. Alliana's government inherits that problem along with the presidency, and its handling of the depositors' claims will be the first measure of whether the new president can steady a country in crisis.
