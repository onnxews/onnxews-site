Meta's Muse AI Can Now Talk Back in Real Time — With a Face That Reacts

The bigger Muse update adds live voice conversations and a realtime avatar, as Meta reorganizes its AI division around superintelligence.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 7, 2026 · 3 min readFacebookX

Meta has rolled out a bigger update to Muse, its AI assistant, that lets users have real-time conversations with it instead of typing prompts and waiting for text replies. The update, released this week, adds Muse Voice, which works with Muse Realtime Avatar technology and lets users watch their AI assistant react and interact with facial expressions as it talks.

The move turns Muse from a chatbot into something closer to a conversational companion. Users launched Muse in the United States in September, and the new version is designed to make talking to the assistant feel natural, with the avatar responding visually as well as verbally. It is the most ambitious version of the product Meta has shipped so far.

The update arrives as Meta reorganizes its artificial intelligence work. Chief executive Mark Zuckerberg has reportedly restructured the company's AI division and created Meta Superintelligence Labs, a new group built from the existing Meta AI division. The restructuring signals that Meta sees its AI assistant as a central product, not a side project.

Muse is also part of a much larger spending push. Meta acquired AI coding startup Conductor this week, adding multi-agent coding tools to its engineering arsenal, and the company is one of the hyperscalers pouring billions into AI infrastructure. The same week, investors punished AI stocks over worries about data-center regulation and cash burn, a reminder that the spending has to produce products people want.

Real-time voice is where the industry is heading. OpenAI, Google and Anthropic have all shipped voice modes for their assistants, and the competition is now about which one feels most natural to talk to. An avatar that reacts with facial expressions is Meta's attempt to stand out, leaning on the company's long investment in avatars, virtual reality and social presence.

The technology raises familiar questions. A voice assistant that can hold a real-time conversation is more engaging than a text box, but it is also easier to mistake for a person, and security researchers have already warned about vulnerabilities in AI agents that can act on the web. Meta will need to show that Muse's voice mode is safe as well as smooth.

For Meta, the stakes go beyond a single feature. The company has spent years trying to make AI assistants a daily habit, from its Ray-Ban smart glasses to its messaging apps. Muse is the thread tying those efforts together, and a voice-first version is the clearest sign yet that Meta wants the assistant to be something people talk to, not just type at.

The update is rolling out now, and early users will quickly discover whether the avatar feels like a genuine step forward or a gimmick. The test for any voice assistant is simple: does it understand you the first time, does it answer in a way that helps, and do you come back tomorrow to talk to it again.

If Muse Voice passes that test, it could become Meta's most important AI product yet. If it does not, it will join a long list of voice assistants that promised a conversation and delivered a novelty. Either way, the race to build an AI you can actually talk to just got louder.
