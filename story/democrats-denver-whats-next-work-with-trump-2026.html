Newly Elected Democrats in Denver Urge Party to Work With Trump, Not Just Investigate Him

At the 'What's Next' conference in Denver, the Democrats who just won the midterms told their party to chase results with President Trump rather than spend two years on investigations — a message that puts them at odds with the party's 'lawyer up' wing.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 17, 2026 · 6 min readFacebookX

Congressional Democrats face a choice as they prepare to take power in January: spend the next two years investigating President Donald Trump, or find ways to work with him and deliver results. At a two-day conference in Denver, the party's newly elected leaders argued forcefully for the second path, warning that voters who handed them Congress in the November 3 midterms expect governing, not payback.

The tension has been building since election night. Party leaders immediately warned Trump and Republicans to 'lawyer up' and began outlining investigations into the administration's handling of immigration enforcement, the president's business interests and the Justice Department's actions. But at the 'What's Next' conference, hosted by the group Majority Democrats, the newest winners struck a different tone, telling Fox News Digital that the party should seek opportunities to cooperate with the president.

Iowa Governor-elect Rob Sand was the bluntest. He cautioned his party against making investigations and impeachments the focus of its new momentum, particularly when the underlying issues have already been reported, and said he did not want Democrats 'throwing bombs' at Republicans. 'There are people who are really struggling right now, whose lives have been ruined by high gas prices, by all kinds of policy choices, and they need results,' Sand said. 'They need public servants who are actually trying to serve the public.' Asked whether that meant working with the Trump administration, he said yes: 'He's there, he was put there two years ago. He's gonna be there another two years. You don't get to pick and choose who you work with. The people do.'

Senator-elect James Talarico of Texas, the first Democrat in nearly four decades to win a statewide election in Texas, made the same case in sharper terms. 'Some of these reforms may not pass until 2029, but the American people cannot wait two years for change,' Talarico said. 'We must work with President Trump.' He urged Democrats to make the Republican president's final two years in office 'the most productive of any presidency in American history' and warned against getting 'bogged down in the same petty fights.'

The strategists in the room offered a related caution: do not mistake a backlash against Trump for an embrace of the Democratic Party. Lis Smith, a senior adviser for Majority Democrats, said the election was 'largely a referendum on Donald Trump and less a validation of Democrats and their policies,' and warned the party not to treat its wins as a mandate for policies it did not campaign on. 'We should not read into these election results as a mandate for far left policies advocated only by a handful of candidates,' she said. The point was echoed by Representative Jason Crow of Colorado, who told NPR's team at the conference: 'We won races in places where we haven't won in a long time, even in some places where we've never won. And the message shouldn't be that [voters] are fully embracing the Democratic Party.'

A Reuters/Ipsos poll published this month gave the pragmatists ammunition: nearly three-quarters of Americans said they wanted Democratic leaders in Congress to make a concerted effort to work with the president. The conference's own agenda pointed the same way, with sessions on what voters want and what needs to change, and speakers returning again and again to the cost of living. Former Transportation Secretary Pete Buttigieg told the gathering that Democrats should learn from the current White House, saying they needed 'a bias towards action and a readiness to get things done coming out of the gate and at the very same time a readiness to plan for the long-term.'

Not everyone in Denver was sold. Senator Elissa Slotkin of Michigan backed working with Trump on substance, saying, 'If slapping Trump's name on it and putting it in gold letters would get it through — hell yes, I would do that.' But New Jersey Governor Mikie Sherrill warned that the president was a bad-faith negotiating partner. 'The art of the deal to Trump is he figures out what you want, he leverages the you know what out of you, and keeps taking, taking, taking, and never delivers,' Sherrill said. 'My advice to Sen. Talarico is if Trump wants to work with us, we will lay out the agenda, and he can sign it.'

The debate matters because the new Democratic majorities will have real power when Congress convenes in January — and real limits. Presumptive next Speaker Hakeem Jeffries told the conference he plans a robust agenda that includes reversing Medicaid cuts, banning congressional stock trading, mid-decade redistricting, establishing a statutory right to abortion and overturning the Supreme Court's Citizens United decision. But Trump still holds the veto, and the Senate filibuster still requires 60 votes, so much of that agenda can only become law with Republican votes — or the president's signature.

Even Senator Bernie Sanders, who has spent his career as the party's most prominent progressive voice, warned Democrats not to spend all their time investigating Trump. The split in Denver is now the central strategic question for the party: whether the next two years are spent building a case against the president or building a record with him. Maryland Senator Chris Van Hollen, who told reporters in Denver he is no longer considering a White House bid and is instead pursuing the Senate's top leadership post, embodies the tension — the party's next leaders must choose between the investigations their base wants and the results the voters who elected them expect.

Why it matters: The Denver conference exposed the fault line that will define the next Congress. The 'lawyer up' wing wants accountability for the Trump administration, and it will have the votes to launch investigations the moment Democrats take the gavels. But the newly elected members who actually won the swing races are warning that investigations alone will not keep those seats — and the polling backs them, with nearly three-quarters of Americans wanting cooperation. The next two years will test whether a party that won by running against Trump can govern alongside him.
