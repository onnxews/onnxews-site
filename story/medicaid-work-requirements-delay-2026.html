Trump Administration Gives States Six More Months Before Medicaid Work Requirements Bite

The Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services told states on Friday they will not have to remove anyone from Medicaid for failing to meet new work requirements until July 1, 2027 — a six-month reprieve for state agencies scrambling to build the systems to enforce the rules.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 16, 2026 · 5 min readFacebookX

The Trump administration is giving states an optional six-month grace period for enforcing new Medicaid work requirements, which were due to go into effect on January 1. The Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services announced on Friday an optional "hold harmless" period through June 30, 2027, to give states a transition period to further modernize systems, improve processes and streamline the applicant and beneficiary experience.

During the hold harmless period, states will still assess whether Medicaid applicants and beneficiaries meet the work requirements or qualify for an exclusion, but otherwise eligible people will not lose or be denied coverage solely because they fail to meet the requirement, CMS said. States that are ready may enforce the rules from January; states that are not ready will not face federal penalties until July. CMS asked states to request the flexibility in their mitigation plans by December 11.

Under the policy, many Medicaid beneficiaries must work or volunteer at least 80 hours a month, or be in school at least half-time. The requirements apply to certain Medicaid applicants and beneficiaries ages 19 to 64, with exemptions for the medically frail, disabled people and others who are unable to meet the mandates, including those caring for children under 14. The policy applies only to people who receive Medicaid under the voluntary expansion that 40 states undertook to cover more lower-income adults without children at home.

The work requirements were established as part of last summer's massive H.R. 1 legislation, or the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, which President Donald Trump signed in July 2025. CMS issued an interim final rule in June 2026 spelling out the qualifying activities, including 80 hours per month of work, community service or participation in a work program, enrollment in an educational program at least half-time, a combination of qualifying activities totaling at least 80 hours per month, or meeting a specified monthly income threshold.

States have said they were not given enough time to set up the staffing, training and technology needed to police the new rules by January 1. "States have made real progress, and they've been clear with us about what it takes to get this right for beneficiaries — at scale and for the long term," Dan Brillman, Deputy Administrator and Director of the Center for Medicaid and CHIP Services, said in a statement. "If the state can use data to show that someone is working or qualifies for an exclusion, that person should not have to track down paperwork to prove it."

The delay also follows pressure from Democrats in Congress. Senator Ron Wyden of Oregon and Representative Frank Pallone Jr. of New Jersey wrote to CMS Administrator Dr. Mehmet Oz this week asking him to use his discretion to give states more time, citing in part a recent decision out of a Boston federal court that determined medically frail people should not be bound by the work requirement qualification rules. A coalition of 26 states has sued the Trump administration over the rollout, arguing that key provisions could cause eligible people to lose coverage, and in October a Massachusetts judge sided with the states and rejected the administration's definition of "medical frailty" for critical exemptions.

Early results from Nebraska, the first state to launch the requirements in May 2026, illustrate the potential consequences of documentation problems. Preliminary data presented to the state's Medicaid Advisory Committee showed that more than 9 in 10 people subject to the work requirement and due to have their benefits renewed in July and August qualified. But 533 people were denied continuing coverage, and for most of them it was over paperwork: they did not show that they were working or were exempt from the requirement. Of those denied, 43 lost coverage because they did not meet the new requirement. "There are likely people who are still eligible for coverage who lost it," said Amy Behnke, the CEO of the Health Care Center Association of Nebraska.

Critics say most enrollees covered by the mandate are already working, and that people are more likely to lose coverage because of the paperwork to prove it than because they fail to work. Studies estimate that between 5 million and 10 million people could lose Medicaid coverage in 2028 following the rollout of the new work requirements. State Medicaid administrators, health insurers, physicians and hospitals have pushed back on the requirements, citing concerns about financial and operational challenges, a spike in uncompensated care and the rule's strict medical frailty exemptions.

The decision also could give some breathing room to Congress to consider changes to the requirement. Democrats who are taking control of both chambers in January after their sweeping election victories last week have said they want to roll back the requirements. "I think these extra six months are going to give states the ability to respond to the change, and there will be fewer people that lose their coverage due to administrative failures" resulting from states' inability to prepare, Kathy Hempstead, senior policy officer for the Robert Wood Johnson Foundation, told The Associated Press. Still, she said it is not clear which states will opt to use the additional time.

Why it matters: The six-month pause changes the timing, not the policy: the work requirements are still the law, and enforcement arrives on July 1, 2027, whether states are ready or not. But the delay matters because the early evidence from Nebraska suggests the biggest risk is not people refusing to work — it is eligible people losing coverage over paperwork. It also hands the incoming Democratic Congress a window to try to change or repeal the rules before anyone is actually removed for failing them, which turns a technical implementation delay into a live political fight over the next six months.
