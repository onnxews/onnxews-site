Paraguay's President Resigns as Bank Collapse Freezes the Savings of Three Million People

Santiago Peña submitted his resignation hours after the central bank seized Ueno Bank, and lawmakers voted 78-0 to impeach him minutes later.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 6, 2026 · 2 min readFacebookX

Paraguayan President Santiago Peña announced his resignation on Thursday after the collapse of digital lender Ueno Bank triggered widespread protests, froze millions of people's savings and cost him the support of his own party. Minutes after his announcement, the lower house of Congress voted 78-0 to impeach him.

Ueno Bank had grown from an obscure finance company into a household name in less than five years, claiming around 3 million customers, more than 60 percent of Paraguay's adults, in a country of about 6.5 million people. The bank also held roughly 800 million dollars in public deposits, including money from the state pension and health funds and the operators of Paraguay's giant hydroelectric dams.

Paraguay's central bank seized the lender on Tuesday after determining that its solvency ratio had fallen to 5.56 percent, less than half of the 12 percent regulatory minimum. The intervention immediately blocked Ueno's app, payment cards and transfer services, leaving customers unable to access their accounts. The central bank said it would transfer deposits to other banks and guarantee up to about 36,000 dollars per depositor.

Peña, a former International Monetary Fund economist elected in 2023, had faced mounting scrutiny over his past business ties to Grupo Vázquez, the conglomerate behind Ueno. He said he sold his stake in the group in March 2025 and has denied wrongdoing. Prosecutors said Thursday he is among those being investigated over the bank's failure. A judge ordered six Ueno executives jailed pending trial and two others placed under house arrest.

In a video message, Peña said he was stepping down for the sake of the country's stability, social peace and democratic institutions. Congress has not yet formally accepted his resignation, and the Senate has opened an impeachment trial. Vice President Pedro Alliana has not been sworn in — removing Peña requires 30 of the Senate's 45 votes. The Labour Ministry estimates the collapse has affected about 7,000 direct and indirect jobs.

Correction (Nov. 6, 2026): An earlier version of this article said Vice President Pedro Alliana would be sworn in without waiting for a Senate trial. In fact, Congress has not yet formally accepted President Peña's resignation, the Senate has opened an impeachment trial, and Alliana has not been sworn in.
