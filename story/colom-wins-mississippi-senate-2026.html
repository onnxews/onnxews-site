Democrat Scott Colom Wins Mississippi Senate Seat in Historic Upset

Scott Colom defeated Republican incumbent Cindy Hyde-Smith by fewer than 5,000 votes, becoming Mississippi's first Black U.S. senator since Reconstruction and the first Democrat to win a Senate race in the state since 1982.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 12, 2026 · 5 min readFacebookX

Democrat Scott Colom has won Mississippi's U.S. Senate race, defeating Republican incumbent Senator Cindy Hyde-Smith in one of the biggest upsets of the 2026 midterm elections. The Associated Press called the race for Colom on Wednesday, November 11, more than a week after Election Day, after late returns from counties across the state widened his narrow lead past the point where it could be overtaken.

Colom garnered 49.2% of the vote, compared to Hyde-Smith's 48.7%, with more than 95% of the ballots tallied on Wednesday, according to the New York Post. The two candidates were separated by fewer than 5,000 votes, a margin of roughly half a percentage point. The Associated Press called the race on Wednesday after a late update from Jackson County widened his lead. The result remains unofficial until counties submit certified returns and the state certifies the result.

Colom's victory is historic on two counts. He becomes the first Black U.S. senator from Mississippi since Blanche K. Bruce left office in 1881, and the first Democrat to win a Senate race in the state since John Stennis won his final term in 1982. Mississippi had not elected a Democrat to the Senate since 1982, and Colom won without reaching 50% of the vote, with independent candidate Ty Pinkins taking about 2%.

Hyde-Smith conceded within the hour, congratulating Colom in a video posted on X. "While this is not the outcome we worked for or hoped for, I respect the decision of Mississippi voters," Hyde-Smith said. "I want to congratulate Scott Colom and wish him well as he takes on the tremendous responsibility of representing our great state." Hyde-Smith, who was first appointed to the Senate in 2018, had won her 2020 race by a comfortable 10-point margin.

The race was one of the longest counts in the country. Colom's lead grew slowly as counties processed late-arriving absentee and provisional ballots over eight days. President Trump campaigned in Mississippi in the run-up to Election Day in a bid to shore up support in a state he won by 22.9% in 2024, up 6.4 percentage points from his 2020 performance there, but polls had warned of trouble for Republicans and forced the trip. Colom, meanwhile, was boosted on the campaign trail by former President Bill Clinton, former Vice President Kamala Harris, and former Transportation Secretary Pete Buttigieg.

The two candidates also carried a personal history into the race. Colom, 43, the district attorney for Mississippi's 16th Judicial District, was nominated by former President Joe Biden to the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Mississippi. Although he received the support of Mississippi Republican Senator Roger Wicker, Hyde-Smith blocked the nomination in April 2023 by withholding a document known as a "blue slip," which is required to advance judicial nominees through the Senate Judiciary Committee. Hyde-Smith cited "concerns" about Colom's record, and specifically pointed to his position on transgender rights. Years later, the two found themselves competing for the same Senate seat, with Hyde-Smith continuing to accuse Colom of defending transgender rights while he criticized her support of tariffs and health care cuts.

Colom's win is the Democrats' eighth Senate pickup of the midterms and gives the party 55 seats in the Senate that takes office in January, an 11-seat majority. One race remains uncalled: in Alaska, Democrat Mary Peltola leads incumbent Republican Senator Dan Sullivan by about 2 percentage points, and the state expects another substantial results update on Friday, with ranked-choice tabulation to follow if needed.

The result caps a midterm rout for Republicans that saw Democrats flip Senate seats in Iowa, Ohio, Kansas, South Carolina, and Texas, along with both chambers of Congress. In Mississippi, a state where Democrats had not won a Senate race in more than four decades, the upset was the most symbolically charged of the cycle: a Black district attorney who was blocked from a federal judgeship by his opponent will now take her seat in the Senate. Colom will be sworn in when the new Congress convenes on January 3, 2027, and his first task will be joining a Democratic majority that is already planning an aggressive first-100-days agenda.

For Hyde-Smith, the loss ends a Senate career that began with her appointment in 2018. For Mississippi, the result means the state will be represented in part by its first Black senator since the Reconstruction era, when formerly enslaved people first entered the chamber. The margin was narrow enough that the result will be studied for years as a measure of how far the state's politics have moved, and how much of the shift was driven by the national backlash against the Trump administration that defined the 2026 midterms.

Why it matters: Colom's win is the single most consequential result of the midterm cycle after the battle for control of Congress itself, because it shows the Democratic wave reached places no one expected it to. Mississippi is a state Trump carried by nearly 23 points two years ago, and Democrats had not won a Senate race there since 1982. That Colom won it — as the first Black senator from the state since Reconstruction, and against the same opponent who blocked his judicial nomination three years ago — turns a routine pickup into a story about the breadth of the backlash. It also locks in a 55-seat Democratic Senate majority before Alaska is even decided, giving the incoming majority real room to lose votes on its own side and still pass its agenda.
