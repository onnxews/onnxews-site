Indian Man Gets 19 Years and 7 Months for Plot to Kill Sikh Separatist Leader in New York

Nikhil Gupta, 55, was sentenced in Manhattan federal court on Friday for a murder-for-hire plot directed by an Indian government employee that targeted Sikh activist Gurpatwant Singh Pannun.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 22, 2026 · 6 min readFacebookX

A federal judge in Manhattan on Friday sentenced Indian national Nikhil Gupta to 235 months in prison — 19 years and seven months — for his role in a foiled murder-for-hire plot to assassinate a Sikh separatist leader in New York City. U.S. District Judge Victor Marrero imposed the sentence after a hearing of about 80 minutes in the Southern District of New York, closing one of the most diplomatically sensitive criminal cases between Washington and New Delhi in recent years.

Gupta, 55, pleaded guilty in February 2026 to murder-for-hire, conspiracy to commit murder-for-hire and conspiracy to commit money laundering. Prosecutors said he worked at the direction of an Indian government employee to arrange the killing of Gurpatwant Singh Pannun, a U.S. citizen, lawyer and vocal advocate for a sovereign Sikh state in Punjab, the northwestern Indian state where Sikhism was founded. Pannun leads Sikhs for Justice, a U.S.-based group that promotes the Khalistan separatist movement, and India has banned him and his organization.

According to the Justice Department, Gupta conspired in 2023 with Vikash Yadav, an employee of the Government of India's Cabinet Secretariat, which houses the country's foreign intelligence service, the Research and Analysis Wing. In May 2023, Yadav recruited Gupta to orchestrate Pannun's assassination in the United States. Gupta then contacted a person he believed was a criminal associate but who was in fact a confidential source working with the Drug Enforcement Administration, and the source introduced him to a purported hitman who was actually a DEA undercover officer.

Yadav agreed to pay the undercover officer $100,000 to murder Pannun, and on June 9, 2023, Yadav and Gupta arranged for an associate to deliver $15,000 in cash to the officer as an advance payment. Yadav also supplied Gupta with Pannun's home address in New York City, phone numbers and details about his daily routine, which Gupta passed on to the supposed hitman, along with surveillance photographs. Gupta told the officer to carry out the murder as soon as possible, but specifically instructed him not to do it around the time of the Indian prime minister's official state visit to the United States, which was scheduled to begin on June 20, 2023.

The plot took a darker turn two days before that visit. On June 18, 2023, masked gunmen murdered Hardeep Singh Nijjar, an associate of Pannun and a leader of the Sikh separatist movement, outside a Sikh temple in British Columbia, Canada. The next day, Gupta told the undercover officer that Nijjar "was also the target" and "we have so many targets," adding that, in light of Nijjar's murder, there was "now no need to wait" on killing Pannun. Gupta was arrested on June 30, 2023, in the Czech Republic at the request of the United States and extradited to the United States in June 2024.

At Friday's hearing, prosecutors argued that Gupta had been an enthusiastic participant in the conspiracy and sought a sentence of about 24 years. The defense asked for 12 years followed by three years of supervised release, arguing that the plot never came to fruition and that Gupta had no criminal record in India. The 235-month sentence sits at the low end of the federal sentencing guidelines range of 235 to 293 months. Judge Marrero called the sentence "reasonable and appropriate" given the nature of the crime, and he imposed no term of supervised release, saying Gupta will face deportation and removal proceedings once he completes his prison term. Gupta was also ordered to pay a $300 special assessment.

Gupta addressed the court in a brief six-minute statement delivered in English, apologizing to Pannun and the Sikh community. "I cannot undo what I have done. I am sorry to Pannun, to the community and the fraternity that I have hurt. I'm sorry," he said, turning to look at Pannun, who was seated in the courtroom. Gupta said his family, wife, mother and children had suffered for something they did not do, invoked the teachings of Mahatma Gandhi, Mother Teresa, Nelson Mandela and Martin Luther King, and said he would spend the rest of his life making up for what he had done. He appeared to choke and grow emotional as he spoke, and his lawyer patted him on the back as he sat down.

Pannun also addressed the court, speaking for about 20 minutes before the judge asked him to summarize and wrap up. He said he has not been able to carry on his regular legal practice for more than 1,200 days since the threat to his life was uncovered. A large crowd of his supporters had lined up outside the Manhattan federal courthouse to attend the hearing.

The case has tested relations between the United States and India, which has denied involvement in assassination plots against Sikh separatists. U.S. and Canadian authorities have described the Pannun plot as part of a broader effort to target Indian dissidents abroad, and Yadav has been charged in the case but has not been arrested. In a statement announcing the sentence, U.S. Attorney Jamie McDonald said the prison term "reflects the seriousness of Nikhil Gupta's conduct and should serve as a clear warning" that the Justice Department "will pursue and hold accountable anyone who attempts to commit violence on U.S. soil or against U.S. citizens."

Why it matters: The sentence closes the American chapter of a case that has strained U.S.-India relations for more than three years, but it leaves the bigger questions open. Yadav, the Indian government employee prosecutors say directed the plot, remains at large and unarrested, and India continues to deny any involvement. The case also sits alongside the still-unsolved killing of Hardeep Singh Nijjar in Canada, which Gupta himself linked to the Pannun plot in recorded conversations. For Sikh activists in the United States and Canada, the 235-month term is a measure of how seriously American courts treat foreign-directed violence on U.S. soil; for diplomats, the next test is whether the two governments can move past a case that neither side has fully resolved.
