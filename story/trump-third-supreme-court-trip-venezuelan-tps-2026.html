Court Freezes Venezuelan TPS Ruling Hours After Trump Administration's Third Supreme Court Bid

The Justice Department's third emergency trip to the Supreme Court lasted a day: an appeals court froze the ruling that would have restored Venezuelan deportation protections, and the administration withdrew its request.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 20, 2026 · 5 min readFacebookX

A federal appeals court stepped in hours after President Donald Trump's administration asked the Supreme Court for a third time to keep deportation protections ended for hundreds of thousands of Venezuelans — and the administration withdrew its Supreme Court request the next day, after the appeals court gave it the pause it had been seeking.

The Justice Department filed an emergency request on Wednesday asking the Supreme Court to again put on hold a federal judge's ruling that officials lacked the authority to end the Temporary Protected Status, or TPS, granted to the migrants under former President Joe Biden, while a legal challenge to the policy proceeds. The ruling by San Francisco-based U.S. District Judge Edward Chen was due to take effect on Friday unless a federal appeals court or the Supreme Court moved to suspend it further.

"Absent judicial intervention between now and November 20, the Department of Homeland Security will have to resurrect TPS status for hundreds of thousands of Venezuelans after a 19-month hiatus," the Justice Department said in its filing.

Hours after that filing, the San Francisco-based 9th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals extended its freeze on Chen's ruling until seven days after it issues a full ruling on the administration's appeal, the Associated Press reported. The order means the administration remains free to detain and deport Venezuelans who had previously been granted temporary protected status while the appeal plays out.

On Thursday, the administration withdrew its emergency request to the Supreme Court, telling the justices it no longer needed their intervention in light of the appeals court's order. Solicitor General D. John Sauer said the government would immediately return to the Supreme Court if the appeals court rules against it. The case is Mullin v. National TPS Alliance, Supreme Court docket No. 26A714.

Reviving TPS would scuttle the deportation of hundreds of people with final orders of removal and require the government to immediately release around 1,800 Venezuelans who are currently detained, the Justice Department said in its filing. For now, nothing changes for the Venezuelans who once held TPS: the termination remains in force, so they have no protection from detention or removal under TPS and no work permits based on it.

The plaintiffs who challenged the administration's actions, including TPS holders and an advocacy group, said that since the previous Supreme Court intervention in the case, Venezuelan TPS holders have been detained, separated from children who are American citizens, deported to Venezuela, and even sent to so-called "third countries" where they have no ties.

The Supreme Court in June backed the administration's separate actions ending TPS for hundreds of thousands of Haitians and Syrians, giving a boost to the Republican president's hardline approach toward immigration.

TPS is a designation that lets migrants from countries stricken by war, natural disaster or other catastrophes live and work in the United States while it is unsafe for them to return to their home countries. The U.S. State Department currently warns people to reconsider travel to Venezuela due to the risk of crime, kidnapping and poor health infrastructure, among other issues.

The U.S. government under Biden, a Democrat, designated Venezuela for TPS in 2021 and 2023. Just days before Trump returned to office, Biden's administration announced an extension of the 2023 designation to October 2026. The Biden administration had cited political repression, economic collapse and health crises among the justifications for the action. Former Homeland Security Secretary Kristi Noem, a Trump appointee, rescinded the extension and moved to end the TPS designations, and the Trump administration has said such protections were always meant to be temporary.

Chen ruled last year that Noem violated a federal law that governs the actions of federal agencies. That ruling, later upheld by the San Francisco-based 9th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals, was paused by the Supreme Court at the administration's behest in October 2025. That pause expired this month. The Supreme Court also previously lifted a temporary order that Chen had issued at an earlier stage of the case, and it has since ordered the 9th Circuit to apply its ruling involving Haitian and Syrian TPS holders to the dispute over the Venezuelan migrants.

The 9th Circuit has "forced the government to return to this court for a third time and rehash nearly identical arguments without even a hint of justification," the Justice Department said in its filing. What happens next now turns on the appeals court's full ruling: protections stay frozen for a week after that decision, leaving time for either side to seek further review — and the administration has said it will be straight back at the Supreme Court if it loses.

Why it matters: This is the third time the Venezuelan TPS fight has reached the Supreme Court, but this round lasted barely a day: the appeals court gave the government the pause it wanted within hours of the filing, and the administration pulled its request the next morning. The Friday deadline that drove the emergency filing is now moot, and the fight moves back to the 9th Circuit, whose full ruling will decide whether hundreds of thousands of Venezuelans get their protections and work permits back — or whether the termination stands while the case continues.
