Judge Names Ex-U.S. Attorney to Probe Ethics in Trump Golf Course Case

U.S. District Judge Ana Reyes says she intends to appoint former D.C. U.S. Attorney Jessie Liu as a special master to investigate whether Trump administration lawyers misled the court over the East Potomac golf course renovation.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 8, 2026 · 4 min readFacebookX

A federal judge said on Saturday she would appoint a former top federal prosecutor to investigate whether Trump administration lawyers committed ethical violations in their defense of President Donald Trump's plans to close and renovate a historic public golf course in Washington. U.S. District Judge Ana Reyes named Jessie Liu, the former U.S. attorney for the District of Columbia, as a special master to examine the conduct of the departments of Justice and Interior during litigation over the fate of the East Potomac Golf Links course.

Liu served as the District of Columbia's top federal prosecutor from 2017 to 2020, during Trump's first term, when she oversaw the criminal case against the president's longtime friend and adviser Roger Stone. She previously served as an assistant U.S. attorney in the same office. Her appointment as special master would put a prosecutor with deep experience in the District's federal courts in charge of investigating the government's own lawyers.

Reyes named Liu one week after an October 30 hearing in which the judge ordered an ethics probe and expressed concerns that government attorneys misrepresented the Trump administration's intention to continue with the renovation of the East Potomac course despite her temporary order blocking the project. The investigation would examine whether Department of Justice or Department of the Interior counsel committed ethical violations in the matter through October 31, 2026, and would be conducted at the government's cost.

The lawsuit was filed in February by a nonprofit group, the DC Preservation League, and two Washington residents. It asserts that the planned reconstruction of East Potomac Park, including the golf course, violates environmental laws and a congressional preservation act. Preservationists and golfers who sued to block the changes worried that Trump's plan could change the identity of the course and remove a public and affordable place for golfers in the Washington area to play.

At the center of the dispute is Associate Attorney General Stanley Woodward, the Justice Department's third-ranking official. Reyes acted after learning that the government had been working to submit a redesign plan to the National Capital Planning Commission and had told the court the project was on hold, even as the administration moved ahead with plans to close the site. Several days before the final plan was filed on the docket, the government submitted a notice of its intention to close the park on November 15, consistent with the requirements of the existing preliminary injunction.

The administration intends to close the golf course and surrounding parkland on November 15 for a renovation expected to last 15 months. The plaintiffs began by alleging that Trump intended to destroy East Potomac Golf Course and build in its place a championship-style course suitable for professional tournaments. The plaintiffs have asked Reyes to temporarily block the closure, and a hearing in the case is scheduled for Friday, November 13.

Liu's appointment is not yet final. Reyes said she would give both sides until 10 a.m. on Friday, November 13, to respond to her order in writing. Any response by the government will be without prejudice to its argument, if any, that the court should not conduct an ethics inquiry at all. If the court appoints Liu, it will enter a detailed order doing so.

The move adds another chapter to the Trump administration's running battles with the judicial branch. A federal judge ordering an ethics investigation into the conduct of Justice Department lawyers, and proposing to put a former U.S. attorney in charge of it, is a rare step, and it signals how seriously Reyes views the question of whether the court was misled.

For the golfers and preservationists who brought the case, the ethics inquiry is a second front in a fight that began as a dispute over a golf course. East Potomac Golf Links sits on national parkland along the edge of the nation's capital, and its defenders say it is one of the few affordable public courses in the area. They have argued from the start that the renovation plan was pushed through without proper regard for environmental law or the park's protected status.

What happens next depends on the responses due on November 13 and on the hearing that follows. If Liu is appointed, she will conduct an inquiry into the conduct of government lawyers through Halloween, with the power to report back to the court on whether ethical rules were broken. The renovation itself remains on hold under the court's temporary order, and the planned November 15 closure is the next flashpoint in a case that has become as much about the integrity of the government's representations to a court as about the future of a golf course.
