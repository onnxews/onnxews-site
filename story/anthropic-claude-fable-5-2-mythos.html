Anthropic Unveils Claude Fable 5.2 and Mythos 5.2, Its Most Powerful Models Yet for Coding and Science

The new models are faster and cheaper than their predecessors, and in testing one of them built a working operating system from scratch with the help of more than 1,000 AI agents.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 10, 2026 · 4 min readFacebookX

Anthropic on Monday released two new AI models, Claude Fable 5.2 and Claude Mythos 5.2, aimed at the hardest technical work its customers do, from writing complex software to tackling open problems in science. The launch is the company's latest move in the race to build models that do more than answer questions, and it comes with a striking demonstration: in internal testing, Fable 5.2 coordinated more than 1,000 AI agents over eight days to build a working operating system from scratch.

The two models share the same underlying capabilities, but Anthropic is splitting access to them according to risk. Fable 5.2 is available to everyone, with safeguards that block potentially dangerous cybersecurity and biology requests. Mythos 5.2, by contrast, is limited to verified users working in cyber and life-science fields, a restriction that reflects the company's concern about how its most capable models could be misused. It is an unusually explicit admission that the same technology can be both a research tool and a risk, depending on who holds it.

On price, Anthropic is going in the opposite direction from the rest of the industry's recent trend. Standard pricing for the new models is 8 dollars per million input tokens and 40 dollars per million output tokens, and the company says responses come up to 30 percent faster than their predecessors while costing less to run. In a market where the most powerful models have usually also been the most expensive, a faster and cheaper flagship is a deliberate attempt to win over developers who run AI at scale.

The operating system experiment is the headline-grabbing part of the announcement. An Anthropic engineer asked Fable 5.2 to build an operating system from scratch, and the model coordinated more than 1,000 AI agents over eight days to produce a working system that could boot on a laptop, complete with windows, file storage and a login screen. Anthropic also says the models helped solve two previously open problems in black hole physics and designed three working electronic devices, results that, if they hold up, point to AI systems that can genuinely advance research rather than simply summarise it.

The release comes at a busy moment for Anthropic. The company reported 17 billion dollars in third-quarter revenue ahead of a widely expected public listing, and Reuters reported this week that Anthropic circulated details of a potential 69.3 billion dollar initial public offering to bankers before retracting them. The company has not confirmed IPO plans, but the episode underlines how quickly it has grown from a research lab into one of the most valuable private companies in the AI industry.

That growth is also attracting scrutiny. Anthropic was reported last week to have spent 3.4 billion dollars on gas generators to power its AI data centres, a sign of how expensive the infrastructure behind frontier models has become. The company's data centre buildout, its revenue growth and its new model releases are all part of the same story: the AI business is consolidating around a small number of companies with the money, the compute and the talent to compete at the frontier.

For developers and researchers, the practical question is what Fable 5.2 and Mythos 5.2 can do that earlier models could not. Anthropic is pitching them as tools for work that requires sustained reasoning over long periods: building large software systems, running multi-step scientific investigations and coordinating many agents at once. If the operating system experiment is any guide, the models are most impressive not when they answer a single question well, but when they keep a thousand moving parts organised over days of work.

The restricted access to Mythos 5.2 is likely to draw attention from policymakers as well as customers. Governments in the United States, Britain and France have all been pressing AI companies for stronger testing and clearer evidence about safety, and a model that is powerful enough to need gatekeeping is exactly the kind of system regulators want to understand. Anthropic's split release is, in effect, a bet that it can serve the most demanding users while keeping its most capable technology out of the wrong hands.

What happens next is a test of both the models and the strategy behind them. Developers will quickly find out whether the speed and price improvements hold up on real workloads, researchers will probe the black hole physics claims, and rivals will respond with their own releases. But the operating system built by a thousand agents is already the story people will remember from this launch, because it suggests a future in which the hardest engineering problems are solved not by a single brilliant model, but by a crowd of them working together.
