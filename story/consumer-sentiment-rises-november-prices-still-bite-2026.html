Consumer Sentiment Ticks Up, but 57% Say Prices Are Eating Their Living Standards

The University of Michigan's preliminary November reading beat forecasts, rising to 51.0 from 48.0 — but 57 percent of consumers say high prices have eroded their living standards, the most since May.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 8, 2026 · 3 min readFacebookX

American consumers felt a little better about the economy in early November, but the improvement came with a blunt warning about the cost of living. The University of Michigan's preliminary consumer sentiment index rose to 51.0 this month, up from a final reading of 48.0 in October, beating the 48.0 reading that economists polled by Reuters had expected.

The three-point gain was the first real lift in months, and it was broad enough to be noticed on Wall Street. Consumer sentiment ticked up about 6 percent in the preliminary read, said Joanne Hsu, the director of the Surveys of Consumers, and the improvement was concentrated among respondents who identify as political independents, with gains also among lower- and middle-wealth households.

The details underneath the headline were more encouraging still. The index of current economic conditions rose to 51.0 from 46.7, while the index of consumer expectations climbed to 50.9 from 48.8. At the same time, the survey's measure of what consumers expect inflation to be over the next year fell to 4.3 percent from 4.7 percent last month, the lowest since August, and expectations for inflation over the longer run slipped to 3.4 percent from 3.5 percent.

But the survey also captured the issue that dominated the midterm elections and still dominates household budgets. About 57 percent of those surveyed volunteered, without being prompted, that high prices have eroded their living standards, the highest share since May and up from 46 percent in February. Prices, not politics, remain the story for American families.

There is an important caveat to the numbers. The interviews ran from October 20 to November 2, so the survey closed the day before the midterm elections, and it captured neither the election results, nor the October jobs report released on November 6, nor the market turbulence that followed. The final November reading, due on November 20, will be the first one to include all three.

The survey also revealed a sharp divide by wealth. Sentiment fell 5 percent for consumers with the largest stock portfolios, a reminder that the week's market selloff hit the wealthy hardest, while sentiment was unchanged from November 2025 overall, a sign that the improvement, while real, has not restored confidence to anything like a healthy level.

Markets reacted quickly to the release. Gold moved off its post-payrolls lows after the data, recovering toward 4,050 dollars an ounce, and the figures offered a small counterweight to a week in which the jobs report and the Federal Reserve's rate outlook had pushed stocks lower. Reuters also reported that households are increasingly worried about a possible artificial intelligence bubble, a new anxiety layered on top of the old one about prices.

What the survey really measures is the central tension in the American economy as the holiday season approaches. Employers added 165,000 jobs in October and unemployment slipped to 4.1 percent, yet more than half of consumers say their standard of living is going backwards because of prices. That gap between a strong jobs market and squeezed households is the same gap voters punished at the ballot box on Tuesday.

The final November reading on November 20 will show whether the early improvement survived contact with the election results and the market selloff. For now, the message from consumers is mixed but clear: things feel slightly less bad than they did a month ago, and prices are still the problem that matters most.
