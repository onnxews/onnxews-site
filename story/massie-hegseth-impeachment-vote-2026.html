Massie Forces Hegseth Impeachment Vote as House Clock Runs to Wednesday

Thomas Massie refiled eight impeachment articles against Pete Hegseth on Monday, starting a two-day clock toward a House vote by Wednesday.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 10, 2026 · 4 min readFacebookX

Rep. Thomas Massie of Kentucky reintroduced articles of impeachment against Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth on Monday, forcing the House to decide within two days whether to impeach a sitting cabinet secretary over the war with Iran.

The 34-page privileged resolution lists eight articles accusing Hegseth of high crimes and misdemeanors. Three of the articles charge him with illegally waging war against Iran in violation of the War Powers Resolution, and others accuse him of waging war against Yemen without congressional authorization, ordering extrajudicial killings, failing to minimize civilian casualties, and suppressing free speech.

The free-speech charge centers on disciplinary action Hegseth took against Sen. Mark Kelly, a Democrat from Arizona, over comments urging military personnel to refuse what Kelly called illegal orders. The resolution also covers military operations in Venezuela, the Caribbean and the eastern Pacific.

Because Massie offered the resolution as a question of the privileges of the House, leaders have up to two legislative days to act on it, setting up a vote by Wednesday, November 11. The House can vote on the articles themselves, send them to committee, or vote to table them. Massie told the New York Post he believes passage is now more likely than it was before the election, saying, 'I think it's more likely to pass after the election than before the election.'

Massie first tried to force the vote in September, reading the resolution on the House floor for more than an hour on September 15. Speaker Mike Johnson then sent the House into its pre-election recess a day early, and the resolution expired without a vote. Massie's reintroduction on Monday restarted the clock.

The politics have shifted since September. Massie lost his Republican primary in May to a Trump-backed challenger and leaves Congress in January, giving him little left to lose. Democrats, meanwhile, won control of both chambers in the November 3 midterms, and House Democratic Leader Hakeem Jeffries has called himself a 'hard yes' on impeachment. House Democrats meet on Tuesday morning to decide how the caucus will vote.

The arithmetic is tight. The House currently has 218 Republicans, 214 Democrats, one independent and two vacancies, for 433 seated members. A simple majority of those voting is needed to impeach, which means 217 votes if everyone votes. If all 214 Democrats vote yes and Massie joins them, the count reaches 215, leaving two more votes to find among the remaining Republicans or the independent, Rep. Kevin Kiley of California.

There is a recent precedent for Republican defections. A war powers resolution on Iran passed 215-208 in June with four Republicans joining Democrats: Massie, Warren Davidson, Tom Barrett and Brian Fitzpatrick. Massie has said he expects 'a handful of Republicans' to back impeachment this time, and CNN reported that multiple Republican sources expect the measure to pass.

President Donald Trump has stood by his defense secretary. He told reporters on Monday that Massie was a 'stone cold loser' and said he would stand by Hegseth if he was impeached. Trump had said last week that Hegseth was 'doing a very good job' and that he was not considering replacing him. Hegseth, who has dismissed the effort and defended his record in a video released last week, is scheduled to hold a rare press conference at 8 a.m. on Tuesday, his first in 180 days, to respond to the impeachment effort.

Even if the House impeaches Hegseth, removing him would require a two-thirds vote in the Senate after a trial, or 67 of 100 senators, a far higher bar. Massie has said there is only one way he will drop the effort: if Hegseth resigns first. With a vote due by Wednesday, the lame-duck House is about to put every member on the record on a war that voters and the president himself have cited as a major reason for the Republican defeat.
