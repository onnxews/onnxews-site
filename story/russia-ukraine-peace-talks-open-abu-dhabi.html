Russia and Ukraine Open First U.S.-Brokered Peace Talks in Nine Months in Abu Dhabi

Trilateral negotiations began on Tuesday with American envoys saying the gaps have narrowed, but Moscow and Kyiv remain far apart on territory and security guarantees.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 10, 2026 · 5 min readFacebookX

Russian and Ukrainian officials met in Abu Dhabi on Tuesday for their first United States-brokered talks since February, as the Trump administration renewed its push to end a war that is now in its fifth year. The two-day session, which runs through Wednesday, opened with American envoy Jared Kushner telling delegates that recent contacts with both sides had really identified and narrowed the gaps, and that the United States had some good ideas on how to bridge their differences. But more than four and a half years after Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine, Moscow and Kyiv remain far apart on the terms of any settlement.

The talks are being held in a trilateral format, with the United States acting as mediator. Before the start of the main meeting, the Ukrainian delegation met separately with European, NATO and European Union representatives and the two American envoys, Kushner and Steve Witkoff. Ukraine's chief delegate, Rustem Umerov, the head of the country's Foreign Intelligence Service, said in his opening remarks that Kyiv was focused on achieving security guarantees and a lasting peace. Kushner said on social media that he and Witkoff were working on a negotiated solution that can deliver lasting peace and create the conditions for a sovereign, secure and prosperous Ukraine within a more stable European security environment.

The delegations are led by familiar faces. Ukraine's team is again led by Umerov, joined by Kyrylo Budanov, the head of President Volodymyr Zelenskyy's office, Davyd Arakhamia, leader of the presidential ruling party's parliamentary faction, and Serhii Kyslytsia, first deputy head of the presidential office. Russia's delegation is again led by Kremlin aide Vladimir Medinsky, who has represented Moscow in negotiations since the first direct peace talks with Ukraine in Istanbul in March 2022. He is joined by Deputy Foreign Ministers Sergei Ryabkov and Mikhail Galuzin, military intelligence chief Igor Kostyukov and senior military planner Yuri Zhigarlovsky.

The central question in Abu Dhabi is what security Ukraine would receive after any ceasefire. Kyiv wants firm guarantees that Russia will not launch another invasion if a ceasefire or peace agreement is reached, and Ukrainian officials have discussed arrangements modeled on Article 5 of NATO's founding treaty, under which an attack on one member is treated as an attack on all. Under the proposed deal, Ukraine would get an end to the war, security guarantees and a roadmap to accession to the European Union. Russia, meanwhile, wants its own security concerns included in a wider agreement, and Foreign Minister Sergei Lavrov underlined Moscow's preference for negotiating a broad European security framework before tackling the specifics of the war.

European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen voiced the opposite view on Monday, saying wider security talks should come only after a ceasefire in Ukraine. National security advisers from the United Kingdom, France, Germany and Italy, as well as officials from NATO and the European Commission, are in Abu Dhabi for separate meetings with the Ukrainian and American delegations. Kremlin spokesperson Dmitry Peskov said on Monday that Europe has its own channels of communication with Kyiv and Washington, while reaffirming that the talks are a trilateral negotiation involving Russia and Ukraine, with the United States acting as mediator.

The territorial dispute remains the hardest issue. Russian President Vladimir Putin wants Ukraine to pull its troops back from Donetsk, Luhansk, Zaporizhzhia and Kherson, the four regions Moscow illegally annexed in September 2022 but never fully captured. Putin is still making maximalist demands previously rejected by Kyiv, including Russian control over the entire Donbas region, a reduction in the size of Ukraine's military and the abandonment of Kyiv's ambitions to join NATO. Zelenskyy, who was visiting Denmark on Tuesday, rejects Putin's demands to voluntarily cede territory that Russia has not been able to take on the battlefield.

The war has continued unabated even as diplomats talk. Russian forces hold about a fifth of Ukraine's territory, and Moscow has escalated its aerial attacks in recent weeks. At least 55 people were killed last Monday when Russia dropped several bombs on a school building used as a shelter and aid center in Zaporizhzhia, marking one of the deadliest single incidents of the war to date. With the full-scale invasion now in its fifth year and gains on the battlefield remaining difficult to come by for either side, the human cost of the fighting continues to mount.

This is the fourth round of trilateral talks, and all previous rounds have ended without significant breakthrough. The United States postponed a round that was expected to take place in March due to the war in Iran. The White House has described itself as cautiously optimistic about this week's talks, and American officials have indicated that private discussions have revealed some room for movement, even though Russia and Ukraine continue to maintain sharply different public positions. No immediate peace agreement is expected from the Abu Dhabi meeting.

The two days in Abu Dhabi will test whether that limited room for movement can produce anything concrete. The immediate objective is not necessarily to sign a final peace agreement, but to establish whether the two sides can agree on the framework of one, starting with security guarantees and a ceasefire. For Ukraine, the talks are a chance to lock in the protections that would make any peace durable. For Russia, they are a chance to secure the territorial and security concessions it has sought since the war began. For the United States, they are a test of whether its mediation can succeed where three previous rounds have not.
