Judge Extends Block on Trump's Triumphal Arch for Two More Weeks

U.S. District Judge Tanya Chutkan extended the restraining order blocking construction of the proposed 250-foot arch, keeping the project frozen until December 3 while she weighs a longer-term injunction.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 19, 2026 · 4 min readFacebookX

A federal judge on Wednesday extended an earlier ruling that blocked President Donald Trump's administration from constructing a proposed 250-foot arch near Arlington National Cemetery, handing the White House another setback in its effort to leave a lasting architectural mark on the nation's capital. In a brief order, U.S. District Judge Tanya Chutkan extended for another two weeks a restraining order she issued on November 5, according to Reuters.

The earlier order was set to expire on Thursday. Chutkan's extension keeps the project on hold until December 3, or until she rules on a pending request for a more permanent preliminary injunction. She said the 14-day extension was warranted because nothing has significantly changed in the litigation in the two weeks since she issued the original order.

The 250-foot arch, shown in a rendering to include a flame held by a 60-foot gold winged female figure and flanked by gold flying eagles, would be situated near the Potomac River between Arlington National Cemetery and the Lincoln Memorial. It is one of several projects Trump has undertaken as he remakes the city in his second term, alongside a White House ballroom project, a renovation of the Lincoln Memorial's reflection pool and a proposed championship-level golf course two miles from the White House.

The legal fight moved quickly. The District of Columbia and Virginia filed suit on October 31, and Chutkan issued her original order on November 5, shortly after a hearing of the National Capital Planning Commission ended with the Trump-heavy commission giving the arch its final approval in an 8-4 vote, despite dozens of witnesses who opposed the construction.

Chutkan said the two-week extension would allow her more time to consider a request by plaintiffs including Virginia and the District of Columbia for a longer-term injunction halting the project. A preliminary injunction, if granted, would keep construction blocked while the lawsuits play out, rather than for a matter of days.

The arch is the most visible symbol of Trump's building ambitions in Washington, and the courts have repeatedly slowed them. The rulings highlight the judiciary's pushback against construction projects the president has pursued in the capital during his second term, from the ballroom to the golf course.

For now, no ground can be broken, and the project stays frozen while the judge weighs the longer-term injunction. The administration has not laid out a public timetable for the project beyond the court fight, and the arch's fate now rests on Chutkan's next ruling.

The case will return to Chutkan's courtroom as the December 3 date approaches. If she grants the preliminary injunction, the arch could be stalled for months while the underlying lawsuits proceed. If she denies it, the administration could move quickly to break ground on a project that has already become one of the most contested building plans in the capital's recent history.

The fight over the arch is also a fight over who decides what the capital looks like. The planning commission's approval came over the objections of dozens of witnesses, and the lawsuits by the District and Virginia challenge the project itself. Whatever Chutkan decides next, the arch has already become a test of how far a president can reshape Washington on his own.

Why it matters: The extension matters less for its length than for what it signals: the courts are willing to keep Trump's signature building projects frozen while the legal challenges run their course. With the order now running to December 3, the White House faces a longer wait for its most symbolic project, and the judge's next decision, on a preliminary injunction, could stall the arch for far longer. The arch fight has become a proxy for the broader struggle over how much a president can remake the capital without the usual checks.
