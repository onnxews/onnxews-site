Senate Passes Sweeping Energy Permitting Bill in 62-33 Vote

The Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act would speed approvals for power plants, transmission lines, mines and data centers, and now faces an uncertain future in the House.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 20, 2026 · 5 min readFacebookX

The U.S. Senate voted 62-33 on Thursday to pass a sweeping bipartisan energy permitting bill aimed at speeding approvals for large projects such as power plants, transmission lines, mines and data centers, marking Congress's most significant effort in years to ease bottlenecks blamed for rising energy costs.

The bill, the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act, now heads to the Republican-controlled House of Representatives. President Donald Trump has said he will sign it if it reaches his desk, but the legislation faces an uncertain future in the House and a shrinking legislative window. If it is not adopted by both chambers, it would have to be reintroduced when the 120th Congress takes over in January.

At its core, the bill would impose tighter deadlines on federal environmental reviews, accelerate planning and construction of transmission lines, and limit the circumstances under which permits can be revoked or challenged in court. The goal, its authors say, is to cut years off the time it takes to build the power plants, grid upgrades and mines the country needs as electricity demand surges, driven in large part by artificial intelligence.

Data centers sit at the center of the deal. The bill would make it easier for developers to get the power they need to run the facilities, but it also makes them pay their own way, charging extra for access to the electric grid and forcing developers to cover the full cost of any upgrades their projects require. Backers say the provision answers one of the loudest complaints of the past year, that ordinary households are subsidizing the AI buildout through higher electricity bills.

The vote was the product of months of painstaking negotiations and an unlikely coalition. The bill was first introduced in September and drew support from the oil and gas industry and the White House as well as clean energy developers and climate activists. Most Republican senators backed it, joined by 14 Democrats and one independent, and Senator Shelley Moore Capito of West Virginia, a co-author, said afterward, “Tonight, the passage of permitting reform in the Senate finally became reality and we took a major step towards addressing the affordability concerns of the American people.”

Senate Majority Leader John Thune said the legislation “cuts through decades of red tape that has made it unnecessarily difficult and costly to build things in America,” and promised it would create better roads and more jobs. He said on the Senate floor that the slow permitting process affects “everything from drinking water to bridge and road repairs,” adding, “It's estimated that this bill will save the average household hundreds of dollars each year.”

Supporters on the climate side pointed to the emissions case for building faster. Senator Sheldon Whitehouse, Democrat of Rhode Island, wrote that the bill is “a big step forward in our fight against climate change,” citing 1.5 gigatons of emissions it would lower through 2040, adding, “That's the same as taking nearly 35 million gas-powered cars off the road.” The bill also bolsters clean energy sources like wind and solar, a point its backers used to hold together a coalition that spans the energy industry and the environmental movement.

But the bill drew sharp criticism from tribal groups, environmentalists and anti-data-center campaigners, who argue it strips away protections to accelerate fossil fuel and data center development. Senator Jeff Merkley, Democrat of Oregon, said it would “put the pedal to the floor” on data center development while running roughshod over the sovereignty of Native American tribes. He said of tribal communities, “This bill ran right over the top of them. They are profoundly insulted.” The environmental advocacy nonprofit Oil Change International called the legislation a “fossil fuel giveaway,” and 17 Democratic state attorneys general wrote a letter seeking revisions to protect laws they say the bill could weaken, including the National Environmental Policy Act and the Clean Water Act.

The politics around data centers have shifted sharply in the past year. Data centers were widely rebuked in the November midterm elections, as dozens of local data center moratoriums passed at the county and city level across the United States, and the bill's critics warned it would accelerate exactly the kind of development voters just rejected. Its supporters counter that the country cannot meet rising demand for power, or compete in artificial intelligence, without building faster, and that the bill's requirement that developers pay for grid upgrades is the fairest way to do it.

What happens next is far from settled. The House must still take up the bill, and with only a short lame-duck session left, its champions will have to move quickly. If the House does not act, years of stop-start negotiations over permitting reform will have to begin again in a new Congress, with a different balance of power after the midterm elections.

Why it matters: This vote matters because it is the first time in years that Congress has come close to rewriting the rules for building energy infrastructure, and it does so at the moment electricity demand is rising fastest. The bill's fate in the House will show whether the bipartisan deal struck in the Senate can survive a compressed lame-duck calendar, and whether the requirement that data centers pay for their own grid upgrades becomes the template for how the AI buildout is financed. If it stalls, the permitting logjam that both parties blame for high energy costs will remain in place into the new Congress.
