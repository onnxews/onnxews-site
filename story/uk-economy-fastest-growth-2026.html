Britain's Economy Grew 0.7% in the Third Quarter, Its Fastest Pace in Two and a Half Years

Britain's economy grew 0.7% between July and September, its fastest quarterly pace in more than two years, as artificial intelligence spending drove a surge in computer programming and business services.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 12, 2026 · 6 min readFacebookX

Britain's economy grew at the fastest rate in two-and-a-half years in the third quarter of 2026, helped by a boost from artificial intelligence and extending a strong first half, official figures showed on Thursday. Gross domestic product expanded by 0.7% in the July-to-September period, in line with a Reuters poll of economists, but faster than its 0.5% growth in the second quarter and the strongest calendar-quarter growth since the first quarter of 2024, the Office for National Statistics said.

The annual growth rate picked up to 1.9% in the third quarter, the strongest reading since the fourth quarter of 2024, and also in line with the 1.9% median forecast in the Reuters poll. On a rolling three-month basis, growth was the fastest since May. The economy has now grown in nine straight three-month periods, and British growth in the third quarter was the second-fastest in the Group of Seven advanced economies, just behind the United States.

"The economy grew robustly in the third quarter almost entirely due to strong and widespread growth in the services sector," ONS chief economist Grant Fitzner said. The services sector, which makes up the majority of Britain's economy, grew 0.9% on the quarter, with business-facing services up 1.0% and consumer-facing services up 0.5%. Eleven of the 14 services subsectors contributed positively to growth, with the largest contributions from administrative and support services, information and communication, and professional, scientific and technical activities.

The standout driver was technology. Information and communication grew by 2.1% in the quarter, mainly driven by increases in computer programming, consultancy and related activities, which rose by 4.4%. "Within services, computer programming again increased strongly, as it has all year, registering its fastest growth in the last three years. We continue to see evidence that AI linked businesses helped to boost this sector" Fitzner said. The ONS noted that computer programming has been the largest driver of GDP growth across 2026, with evidence that many of the businesses reporting the largest increases in turnover are involved in activities related to artificial intelligence and cloud computing.

The rest of the economy was more mixed. The production sector increased by 0.1% in the quarter, with manufacturing up 0.9% while mining and quarrying fell 2.2% and electricity, gas, steam and air conditioning supply fell 2.1%. The construction sector fell by 0.7%, with new work down 1.6% and private housing new work down 4.7%. On the spending side, growth was mainly driven by household consumption, which rose 0.6%, and gross fixed capital formation, which rose 1.7%.

Monthly figures published alongside the quarterly estimate showed the economy grew by 0.2% in September 2026, after showing no growth in August, revised down from 0.1% growth in the previous publication, and unrevised growth of 0.4% in July. Real GDP per head increased by 0.7% in the quarter and is up by 1.7% compared with the same quarter a year ago. Nominal GDP increased by 1.4% on the quarter and is now 4.3% higher than a year earlier.

The figures beat the Bank of England's own forecast for the quarter, and the central bank now expects GDP to expand 1.6% this year, much stronger than the 1.2% forecast by the government's Office for Budget Responsibility after finance minister John Healey's October 28 budget, which did not factor in some recent stronger GDP data and upward revisions to past numbers. The Bank of England attributed some of the strength to spending on artificial intelligence and a small, temporary boost from Healey's fiscal plan. Prime Minister Andy Burnham welcomed the figures, saying the economy was growing despite global events pushing up costs at home.

Not every part of the release was strong. Business investment grew just 0.4% on the quarter, half of what economists had expected in the Reuters poll and the weakest growth since the start of the year. The ONS also cautioned that the third-quarter figure includes non-monetary gold and other precious metals, which it described as an erratic series, and that early estimates of GDP are subject to revision in either direction. The next GDP release is due on December 22, 2026.

Economists expect growth to slow in the final quarter as higher energy bills and rising inflation squeeze household spending power. Official measures showed a fall in retail sales volumes in September, and consumer confidence suffered its sharpest month-on-month fall since April 2020 before the budget. There have also been signs of weakening consumer and business demand in the face of prolonged high energy prices and rising interest rates. The Bank of England voted last week to raise interest rates to cool inflationary pressures, a move that will add to borrowing costs for households and businesses in the months ahead.

For now, though, the numbers tell a story of an economy defying predictions of a slowdown made after the Iran war began. Britain's economy has shown surprising resilience in the first half of 2026 after expanding by 0.6% in the first quarter, and the third-quarter figures extend that run. Whether the AI-driven services boom can keep carrying growth while consumers pull back will decide whether the acceleration lasts into 2027, and the December 22 revision will be the first check on whether the strongest quarter in two and a half years survives closer inspection.

Why it matters: The 0.7% figure matters because it breaks the story Britain has been telling about itself all year. After the Iran war began, economists predicted a slowdown; instead the economy accelerated, and the acceleration came from the same force reshaping every other market in this batch: spending on artificial intelligence. Computer programming grew at its fastest pace in three years, and the ONS says AI-linked businesses are behind much of it. The caveats are real — business investment is weak, consumers are squeezed, and the Bank of England has just raised rates to 4% — but a government that entered the autumn expecting a slowdown now has the strongest quarter in two and a half years to defend its budget with. The test is whether the AI boom in services can outrun the drag from higher rates and energy bills in the final quarter.
