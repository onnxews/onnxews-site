White House Pulls FDA Nominee Heidi Overton on Eve of Senate Vote

The White House pulled Dr. Heidi Overton's nomination to run the FDA on Tuesday, the day before a Senate committee vote, after bipartisan pushback over vaccines, abortion and vaping left her without the support to advance.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 19, 2026 · 4 min readFacebookX

The White House has pulled the nomination of Dr. Heidi Overton to lead the Food and Drug Administration, ending her bid for the job on the eve of a Senate committee vote. A senior administration official told Axios on Tuesday that Overton “lacks the support on the Hill to move forward with her confirmation,” and a White House official confirmed to the New York Post that the nomination was pulled.

Overton, a physician and deputy director of the White House Domestic Policy Council, was nominated by President Trump in August to replace Marty Makary, the previous FDA commissioner, who resigned in May after irking the president by slow-walking flavored e-cigarette approvals. The agency had spent three months without a permanent leader before the White House shifted its focus to Overton, and Kyle Diamantas, who hails from the agency's food regulation division, has been at the helm in an interim capacity since Makary departed.

The Senate Health, Education, Labor and Pensions Committee had been scheduled to vote on her nomination on Wednesday. Overton faced bipartisan pushback from members of Congress, and her confirmation hearing in September drew contention over vaccines, abortions, flavored vapes and whether President Trump is capable of being wrong.

Vaccines were the sharpest flashpoint. Sen. Bill Cassidy, the Louisiana Republican who chairs the health committee, had expressed “strong concerns” about Overton's nomination, citing her “lack of managerial experience” and “active role” in efforts to change childhood vaccine guidelines. “That alone is almost disqualifying,” Cassidy wrote on X.

Overton conceded during the hearing that vaccines approved by the FDA, as well as the abortion pill mifepristone, are safe and effective. But she stopped short of admitting that Trump has been wrong when dispensing medical information out of line with what scientific evidence shows. She had previously described mifepristone as “dangerous to women”, and at the hearing she noted the pill was FDA-approved, having “met its safety and efficacy standards,” and said she would “abide by the statutory standards set out for safety and efficacy.”

The opposition came from both ends of the Republican coalition. Anti-Overton signs appeared all over Capitol Hill in recent days, including one reading “Say no to Heidi Vaperton,” with a crossed-out photo of Overton holding a vape. Her critics cited her alleged “big tobacco ties” and support for vaping, while the anti-abortion group SBA Pro-Life America said it intends to secure “commitments on abortion-drug policy” from whomever the administration taps to replace her.

Sen. Ed Markey, the veteran Democratic lawmaker from Massachusetts, told Fierce Pharma ahead of the vote that he would “vote against her nomination, and I urge my colleagues to do the same.” “The FDA needs a leader who will stand up for science and for our kids' safety, not one who stood by while both were undermined,” he added. Robert Steinbrook of Public Citizen urged the committee to reject the nomination, saying Overton “has done nothing to dispel the well-founded reasons to oppose her candidacy.”

The withdrawal leaves the FDA without a permanent commissioner and extends the uncertainty around its leadership, analysts from Citi said, calling the move a modest negative for sentiment in the biopharma sector. Republicans were discussing Sen. Roger Marshall of Kansas, a doctor who chairs the Senate's Make America Healthy Again caucus and lost his re-election earlier this month, as a potential replacement, according to multiple outlets.

The White House's decision also lays bare a split inside the administration's own health movement. A senior administration official told Axios: “We need to go in a new direction at FDA, and everybody fully supports Secretary Kennedy's vision to Make America Healthy Again and get back to a more populist and common-sense approach to health in America”

Why it matters: The failed nomination matters because the FDA has now gone months without a permanent leader, and the fight over Overton exposed divisions that cut across party lines, from vaccine policy to abortion to vaping. With Democrats set to take the Senate in January, the next nominee will face an even harder path, and the agency's leadership question is now tangled up in the administration's broader health agenda. Whoever takes the job inherits an agency under strain and a confirmation process that has become a proxy war over science itself.
