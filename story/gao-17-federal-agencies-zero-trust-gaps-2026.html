Watchdog Finds 17 of 22 Federal Agencies Falling Short on Cybersecurity Planning

The Government Accountability Office found that only five of 22 major civilian agencies had fully addressed federal zero trust cybersecurity planning guidance as of August, and it issued 40 recommendations to the rest.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 22, 2026 · 6 min readFacebookX

A sweeping federal watchdog report has found that 17 of 22 major civilian agencies failed to fully address key cybersecurity planning guidance, warning that the shortcomings could leave government systems more vulnerable to cyberattacks and operational disruptions. The Government Accountability Office released its findings this week, identifying gaps at the Department of Homeland Security, the Department of Justice, the Department of State, the Department of the Treasury and the Department of Health and Human Services, among others.

The report, GAO-27-108661, published on Nov. 18, examined whether agencies had incorporated selected federal guidance into their plans for implementing zero trust architecture, a cybersecurity approach that requires users, devices and systems to be verified before they can access protected networks. Only five of the 22 agencies had fully addressed the selected guidance as of August 2026, prompting the GAO to issue 40 recommendations to the remaining 17. Thirteen agencies agreed with the recommendations, while four neither agreed nor disagreed. The Nuclear Regulatory Commission, one of the four, provided documentation showing it had already addressed both of its recommendations, which the GAO verified.

The five agencies whose plans fully addressed the selected guidance were the Departments of Labor, Transportation and Veterans Affairs, the General Services Administration and the Social Security Administration. The 17 with gaps include the departments of Agriculture, Commerce, Education, Energy, Health and Human Services, Homeland Security, Housing and Urban Development, the Interior, Justice, State and the Treasury, along with the Environmental Protection Agency, NASA, the Nuclear Regulatory Commission, the National Science Foundation, the Office of Personnel Management and the Small Business Administration.

The gaps were not uniform. The Department of Health and Human Services received six recommendations, the most of any agency reviewed, after investigators found that it lacked a documented enterprise-wide zero trust implementation plan covering all its information systems. Although individual divisions were pursuing cybersecurity improvements, the GAO warned that the absence of a unified strategy could prevent department leadership from maintaining a coherent view of its security modernization efforts. HHS agreed with the recommendations and said it planned to develop an actionable department-wide strategy by the second quarter of fiscal year 2027.

The Department of Commerce received four recommendations after investigators identified shortcomings in its cybersecurity planning documentation. Commerce officials told the GAO that staffing losses had significantly constrained the department's zero trust working group, limiting its ability to coordinate improvements and maintain comprehensive documentation. At the Department of Homeland Security, investigators found that the department had not fully documented cybersecurity maturity levels across all its high-value assets and high-impact systems, with officials attributing the incomplete assessments partly to lengthy government shutdowns during the preceding fiscal year. The Justice Department similarly cited resource constraints for incomplete assessments of critical systems, while State and Treasury were among the agencies that had only partially documented cybersecurity maturity levels.

Investigators also found that 11 agencies had only partially documented procedures for identifying actors within their IT environments, including certain non-person entities such as software applications and service accounts. The watchdog warned that incomplete identification procedures could hinder efforts to ensure only authorized users and systems can access government resources, and that incomplete assessments could leave agency leadership without the visibility necessary to determine where additional cybersecurity resources are needed. "Agencies would therefore be at increased risk of cyberattacks that could interrupt operations or otherwise compromise agency resources," the GAO warned. "Absent a full implementation of ZTA requirements and guidance, agencies will lack an appropriate roadmap to fully incorporate and maintain an effective zero trust architecture," it concluded.

The findings come years after the Biden administration directed federal agencies to adopt zero trust cybersecurity principles through a 2021 executive order, followed by additional guidance in 2022 and 2024. Despite those directives, the GAO found that 17 of 22 agencies still had not fully addressed selected cybersecurity planning requirements and guidance as of August 2026. The report is careful about what it does and does not show: it assesses planning documents against selected guidance, not whether agencies were actually breached, and it does not mean the 17 agencies have no zero trust protections in place.

A senior cybersecurity official offered a more upbeat picture the day after the report's release. Speaking at a GovExec Cyber Resilience Summit on Nov. 19, Greg Bird, the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency's deputy associate director, said federal agencies have made significant progress deploying zero trust capabilities, but must now demonstrate that those investments are reducing risk. Bird reported that as of September, 90 percent of agencies covered by the Chief Financial Officers Act had begun implementing zero trust and were "well on their way towards an optimal level of deployment." At the Defense Department, 20 of 24 major IT programs had begun adopting zero trust as of 2025, he said.

Bird also urged agencies to move beyond measuring whether zero trust tools have been deployed and toward evaluating whether those capabilities can detect threats, limit attackers' movements and protect critical operations. "We need to stop proving that controls exist and start proving that they actually work," he said. "The ultimate measure of cyber resilience is not whether or not our adversary gets in; it's how little they can accomplish, how quickly we can contain them, and how effectively we can continue our mission." Legacy technology, funding constraints, workforce shortages and resistance to new security procedures continue to complicate the work, Bird said, and he acknowledged that significant staff departures at CISA in recent years had affected the agency's ability to support its federal partners.

The report lands in a week when federal cybersecurity is under unusual scrutiny. The FBI is investigating an apparent breach of its National Crime Information Center database, and the Trump administration has fired its national cyber director after a week of technology outages. The GAO's 40 recommendations are all listed as open, and the watchdog says it will update their status when it confirms what the agencies have done.

Why it matters: The GAO report is about paperwork, but the paperwork is the plan, and the plan is what tells an agency which of its systems matter most and who is allowed to touch them. Seventeen of the government's largest civilian agencies still cannot show a complete version of that plan five years after they were ordered to build one, and the agencies themselves blame staffing losses, shutdowns and tight budgets rather than disagreeing with the findings. Coming in the same week as the FBI database breach, the report underlines a gap between the tools the government has bought and the discipline of knowing whether they work. The next test is not another audit but whether the 40 open recommendations actually get closed.
