'How to Rob a Bank' Opens in Theaters With Hoult and Kravitz Leading a Viral Heist Crew

David Leitch's new heist thriller follows social-media-savvy robbers who broadcast their jobs to millions. It opens Friday in the U.S. and the U.K.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 13, 2026 · 4 min readFacebookX

"How to Rob a Bank" opens in theaters on Friday, November 13, in the United States and the United Kingdom.

The film is directed by David Leitch, the filmmaker behind "Bullet Train" and "The Fall Guy" and a co-creator of "John Wick," from a script by Mark Bianculli. It stars Nicholas Hoult as Ryan, a robber known as "the Wolf," and Zoë Kravitz as Reagan Gardner, a hacker working under house arrest.

The crew's twist is the broadcast. Ryan's gang wears animal masks and streams its heists online, presenting itself as a modern Robin Hood outfit, and draws an audience of about 30 million unique viewers, according to Radio Times. The attention turns them into folk heroes — and into targets.

John C. Reilly plays the FBI agent hunting them, John West, while Anna Sawai, Pete Davidson and Rhenzy Feliz round out the cast. Christian Slater appears in a cameo as a bank manager.

Leitch has described the film as a heist movie built for the social-media era, a story about criminals who understand that the audience is part of the job. The film was originally scheduled for release in September before moving to November.

Early reviews are mixed. The film holds a score of 33 percent on Rotten Tomatoes as of Friday, with a Metascore of 47, while Radio Times gave it three stars out of five, calling it highly watchable but uneven.

Hoult's performance is a particular challenge: much of it happens behind a mask, forcing the actor to carry scenes with his voice and body language rather than his face. Kravitz's hacker is the crew's technical brain, plotting the jobs from confinement.

The film is rated R and runs two hours and two minutes. It is the latest test of whether a mid-budget, star-led action film can still draw crowds to theaters in a year dominated by franchise titles.

For Leitch, whose recent work has leaned on large-scale action, the film is a return to a smaller, talkier crime story with a contemporary hook — and for Hoult, coming off a busy run of leading roles, it is another chance to carry a studio film.

Whether audiences show up will depend on the same thing the film is about: attention. The robbers in the story go viral on purpose, and the movie's opening weekend will show whether a heist film about going viral can go viral itself.

The film's central joke is also its premise: the robbers are famous because they perform. Wearing animal masks and streaming every job, they turn each heist into a show, and the audience's attention becomes part of the haul. It is a setup that lets Leitch do what he does best — stage elaborate action — while saying something about a world in which crime, like everything else, is content.

For Amazon MGM Studios, the release is a test of the studio's theatrical strategy. The film is a mid-budget original with stars but no franchise behind it, the kind of movie that used to be a reliable box office bet and now has to fight for screens. A strong opening would show that audiences still turn out for a well-made heist film; a weak one would suggest the genre now belongs to streaming.

Why it matters: The film matters as a test of the mid-budget theatrical action movie, a category that has struggled as studios chase franchise tentpoles. Leitch's name and a cast led by Hoult and Kravitz give it a shot, and the social-media angle is genuinely of the moment — a heist story where the audience is part of the crime. What to watch is the opening weekend: a strong debut would show there is still room for star-led original action on the big screen, while a soft one would confirm how hard that space has become.
