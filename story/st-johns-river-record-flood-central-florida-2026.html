St. Johns River Smashes 73-Year Flood Record as Central Florida Braces for Weeks of High Water

The river near Cocoa stood at 18.76 feet on Saturday morning, more than a foot above a record set in 1953, and the slow-moving crest is still heading for Sanford, DeLand and Astor.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 22, 2026 · 6 min readFacebookX

The St. Johns River held at record levels near Cocoa, Florida, on Saturday morning, leaving neighborhoods west of the Space Coast under water and pushing a slow-moving flood crest north toward Seminole, Volusia and Lake counties after days of torrential rain. The U.S. Geological Survey gauge on the river near Cocoa read a provisional 18.76 feet at 6:15 a.m. EST Saturday, more than a foot above the previous record of 17.55 feet, set in October 1953. The National Weather Service forecast the river to remain steady near 18.8 feet through the weekend and into early next week, with the river flood warning in effect until further notice.

The flooding followed a multiday deluge that was strongest along the Brevard and Volusia coasts. A National Weather Service public information statement issued Friday listed 72-hour rainfall totals of 24.26 inches one mile north of Cocoa Beach, 22.49 inches three miles south of Titusville and 22 inches on Merritt Island. Melbourne International Airport recorded 5.34 inches on Thursday alone, breaking the Nov. 19 daily record of 2.80 inches set in 1987, and its November total reached 22.62 inches by Friday afternoon, against a normal of just 1.70 inches by that date. Daytona Beach set a daily rainfall record on Friday with 2.84 inches, breaking the old mark of 1.21 inches set in 1952. On Friday, the Weather Prediction Center placed the Space Coast and Volusia County under a high risk, level 4 of 4, for excessive rainfall, the highest category it issues.

The water has forced residents from their homes and left some neighborhoods reachable only by boat. Florida Fish and Wildlife Conservation Commission officers, working with the Brevard County Sheriff's Office, the Florida Highway Patrol and the Florida National Guard, spent Friday pulling residents out of flooded homes by airboat. About 80 officers and 13 airboats had been deployed to the Cocoa area, and officials said about 120 people had been rescued in Brevard County and well over 150 in Volusia County as of Friday afternoon. Florida Army National Guard soldiers conducted high-water rescue missions in Rockledge on Thursday, evacuating residents affected by severe flooding. Brevard County Sheriff Wayne Ivey said at a news conference Thursday that no deaths had been attributed to the flooding. "While we may have substantial property damage, we've got vehicles that are damaged, businesses that are damaged, right now we have absolutely no one that has lost their life from this storm," Ivey said. "Very minimal injuries across the board."

The worst of the flooding is still moving. Because the St. Johns flows north across an exceptionally flat landscape, the flood is traveling slowly: the St. Johns River Water Management District said in a Nov. 20 statement that the river descends only about 1 inch per mile, allowing levels to stay elevated long after the rain ends. Upstream of Lake Harney near Geneva, the river stood at a provisional 12.12 feet at 6:30 a.m. Saturday, already in major flood, with the weather service forecasting a crest near 13 feet on Sunday afternoon, above the previous record of 12.7 feet. At 10.8 feet, the weather service said, water covers State Road 46 near Jungle Road and Prevatt Road, and standing water on secondary roads near the river deepens to more than 3 feet in some areas. The Florida Department of Transportation has closed State Road 46 between Sanford and Mims.

Downstream communities are bracing for the crest to reach them over the coming days. Near Sanford, where the river was at 6.8 feet on Friday evening, the weather service forecast major flooding by midweek. Near DeLand, the river read 5.04 feet early Saturday and was forecast to reach about 6 feet early next week, above the 5.3-foot level at which the agency said major flooding affects many structures and marinas. At Astor, the river reached 4 feet on Friday evening, well above its 2.3-foot flood stage, and was forecast to rise to 4.2 feet by midweek. The weather service said flooding at that level is long-lasting, "with levels taking weeks to months to return below flood stage," and water rescues will be necessary. Seminole County Emergency Manager Alan Harris said Thursday that flooding along the river in his county could last through mid-December.

The district's flood-control infrastructure is holding back some of the water. Its reservoirs, canals and water-control structures in the Upper St. Johns River Basin were temporarily storing about 60 billion gallons of water that would otherwise be flowing downstream, the district said. Even so, the water management district's own outlook, shared Friday, put the time the river would spend at or above moderate flood stage at two to three months near Cocoa, one to two months above Lake Harney and at Astor, about a month near DeLand and two to three weeks near Sanford.

Emergency declarations and closures spread across the region on Friday. Volusia County declared a local state of emergency covering the whole county, effective until at least Thursday, and the cities of New Smyrna Beach, Port Orange and Edgewater also declared emergencies. Daytona Beach Mayor Derrick Henry declared a seven-day local state of emergency. Flooding closed parts of State Road 46, U.S. 1 North, Barnes Boulevard and portions of A1A near Patrick Space Force Base, and portions of International Speedway Boulevard, LPGA Boulevard, Dunlawton Avenue, Spruce Creek Road and Maytown Road in Volusia County. Brevard County opened shelters at the Walter Butler Community Center in Cocoa and the Joseph N. Davis Community Center in Melbourne for the general public, people with special medical needs and families with pets. Brightline suspended its passenger train service between Orlando and West Palm Beach on Friday because of flooding in Orange and Brevard counties.

The flooding also wiped out a Thanksgiving-week tradition. Flooding in Volusia County led to the cancellation of the annual Daytona Turkey Run, which had been scheduled for Nov. 25-29. "The safety of our show cars, vendors, sponsors, spectators, and staff is our top priority," organizers wrote in a statement. "Rising water levels have made it impossible to hold the event safely." All pre-purchased spectator tickets will be automatically refunded by next Friday. Mostly dry weather is expected Sunday and Monday, but forecasters said another low could form over the Gulf and move across the peninsula Tuesday and Wednesday, bringing increasing rain chances. Any additional rainfall will lead to more river rises, the weather service said, and officials urged residents never to drive through flooded roads or around barricades.

Why it matters: The St. Johns flood is a reminder that a hurricane is not the only way Florida floods, and that the damage can outlast the rain by months. The river's flat, slow course means the crest now passing Cocoa will still be working its way through Sanford, DeLand and Astor well into December, and the water management district's own outlook keeps parts of the river above flood stage into the new year. For the families already rescued by airboat and the businesses watching the water rise toward Thanksgiving week, the question is no longer how much rain fell, but how long the river takes to give it back.
