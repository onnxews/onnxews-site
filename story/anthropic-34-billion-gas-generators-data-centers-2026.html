Anthropic Reportedly Spent $3.4 Billion on Gas Generators to Power Its AI Data Centers

The Claude maker took over 1.7 gigawatts of Caterpillar generator orders from a Texas supplier, the Financial Times reported — the largest known single purchase of power equipment by an AI lab.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 9, 2026 · 4 min readFacebookX

Anthropic has agreed to pay about $3.4 billion for gas-fired generators to power its data centers, the Financial Times reported on Sunday, in the largest known single purchase of power-generation equipment by an AI laboratory. The deal underlines how the race to build bigger AI models has become, in large part, a race to secure electricity.

According to the FT, Anthropic secured commitments in late September for 1.7 gigawatts of heavy-duty gas-fired generators, taking over orders that San Antonio-based OnePWR Solutions had placed with Caterpillar about two years earlier. OnePWR had needed financing to complete the purchase, and Anthropic stepped in. The agreement also covers the engineering and construction work needed to install the machines.

Anthropic did not acquire OnePWR itself, and none of the supplier's employees transferred to the AI company, the report said. Neither Anthropic nor OnePWR responded to requests for comment, and Reuters said it could not immediately verify the report. No sites have been named for the generators, and no delivery schedule has been disclosed.

The scale of the purchase works out at roughly $2 million for each megawatt of capacity, and it fits a pattern that has defined the AI industry this year: the companies building the biggest models are increasingly buying their own power infrastructure rather than waiting for the grid. Generators and other equipment must be ordered years in advance, Anthropic warned in its IPO prospectus, and turbines and generators are among the longest lead-time items in a data center build.

That prospectus, filed with the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission on October 26 ahead of Anthropic's planned Nasdaq listing, is explicit about the problem. It says access to electricity limits data center expansion in several markets, that new power sources and grid connections can take years to develop, and that certain planned facilities rely on dedicated on-site natural gas-fired generation. An amended filing on November 2 repeated the warnings.

Anthropic has been preparing for this constraint in public for months. In February, the company committed to pay for 100 percent of the grid upgrades needed to connect its data centers and to bring net-new power generation online to match their electricity needs. The generator deal is the most concrete step yet toward delivering on that promise with hardware it controls.

The financial stakes are enormous. Anthropic disclosed about $895.4 billion in aggregate committed spending through its purchase agreements and lease arrangements, substantially related to computing resources and infrastructure, according to its filings. The company is expected to begin its IPO roadshow imminently, with shares set to debut on November 20, and investors will be watching how it plans to power the growth it is promising.

The reported purchase also lands in the middle of a political fight over who pays for the AI buildout. Voters and local officials in several states have begun pressing data center developers on electricity costs, water use and grid strain, and protesters stormed a Microsoft data center construction site in Amsterdam in recent days. Owning generation outright is one way for an AI company to sidestep the argument that its data centers are draining the public grid.

Whether gas generators are the right answer is a separate question. Gas-fired power is faster to deploy than new grid connections and more reliable than renewables alone for a facility that must run around the clock, but it ties Anthropic's expansion to fossil fuels at a moment when the industry's energy appetite is under growing scrutiny. The company has not said how the generators fit with its climate commitments.

For now, the deal remains attributed to the FT rather than confirmed by the companies involved. What is not in doubt is the underlying problem it points to: the AI industry's ambitions have outrun the power grid, and the companies that can secure electricity — by whatever means — will be the ones that can keep building.
