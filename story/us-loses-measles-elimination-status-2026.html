U.S. Loses Measles Elimination Status After 26 Years as Cases Hit Highest Level Since 1991

The Pan American Health Organization has determined the United States no longer qualifies as having eliminated measles, after the same strain of the virus circulated without a break for more than a year.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 15, 2026 · 5 min readFacebookX

The United States has lost the measles elimination status it held since 2000, after an independent commission of experts reporting to the Pan American Health Organization determined that the country no longer qualifies as having eliminated the disease. The news was first reported by The Wall Street Journal on Friday, and a source close to the process confirmed the outcome to CNN the same day.

Elimination status is a formal designation, not a claim that a country has zero cases. A country loses it when the same strain of measles has circulated without interruption for at least 12 months, a sign that transmission has become established rather than imported. The U.S. achieved elimination status in 2000 after robust immunisation campaigns and the creation of a programme that made shots free for millions of children.

The evidence that tipped the decision came in part from Utah, where scientists at the state's public health laboratory published a preprint study in August showing that the same strain of measles had been circulating for more than a year, accounting for more than 95 percent of cases there. The strain travelled from the southwest of the state to central Utah, ultimately causing more than 600 cases between June 2025 and June 2026. There have also been sustained outbreaks in South Carolina, Pennsylvania, Wisconsin and New York, as well as other states.

The scale of the resurgence is stark. More than 5,400 measles cases have been reported in the United States this year, the highest number since 1991, according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. Most are linked to the 43 new outbreaks this year, but more than 1,000 cases are linked to outbreaks that began last year. People have been hospitalised in roughly one in nine reported cases, and at least 10 people have died after catching measles, according to state reports.

The Wall Street Journal reported that Pennsylvania, New York and Wisconsin have reported five measles deaths so far in November from unrelated outbreaks. Nearly all of this year's cases have been in people who had not received the measles-mumps-rubella vaccine or whose vaccination status is unknown. The MMR vaccine is so effective that two doses cut an exposed person's chances of getting sick by 97 percent compared with someone who is unvaccinated.

The decision was widely expected after years of falling vaccination levels and surging case counts, but it is still a black mark on the nation's public health policy. "Measles elimination is a vital sign for our public health system. It shows cracks in our foundation. Losing elimination status means that we are less prepared as a nation to address infectious and noninfectious disease challenges," said Dr. Demetre Daskalakis, former director of the National Center for Immunization and Respiratory Diseases at the CDC.

Vaccination coverage has slipped below the level needed to stop the virus spreading. At least 95 percent of a population needs to be vaccinated to prevent measles from spreading, which also protects babies and people with conditions who cannot be immunised. But MMR vaccination rates for kindergartners dropped to 92.4 percent in the school year that ended in June, down from 95.2 percent six years earlier, according to the CDC. Rates are far lower in some communities where recent outbreaks have hit hardest.

The acceleration of cases coincides with Robert F. Kennedy Jr.'s tenure as health secretary. Since joining the administration, Kennedy has tried to cut the number of shots on the childhood vaccine schedule, promoted long-debunked claims that immunisations cause autism and cut federal funding for some vaccine research, the Journal reported. Under Kennedy, the CDC has backed away from long-held immunisation recommendations, and the administration has called for children to receive fewer vaccines and to separate the MMR vaccine into individual shots, a shift public health experts say would sow confusion.

The United States is not alone. Canada lost its measles-elimination status in 2025, and the United Kingdom and some European nations lost the status earlier this year. Brazil lost its status in 2019 and re-established it in 2024 after national efforts to increase vaccination rates. To regain elimination status, the United States must halt the continuing spread of the disease for at least 12 months, and PAHO recommends that countries that lose the status submit a corrective action plan and bolster immunisation and surveillance.

PAHO did not immediately respond to requests for comment, and the decision had not been publicly announced by the organisation as of Friday. "This is just so devastating and so sad that we are where we are today," said Dr. Nuwan Gunawardhana, an infectious disease epidemiologist at Columbia University Irvine Medical Center in New York. "This just kind of points towards the direction of really reversing a generation of public health progress." Dr. Peter Hotez, co-director of the Center for Vaccine Development at Texas Children's Hospital, called the loss "incredibly embarrassing" but added: "Basically, measles is back."

Why it matters: Losing elimination status is a formal recognition of what the case counts already showed: measles is spreading in the United States again, and it has been spreading long enough to count as endemic. The 26-year run ended not because of a single outbreak but because vaccination coverage slipped below the level that stops the virus, and because outbreaks in several states were never fully extinguished. Regaining the status will take at least a year without continuous transmission, and it will require the kind of vaccination effort that has become politically contested.
