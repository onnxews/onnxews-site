Second Epstein Files Bill Heads for House Floor as Petition Hits 218 Signatures

A discharge petition forcing a vote on a second bill compelling the release of Jeffrey Epstein's Justice Department files reached the 218 signatures it needed on Wednesday, with all 214 Democrats and four Republicans signing on.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 12, 2026 · 5 min readFacebookX

A discharge petition sponsored by Kentucky Republican Representative Thomas Massie to force a vote on the Epstein Files Transparency Act II amassed 218 signatures on Wednesday, November 11, teeing up additional floor action on legislation that would empower state attorneys general, victims, and members of Congress to sue the U.S. attorney general for unlawfully withholding or redacting investigative records related to Jeffrey Epstein.

With Representative John Garamendi's signature, the petition reached the 218 names needed to force House Speaker Mike Johnson to call a vote on the legislation, after Massie and Representative Ro Khanna launched the effort in early September. All 214 Democrats signed, along with four Republicans: Massie, Representative Brian Fitzpatrick of Pennsylvania, Representative Nancy Mace of South Carolina, and Representative Ralph Norman of South Carolina. The petition was one signature short as of Tuesday.

The bill would hand state attorneys general, people who say they are survivors of Epstein's abuse, and members of Congress the ability to sue the attorney general to demand disclosure of documents the lawmakers believe the government is unlawfully withholding. It would also force the Justice Department to hand alleged victims unredacted records relating to themselves, and allow members of Congress to demand unredacted files from the department.

The legislation would also bar Attorney General Todd Blanche from withholding documents by using "deliberative process privilege", a legal doctrine the department has invoked to shield internal decision-making. "You have to give us those internal memos about decisions on whether to prosecute, whether to investigate, because that's central to this whole thing," Massie told CNN. "What we do in the new bill is we say, 'When we said you have to tell us about your decisions, that covered deliberative process privilege.' But we're saying it explicitly in this bill: 'You can't assert that deliberative process privilege.'"

Massie and Khanna say they are not satisfied with the Justice Department's compliance with the first Epstein files law, insisting officials are trying to hide information in still-unreleased or unredacted files. They believe the department is withholding more than 3 million files that it is required to release under the November 2025 law and is "maintaining heavy redactions" on others. The first bill was forced through the same discharge-petition tactic almost exactly a year ago, passed the House, and was signed into law by President Trump, leading the administration to release millions of files linked to Epstein.

The development marks the second time in almost exactly one year that Massie is on track to force the government to release files by leveraging the procedural tactic. Discharge petitions, once rare, have succeeded eight times so far in the 119th Congress. The tool lets rank-and-file lawmakers bypass the majority's leadership and force legislation to the floor for consideration, and it requires a majority of the House, 218 signatures, before it can be used.

Reaching 218 signatures does not produce a vote immediately. Once a discharge petition secures the threshold, the motion is placed on the calendar for at least seven legislative days, after which one of the signers would need to offer a motion on the discharge petition. If that happens, the House speaker would then have two legislative days to act on the motion, and if the motion passes, the House would move to consider the legislation. Massie said he is working with Representatives Pramila Jayapal, Teresa Leger Fernandez, and Khanna to push the bill to the floor "for a vote soon."

Any floor action on the proposed Epstein II bill would come after the Thanksgiving recess, potentially as early as December 2 or 3, according to ABC News reporting carried by MyCentralOregon. The timing matters because the current Congress has only weeks left: the House is scheduled to sit for a limited number of days before government funding expires on December 11, and all pending petitions expire when the 119th Congress ends on January 3, 2027.

Passage of the second bill is not guaranteed even if it reaches the floor. Senate Republican leaders could still decline to consider the measure before the new Congress takes office in January, and the White House has not said whether the president would sign it. But the petition's success shows that the Epstein files remain one of the few issues capable of uniting nearly every Democrat with a handful of Republicans willing to defy their own leadership, and it guarantees the Justice Department's handling of the files will stay in the headlines through the lame-duck session.

Why it matters: This is the second time in a year that Massie has used the discharge petition to drag the Epstein files onto the House floor over the objections of his own party's leadership, and the second time it has worked. The first bill produced a law and millions of pages of releases; the second is built on the claim that the Justice Department is still hiding more than 3 million files behind redactions and privilege claims. The politics are unusual: all 214 Democrats signed, and the only four Republicans to join are all leaving the House in January, which means they have nothing to lose. The December 2 or 3 window is the real deadline — if the vote slips past the end of the Congress, the bill dies and the whole effort starts again under a Democratic majority that may pursue it very differently.
