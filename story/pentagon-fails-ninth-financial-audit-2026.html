Pentagon Fails Its Financial Audit for the Ninth Year in a Row, Even as Most of Its Units Pass

Independent auditors again could not verify the Defense Department's books for fiscal 2026, issuing another disclaimer of opinion on more than $4.8 trillion in assets. But 14 of the Pentagon's 25 reporting entities passed their own audits, the first time a majority of its assets carried clean opinions.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 17, 2026 · 5 min readFacebookX

The Pentagon failed its financial audit for the ninth consecutive year, according to an inspector general report released on Monday, November 16. Independent auditors once again issued a disclaimer of opinion on the department's books for fiscal 2026, which ended September 30, meaning the department could not provide enough reliable evidence for auditors to judge whether its financial statements are accurate. The verdict covers an Agency Financial Report reflecting more than $4.8 trillion in assets.

The headline failure hides real progress underneath. Defense Department Chief Financial Officer Jules Hurst told Reuters that 14 of the Pentagon's 25 standalone reporting entities passed their individual audits in fiscal 2026, up from 11 the year before. Those 14 entities account for 56 percent of the department's assets and 86 percent of its liabilities. "For the first time ever, a majority of assets from the department are on clean opinions," Hurst said. Among the entities passing for the first time was the Pentagon's Medicare-Eligible Retiree Health Care Fund.

A disclaimer of opinion is not a finding that money was stolen, and it is not proof that equipment is missing. It is a finding that auditors could not gather enough dependable evidence to sign off on the combined statements. That distinction matters, but it is also the reason the failure keeps repeating: the Pentagon cannot prove, to professional auditing standards, where all of its money and property are.

The department's own explanation for the problem is the state of its bookkeeping. The Pentagon runs about 149 financial management systems, many of them inadequate and outdated, including technology built on 50-year-old computer code. To complete the fiscal 2026 audit without relying on those antiquated systems, officials drew from 61 core systems in the department's more modern cloud-based War Data Platform and used statistically significant data samples. The department is also using artificial intelligence tools from Palantir and xAI, alongside in-house applications, to automate financial reconciliations and documentation. Bloomberg Government reported that Ernst & Young has been awarded a five-year, $990 million contract to help the department make the grade.

The stakes are rising because the money is rising. As the administration seeks a record $1.5 trillion in national defense spending from Congress, modernizing the Pentagon's financial systems has become a priority, and the department is trying to show lawmakers it can be trusted with the largest budget in its history. The Government Accountability Office, the watchdog arm of Congress, warned in May 2026 testimony that the Pentagon's new audit strategy may document the biggest balances without fixing the underlying control problems, and that any pass in 2028 may not be sustainable.

The Pentagon is the only one of the 24 major federal agencies covered by the Chief Financial Officers Act of 1990 that has never passed an audit. It was first audited in 2018 and has failed every full-scope annual audit since. Congress gave the department 10 years to pass, a window that falls in the fiscal year starting in October 2027, and the department is aiming for a full department-wide clean opinion in fiscal 2028.

Beginning this year, the Pentagon plans to consolidate its 25 separate audit opinions into eight, ahead of seeking that full department-wide opinion in fiscal 2028. Hurst framed the work as a matter of time rather than a question of whether it can be done. "Passing the audit is ultimately a time problem," he said, arguing that artificial intelligence lets auditors test more data than before.

For taxpayers, the ninth failure is both familiar and strange: the same department that cannot produce auditable books is asking Congress for a record budget, and Congress keeps writing the checks. Passing an audit is not a legal prerequisite for receiving appropriations, and a disclaimer does not automatically cut funding. What it does is leave the public, and lawmakers, without an independent assurance that the world's largest military bureaucracy knows what it owns and what it spends.

The next test comes with the fiscal 2027 budget debate and the consolidation of the audit opinions now under way. If the department's new systems and the Ernst & Young contract deliver, the 2028 target is within reach; if the Government Accountability Office's warning proves right, the Pentagon could produce a clean opinion on paper while the underlying controls remain broken. Either way, the ninth disclaimer means the department goes into that debate still unable to prove its own numbers.

Why it matters: Nine years of failure is a long time to keep failing the same test, and the Pentagon's own numbers explain why this year feels different: for the first time, most of its assets sit in entities that passed. That is genuine progress, and it is also a measure of how far there is to go — the department as a whole still cannot be verified, and the Government Accountability Office's warning that the new strategy may paper over the underlying problems is the one to watch. The audit matters because the budget is about to get much bigger. A $1.5 trillion defense request deserves books that auditors can actually sign, and until the Pentagon can produce them, every dollar debate will rest on numbers the department itself cannot prove.
