Blue Man Group Brings Holiday Show to Broadway After 33-Year Astor Place Run

Blue Man Group: A New Holiday Surprise opened at the Lunt-Fontanne Theatre on November 17, bringing the blue-painted trio to Broadway for the first time in a limited run through January 3.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 19, 2026 · 4 min readFacebookX

Blue Man Group made its Broadway debut on Tuesday, November 17, with a new holiday show at the Lunt-Fontanne Theatre. The production, called Blue Man Group: A New Holiday Surprise, is the group's first holiday production and its first time on Broadway, and it runs for a limited engagement through January 3, 2027.

The debut comes after a long run downtown. Blue Man Group performed off Broadway at the Astor Place Theatre from 1991 to 2025, a 33-year run that ended in February 2025. The move to Broadway, into the 1,500-seat Lunt-Fontanne on West 46th Street, is a big step up in scale for a show that was built for a small, intimate room.

The show runs 90 minutes with no intermission, and tickets start at $50. It is directed by Matt Ramsey, with Byron Estep as music director and Scott Adam Davis on scenic design. The cast includes Dan Cooper, Wes Day, Mark Frankel, Alexander Pobutsky, and Pete Simpson as the Blue Men, with Kaylie Brooke and Anastasiya Kostomina as the Spirits, a pair of new characters for the holiday show.

The group was founded in 1987 by Chris Wink, Matt Goldman, and Phil Stanton, and its wordless, percussion-driven performances became one of New York's longest-running attractions. The signature bits are still there: the paint-drumming, in which the Blue Men splash colored paint onto drums and canvases, the marshmallow-catching, the drumbone, a trombone made of PVC pipe, and the giant silver bells.

Critics have been split on the Broadway version. BroadwayWorld's review roundup put the average rating at 56.7%, and several reviewers said the show is largely the old show with Christmas carols added, rather than a new production. Vulture, TheaterMania, and New York Theater were among the less enthusiastic, with TheaterMania the most severe, calling the show a lump of coal. One critic gave it three stars out of five.

Others were more positive. Stage and Cinema praised the show, and Caroline Cao, writing for the New York Theatre Guide, said the percussion hit like an earthquake, even as she wished for the intimacy of the old Astor Place room. The recurring theme in the reviews is that the show works best up close, and that the 1,500-seat Lunt-Fontanne is a very different room from the one where the Blue Men made their name.

The holiday framing is new, and the show leans into it with carols, falling snow, and seasonal jokes. But the core of the evening is the same wordless, physical comedy that has defined the group for nearly four decades, and the question for audiences is whether that core still lands in a big Broadway house.

The limited run through January 3 is a test of whether the Blue Men can find a new audience on Broadway, and whether the holiday show can become a seasonal fixture. For now, the show is on sale for the holiday season, and the blue paint is back on a New York stage.

Whether the critics' reservations matter to the box office is another question. Blue Man Group has always been a show that audiences discover by word of mouth, and the holiday season is the busiest time of the year for Broadway. If families turn up, the run could extend well beyond January.

Why it matters: This is a gamble on scale. Blue Man Group built its reputation in a small downtown theatre where the audience could feel every drumbeat, and moving to a 1,500-seat Broadway house changes the experience in a fundamental way. The mixed reviews, averaging 56.7%, reflect that tension: the show itself is still the same wordless, physical comedy, but the room is different. The holiday framing gives the production a reason to exist on Broadway now, and the limited run through January 3 lets the producers test the waters without committing to a long run. If it works, it could become a new seasonal tradition; if it does not, it will be a reminder that some shows belong in the room they were built for.
