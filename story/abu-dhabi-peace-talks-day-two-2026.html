Russia and Ukraine Open Second Day of Abu Dhabi Peace Talks

Negotiators resumed U.S.-brokered talks in Abu Dhabi with de-escalation and a possible 1,500-prisoner swap on the table, but Russia still demands the whole Donbas.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 11, 2026 · 4 min readFacebookX

Russian, Ukrainian and U.S. officials began a second day of peace talks in Abu Dhabi on Wednesday, resuming meetings that Kyiv and Washington described as constructive but that produced no early signs of a breakthrough. The talks, brokered by the United States, are the first between the warring sides since February, when the Trump administration shifted its focus to the war with Iran.

'We are starting the second day of the talks,' Rustem Umerov, Ukraine's top negotiator, said on the Telegram app. 'First, there will be a trilateral format with the United States and Russia. After that, we will have a separate meeting with the European partners.' The second day is the last day on which meetings are scheduled, putting pressure on the delegations to show progress before they leave the Gulf.

Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy, who like Russian President Vladimir Putin is not attending the talks, said Monday's 'substantive' discussions had covered humanitarian and security issues, and that a potential summit of Russian, Ukrainian and U.S. leaders could be discussed on Tuesday. U.S. Secretary of State Marco Rubio described the first day of talks as very productive, without giving details.

Negotiators on Wednesday are due to focus on de-escalation measures, including possible ceasefires covering the Black Sea and energy infrastructure, Zelenskyy said late Tuesday during a visit to Copenhagen. The two sides discussed humanitarian steps, territorial issues and security guarantees for Ukraine at Tuesday's talks, as well as a potential exchange of up to 1,500 prisoners each, he said, a swap that would be among the largest of the war if it goes ahead.

U.S. envoys Jared Kushner and Steve Witkoff reportedly told participants that Washington had narrowed the differences between the two sides, a claim that neither Moscow nor Kyiv has publicly confirmed. The American delegation has been pushing both sides toward a framework deal, but the gaps on the core questions remain wide, and neither side has shown any willingness to move on them.

Territory is the central obstacle. Russia continues to demand the entire Donbas region, including territory it has not captured, while Ukraine refuses to relinquish land it still holds. Moscow's position would require Kyiv to hand over cities its forces still defend, a concession Zelenskyy has repeatedly said he cannot make. Kyiv, for its part, wants security guarantees that would prevent a future Russian attack, something Moscow has resisted.

The obstacles were on display even as the talks resumed. Russian Foreign Minister Sergei Lavrov rejected the prospect of a ceasefire, accusing European countries of seeking a temporary truce to facilitate the deployment of foreign troops to Ukraine. Meanwhile, Ukrainian Prime Minister Sergii Koretskyi reported Russian strikes against six major electricity substations across four central regions, a reminder that the war on the ground has not paused for the diplomacy.

The talks come after a brutal stretch for Ukraine's energy grid. Russia pounded Kyiv with 172 drones and 36 ballistic missiles days before the peace talks opened, in one of the largest attacks of the war, and the strikes on substations reported on Wednesday suggest the campaign against Ukraine's power system is continuing even while negotiators meet.

The context for the negotiations has also shifted since the last round in February. The Trump administration's attention was consumed for months by the war with Iran, which dominated American foreign policy through the spring and summer, and the Abu Dhabi talks are the first serious attempt to return to the Ukraine file since then. The American team is now trying to show that both wars can be managed at once, even as the lame-duck session at home limits the administration's political room.

What happens next depends on whether the second day produces anything concrete. A prisoner exchange would be a tangible result both sides could take home, and a limited ceasefire covering the Black Sea or energy infrastructure would be a meaningful step toward de-escalation. But without movement on territory and security guarantees, the talks risk ending where so many rounds before them have ended: with warm words about progress and a war that grinds on.
