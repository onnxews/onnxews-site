First Big Winter Storm of the Season Brings Up to Two Feet of Snow to the Sierra

Winter storm warnings cover the Sierra Nevada from Friday morning to Saturday morning, with a foot of snow expected around Lake Tahoe and up to two feet on the crest, while a series of Pacific storms brings rain and huge surf to the California coast.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 13, 2026 · 4 min readFacebookX

The first major winter storm of the season is bearing down on the Sierra Nevada on Friday, with the National Weather Service warning of up to two feet of snow, ridge-top winds near 90 mph, and travel that could become very difficult to impossible into Saturday. The storm is the first in a series of Pacific systems expected to bring rain, snow and dangerous surf to California over the coming week.

The weather service's Reno office issued a winter storm warning for the greater Lake Tahoe area that runs from 10 a.m. Friday to 10 a.m. Saturday, covering Truckee, South Lake Tahoe and the surrounding communities. Forecasters expect about 12 inches of heavy, wet snow above 7,000 feet, up to 6 inches below that elevation, and as much as 20 inches along the crest west of Lake Tahoe, with wind gusts of 70 to 90 mph over the ridges and 45 to 55 mph in Truckee and the lakeside towns. Waves of 2 to 5 feet were forecast on Lake Tahoe itself.

The Sacramento office issued a matching warning for the northern Sierra above 6,000 feet, including Interstate 80, Highway 50 and Lassen Volcanic National Park, with 6 to 12 inches of snow and up to 18 inches on the highest peaks, and winds gusting to 60 mph. In Mono County, around Mammoth Lakes, forecasters expected 1 to 2 feet of snow above 7,000 feet with ridge gusts of 60 to 80 mph. The weather service warned that the snow would be heavy and wet, the kind of Sierra cement that can bring down trees and power lines, with snow levels falling as low as 5,500 feet.

The storm is not a one-day event. Forecasters said a series of storm systems would bring widespread rain, moderate to heavy snow in the mountains, and gusty to strong southerly winds from Friday into early next week. A second storm was expected to arrive Sunday into Monday, and a third storm could bring more rain to the Bay Area around Wednesday.

Down in the valleys and along the coast, the same system is bringing the first prolonged stretch of stormy weather of the season. Rain was expected to reach the North Bay late Thursday night and spread across the rest of the Bay Area by early Friday afternoon, with south winds gusting 25 to 35 mph ahead of the front and brief gusts of 45 mph or higher as it moved through, along with a slight chance of thunderstorms. Through Saturday afternoon, the first storm was forecast to bring 0.50 to 1.25 inches of rain to the North Bay, 0.30 to 0.80 inches across most of the rest of the Bay Area and the Monterey Bay region, and 0.75 to 1.25 inches in the Santa Cruz Mountains and the Santa Lucia Range.

The coast faces a different hazard. High surf warnings were in effect along much of the California and Oregon coast, from Oregon to Big Sur, with breaking waves as high as 30 to 35 feet expected starting Friday and the biggest surf building on Saturday.

For travelers, the timing is the worst part. The weather service said travel could be very difficult, with the hazardous conditions hitting the Friday evening commute. Ski resorts across the Sierra, meanwhile, stand to gain the most from the storm, with several positioned to receive their first significant early-season snow.

The storm is the first in a series of Pacific systems expected to bring rain, snow and dangerous surf to California over the coming week, and the first significant winter storm of the season for the Sierra. For now, the immediate concern is getting through the weekend: the first storm wraps up by daybreak Saturday, though slick roads could linger through late morning, and the second storm is already lined up behind it.

Why it matters: The storm matters because it is both a relief and a hazard arriving at once: the Sierra needs the snowpack after two dry weeks, and California's ski resorts need the early-season base, but the same heavy, wet snow that builds that base can snap power lines, strand drivers on Interstate 80, and turn a Friday evening commute into a rescue operation. With two more storms lined up behind this one, the weekend is less a single weather event than the opening of the winter pattern — and the first real test of whether drivers, utilities and emergency crews are ready for it.
