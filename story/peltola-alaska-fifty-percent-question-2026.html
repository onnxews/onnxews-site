Peltola's 50% Problem: Why a Lead of 11,213 Votes Has Not Settled Alaska's Senate Race

The Democrat sits at 49.94% on the state's full count and just over 50% on another reading — and the difference comes down to 872 write-in votes and what counts as a majority.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 16, 2026 · 4 min readFacebookX

Mary Peltola leads Alaska's unresolved Senate race by 11,213 votes, but whether she has actually won it outright depends on which denominator you use. On the Alaska Division of Elections' full unofficial count, updated on November 14, Peltola holds 140,637 first-choice votes, or 49.94%, against 129,424, or 45.96%, for Republican incumbent Dan Sullivan. On a second reading of the same ballots, one that sets aside unresolved write-in votes, she sits just over 50%.

The difference is 872 write-in votes out of 281,587 counted in the Senate race. Divide Peltola's 140,637 first choices by that full total and her share is 49.94%. Exclude the write-ins from the calculation, leaving 280,715 votes in the denominator, and her share rises to about 50.1%. Peltola is 157 votes short of the 140,794 that would make up half of all 281,587 votes counted so far.

The rest of the field explains where the remaining votes went. Gerald Heikes, a Republican, took 6,002 votes, or 2.13%, while independent Dan Sullivan Jr. — no relation to the incumbent — took 4,652, or 1.65%. All 401 of the state's precincts have reported.

Peltola declared victory on Saturday, November 14, after the latest batch of ballots stretched her lead into five figures. Her campaign announced the declaration on X as the count showed her ahead by more than 10,000 votes, and the margin has since grown to 11,213. On Friday's figures she had led by 10,412 votes, at 49.84% to Sullivan's 46.09%, after winning about 59% of the newly counted Senate votes in that batch.

Sullivan has not conceded, and his campaign says it is waiting for every ballot. His spokesman, Nate Adams, said the campaign was still waiting for every ballot to be counted. Sullivan has not posted publicly since election night. The Associated Press has not called the race, estimating that about 96% of Alaska's ballots have been counted, and it remains the only unresolved Senate contest from the midterm elections.

The counting is not finished. The Division of Elections will keep processing ballots through Wednesday, November 18, the last day it accepts absentee ballots mailed from overseas. If no candidate finishes with a majority of first-choice votes, the state will hold a ranked-choice tabulation on November 18, eliminating the last-place candidate and reallocating those ballots to voters' next choices. State officials aim to certify the results by November 25.

Even if Peltola slips back under 50%, the ranked-choice count is expected to favour her on the preferences counted so far. Alaska's election rules permit unresolved write-in rankings to be excluded from ranked-choice processing under specified conditions, and the Division of Elections expressly distinguishes its summary of first choices from the formal ranked-choice tabulation. One analysis of the state's ballot-level file, by Alaska News, identified roughly 350 ballots with skipped rankings and estimated that accounting for them and the write-ins left Peltola about 250 votes short under its interpretation — that is the outlet's calculation, not an official tabulation.

The stakes reach beyond Alaska. A Peltola win would give Democrats 56 seats in the 100-member Senate, completing nine flips in the midterms, and would settle the final piece of the majority that will take power in January. It would still leave Democrats short of the 60 votes needed to beat a filibuster, a fact that shapes everything from the leadership fight in the caucus to the funding deadline facing the lame-duck Congress.

For now, the race sits in an unusual place: a candidate who has declared victory, a lead of 11,213 votes, and a result that still cannot be called because of a question about 157 votes and 872 write-ins. The next hard date is Wednesday, when the last overseas ballots arrive and the ranked-choice tabulation — if it is needed — begins.

Why it matters: This is a genuine sequential update on a race Orbit has covered since election night, and the new development is not the size of the lead — that was reported on November 15 — but the 50% question itself, which only emerged clearly once the state's full November 14 count was published. It matters because Alaska's majority rule means the difference between an outright win and a ranked-choice tabulation, and because the final Senate number decides whether Democrats hold 55 or 56 seats going into January. The count runs to Wednesday, November 18, and Orbit will report the tabulation when the state releases it.
