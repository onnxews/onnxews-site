Van Hollen Pledges to End the Filibuster as Schumer Allies Predict 40-Plus Votes

At a Denver conference on Friday, Van Hollen promised to scrap the 60-vote threshold, ruled out a 2028 White House bid, and picked up Jeff Merkley's endorsement as Schumer's allies predicted a win.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 15, 2026 · 6 min readFacebookX

Sen. Chris Van Hollen of Maryland, who is challenging Sen. Chuck Schumer for the Democratic leadership of the Senate, pledged on Friday to move to end the filibuster if he wins the post, and ruled out a 2028 presidential run. Speaking at the What's Next conference in Denver, Van Hollen said he would push to abolish the 60-vote threshold that has blocked much of the Democratic agenda, a promise that sharpened the first contested Democratic leadership election in more than three decades into a fight over the rules of the Senate itself. "I'm not running in 2028," Van Hollen said, closing off the presidential speculation that had shadowed his campaign since he began exploring a White House bid earlier this year.

Van Hollen framed the filibuster pledge as a matter of democratic principle. "We need democracy in the United States Senate... so that means 51 votes," he said at the conference. "My personal view has been all along that the filibuster is an anti-democratic — small 'd' — provision. It's not in our Constitution. It was put in there by senators to enhance their power over the power of the people." He said filibuster reform was especially important for "democracy protection measures" designed to safeguard voting rights, a priority for a party that has watched its agenda stall behind the 60-vote barrier even when it held a majority. Schumer, by contrast, has not said whether he would scrap the threshold with at least a 55-seat majority.

The pledge drew an immediate endorsement from Sen. Jeff Merkley of Oregon, a longtime advocate of filibuster reform who backed Van Hollen on Friday. "Senator Chris Van Hollen is that leader," Merkley said in a statement announcing his support. "He has my full support for Majority Leader." Merkley's endorsement, announced on Friday, November 13, followed backing from Sen. Bernie Sanders, and from Senators-elect Abdul El-Sayed of Michigan and Peggy Flanagan of Minnesota, giving Van Hollen four public commitments from current or incoming senators as of Friday. Retiring Sen. Tina Smith of Minnesota also supports him but cannot vote in the leadership election.

Schumer's allies, meanwhile, are projecting confidence that the incumbent will survive. Allies of Schumer predict he will win more than 40 votes in the secret-ballot leadership election, which is scheduled for the first week of December, and they are discussing moving the vote up into November to end the uncertainty sooner. Schumer himself has said, "I have the votes to continue serving as Democratic Leader." His supporters include Sen. Kirsten Gillibrand of New York, who is leading the effort to keep him in the post, along with Sens. Ruben Gallego, John Fetterman, Martin Heinrich, Mazie Hirono, Jack Reed, Richard Blumenthal, Tammy Baldwin, Mark Kelly, Jacky Rosen and Sen.-elect Sherrod Brown of Ohio. Gallego defended the incumbent's record bluntly: "When the manager takes a World Series, you give them another contract."

The arithmetic of the contest is close enough that both sides can claim a path. The winner needs 28 votes in the secret ballot, or 29 if Peltola wins the uncalled Alaska race, out of a caucus that will hold 55 seats, or 56 with Alaska. Van Hollen has acknowledged his position plainly: "Let me be clear, I'm the underdog in this race." He said he decided to run to close what he called a "trust deficit" between Senate Democrats and the voters who returned them to the majority, arguing that the caucus needs a leader willing to use that majority. Schumer has led the caucus since 2017, and no Democrat has challenged him for the post since 1994.

Van Hollen's history on the filibuster gives his pledge weight beyond campaign rhetoric. Most Senate Democrats voted in January 2022 for a carve-out that would have let voting-rights legislation pass with a simple majority, a proposal that failed only because Sens. Joe Manchin and Kyrsten Sinema voted against it. Merkley, who has pushed filibuster reform for years, framed his endorsement in those terms, saying Van Hollen was the leader who could deliver the change the caucus has debated for a decade. The question now is whether senators who voted for reform in 2022 are willing to back a leader who has made it a condition of his candidacy, and whether Schumer's refusal to commit either way becomes a liability with them.

Not every Democrat is picking a side. Sen. Elissa Slotkin of Michigan has called for a change in leadership but has not endorsed Van Hollen, and she told reporters in Denver that she is "not voting for Sen. Schumer." Sen.-elect Adam Hamilton of Kansas has also said he will not back Schumer. Potential further endorsements for Van Hollen could come from Sen. Elizabeth Warren and Senators-elect Troy Jackson of Maine and Juliana Stratton of Illinois, according to The Hill's reporting, and the next several days are expected to bring a wave of public commitments as the election approaches. The jockeying is playing out in public in a way Senate leadership contests rarely do, with both camps counting votes in the open.

For Schumer, the challenge is the most serious of his nine years leading the caucus, and it comes at a moment when Democrats are newly in the majority after the midterms. His allies argue that the party's victory under his leadership speaks for itself, and Fetterman said it was "truly bizarre to me" that anyone would challenge Schumer. Van Hollen's camp argues that winning the majority is precisely the point: a majority that cannot pass its agenda because of a rule the Constitution never required is a majority in name only. The filibuster pledge turns that argument into a testable promise, one that will follow Van Hollen into the job if he wins and haunt Schumer if he does not answer it.

The leadership election, currently scheduled for the first week of December, may be moved up into November at the urging of Schumer's allies, who want the contest settled before the new Congress is seated. Either way, the next two weeks will decide whether the Senate Democrats enter 2027 with the leader who delivered their majority, or with a challenger who has promised to change the rules that have defined the chamber for a generation. Van Hollen has made his case. Schumer has said he has the votes. The secret ballot will settle which of them is right.

Why it matters: This matters because the leadership race has become a referendum on whether a Democratic majority should be able to govern. Van Hollen's filibuster pledge is the clearest dividing line yet: Schumer has not said whether he would scrap the 60-vote threshold with a 55-seat majority, and Van Hollen has now made ending it a condition of his candidacy. With the winner needing only 28 votes in a secret ballot and Schumer's allies predicting more than 40, the contest is close enough that a handful of uncommitted senators will decide it, and the filibuster question will follow whoever wins into the new Congress. If Van Hollen prevails, the Senate's most durable procedural barrier could fall in the first weeks of 2027.
