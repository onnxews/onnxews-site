U.N. Talks on AI Killer Robots End Without Binding Rules as Countries Agree to Two More Years of Discussion

After a week of negotiations in Geneva that ran into the early hours of Saturday, 128 countries agreed only to work toward a possible accord on lethal autonomous weapons by 2028, leaving open whether any future rules would be legally binding.

ONOrbit News NetworkNov 22, 2026 · 4 min readFacebookX

Efforts to set firm rules on AI-powered killer robots and drones ran aground on Saturday as United Nations-backed talks in Geneva produced a softened agreement to extend discussions for two more years. Scores of countries ended the week-long conference on weapons by agreeing to work toward a possible accord on lethal autonomous weapons systems by 2028, but they did not commit to the binding rules sought by U.N. Secretary-General Antonio Guterres, many governments and campaigners.

The compromise emerged in the early hours of Saturday morning, after negotiators worked through the night on the final wording. Countries agreed to "elaborate" a possible accord on the weapons, a weaker formulation than the word "negotiate," which some delegations had resisted. The result is a mandate to keep talking, not a treaty, a prohibition or an enforceable set of restrictions.

Before the meeting, seven countries had proposed launching negotiations on a rulebook that could prevent or limit the ability of machines to kill without human control or accountability. That effort fell short. Austria, Belgium, Brazil, Ireland, Mexico, New Zealand and Panama put forward the proposal, and Brazil's Ambassador Frederico Meyer summed up the frustration afterward, saying, "Technology is moving faster than diplomacy. Diplomats are very slow."

The opposition came from the countries that build the most advanced weapons. The United States, Russia and Israel had already indicated their reluctance to accept binding regulations. On the first day, Russia delayed proceedings by objecting to the meeting's agenda, and the United States proposed a five-year window to discuss nonbinding, optional guidelines for autonomous weapons instead.

The International Committee of the Red Cross, the guardian of the Geneva Conventions, had said before the negotiations that it was urgent for legally binding rules to be drafted within a year. That call was backed by the secretary-general and by Pope Leo XIV. After the outcome, the Red Cross said it regretted the move and renewed its appeal for a binding treaty, while other campaigners called the deal "a real disappointment" and a "nothing burger."

Izumi Nakamitsu, the U.N. high representative for disarmament affairs, hailed a "significant achievement" but acknowledged a "glass half full or half empty" scenario, citing Guterres' hopes for a legally binding deal this year. Guterres, who leaves office at the end of the year, had set a 2026 deadline for launching negotiations toward an agreement on autonomous weapons systems, a deadline that will now pass to his successor.

Campaigners accused military powers of blocking stronger language. Stop Killer Robots lamented a "watered-down mandate," and Beatrice Fihn, founder of the international law advocacy group Lex International, said the decision amounted to "pushing the can down the road for two more years." Max Tegmark, a machine learning specialist at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology and founder of the Future of Life Institute, said: "States have agreed it is sufficient to just talk about the issue, for the 14th year, while these weapons enable atrocities."

The talks centered on the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons, ratified by 128 countries, where decisions are made by consensus. That means any single country can block an agreement, and any deal was likely to be based on a low common denominator. The Swiss chair of the meeting hailed the outcome as a significant achievement for humanitarian disarmament after more than a decade of talks, but the structure of the forum is exactly what made a strong result so hard to reach.

The stakes are rising on the battlefield. In Ukraine, Sudan and Gaza, civilians are being killed by semi-autonomous drones, and the United Nations says that this year in Sudan, 80 percent of civilian deaths were caused by drones. Humans remain, if very remotely, in control for now, but humanitarian organizations fear the world is on the verge of seeing artificial intelligence-assisted weapons make the decision to kill entirely on their own. As one expert in military AI governance put it, the worry is that with two years to talk about rules that might only be optional, the technology will race ahead, uncontrolled.

Why it matters: The Geneva outcome is a measure of how far military interests can narrow international agreement: after 14 years of discussion, the world's governments could not even agree to negotiate, only to elaborate. The consensus rule of the weapons convention means the United States, Russia and Israel effectively hold a veto, and they used it. The real consequence is a two-year window in which autonomous weapons remain unregulated while the technology spreads to actors far beyond the major powers, including nonstate groups. The next test is whether frustrated countries take the issue outside the U.N. framework, as some observers are already suggesting.
